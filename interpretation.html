Unit 1

Introduction: construction, interpretation and the primary rules

The aim of interpretation is to find the intention of the legislature from the words it used. The words are read first in their ordinary sense, then in context, and only then with the help of other rules.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Difference between construction and interpretation
  • Concept and power of interpretation
  • Literal construction and other principles
  • General principles: the primary rule, other main principles
  • Reading words in their context: external and statutory aspects

1.1 Construction and interpretation

  • Interpretation: finding the true sense of the words used, the meaning the author attached to them (Salmond: the process by which courts seek to ascertain the meaning of the legislature through the medium of authoritative forms).
  • Construction: drawing conclusions beyond the direct expression of the text, from elements known from and given in the text, where the words are ambiguous or the case was not foreseen (Cooley).
  • In practice the terms are used interchangeably.

1.2 Concept and power of interpretation

  • Courts interpret statutes because words are imprecise, situations are unforeseen, drafting errors occur, and laws must apply to changing facts.
  • In India, the power flows from the judicial function (Art. 141: law declared by the Supreme Court binds all courts).
  • The General Clauses Act, 1897 provides definitions and rules applicable to all Central Acts unless the context otherwise requires (for example, s.3 definitions, s.6 effect of repeal, s.8 references to repealed enactments, s.13 gender and number, s.21 power to make includes power to amend).

1.3 General principles

1.3.1 The primary rule: literal construction

Words must be given their plain, ordinary and grammatical meaning. If the words are clear, they must be applied, whatever the consequences.

CaseSussex Peerage Case (1844)

Tindal CJ said that if the words of the statute are precise and unambiguous, no more is necessary than to expound those words in their natural and ordinary sense; the words themselves best declare the intention of the lawgiver.

CaseNelson Motis v Union of India (1992)

Where the words of a statutory provision are clear and unambiguous, they must be given effect, and the court cannot add or subtract words on considerations of policy.

CaseTirath Singh v Bachittar Singh (1955)

Under the Representation of the People Act, notice was to be given to "all parties" to an election petition. Literally, that included persons who were not parties to the specific allegation. The Court applied the golden rule and confined notice to those against whom allegations were made, to avoid an absurd result.

CaseSmith v Hughes (1960)

Prostitutes soliciting from balconies and windows were held to be soliciting "in a street", applying the mischief rule: the Street Offences Act aimed to clean up the streets so that people could walk without being molested.

1.3.3 and 1.3.4 Reading words in context

External aspect (external aids)

  • Historical background and the mischief the Act sought to remedy.
  • Statement of Objects and Reasons (to understand background, not to construe clear words).
  • Parliamentary debates, committee and Law Commission reports (increasingly used: R.S. Nayak v A.R. Antulay, 1984; Kalpana Mehta v Union of India, 2018 on parliamentary committee reports).
  • Dictionaries, earlier and later statutes in pari materia, judicial decisions, foreign decisions, contemporanea expositio (contemporaneous official interpretation), scientific and technical meaning.

Statutory aspect (internal aids)

  • Long title and preamble: aid in resolving ambiguity, cannot control clear words.
  • Headings and marginal notes: may be referred to for resolving doubt (Bhinka v Charan Singh, 1959), not to override clear words.
  • Definitions: "means" (exhaustive), "includes" (extensive); "unless the context otherwise requires".
  • Provisos: carve out an exception from the main provision; do not enlarge it (unless it is in substance a substantive provision).
  • Explanations: clarify or explain the main provision; may widen or narrow it.
  • Illustrations: show how a provision applies; cannot curtail the section (Mahesh Chandra Sharma v Raj Kumari Sharma, 1996, on illustrations to the Evidence Act).
  • Schedules, punctuation (limited weight), non obstante clauses ("notwithstanding anything contained in"), and the rule that a statute must be read as a whole.
CaseReserve Bank of India v Peerless General Finance and Investment Co (1987)

Chinnappa Reddy J said interpretation must depend on the text and the context; text is the texture, context gives the colour. A statute is best interpreted when we know why it was enacted, and it must be read as a whole, chapter by chapter, section by section.

Points to remember

  • Interpretation finds meaning; construction draws conclusions beyond the text.
  • The General Clauses Act supplies definitions and rules.
  • Literal rule first (Sussex Peerage); golden rule to avoid absurdity (Tirath Singh); mischief rule (Heydon's case; Smith v Hughes); purposive and harmonious construction.
  • Internal aids (title, preamble, headings, definitions, provisos, explanations, illustrations) and external aids (history, debates, reports, dictionaries).
  • Text and context together (RBI v Peerless).

Unit 2

Beneficial, restrictive and anti-evasion construction

Welfare laws are construed liberally in favour of those they protect; laws that take away rights or create offences are construed strictly; and courts will not allow statutes to be evaded or powers to be abused.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Beneficial construction
  • Restrictive construction: consequences, presumption against changes in common law, mens rea in statutory offences
  • Construction to prevent evasion and abuse of powers

Beneficial construction

  • Social welfare and remedial statutes (labour, consumer, rent control, maternity benefit) are construed liberally to advance their object and benefit the class they protect, if the words permit.
CaseWorkmen of American Express International Banking Corporation v Management (1985)

The term "actually worked" under the Industrial Disputes Act (for computing continuous service) was read to include holidays and leave with wages, since a beneficial provision should receive a liberal construction in favour of workmen.

CaseMunicipal Corporation of Delhi v Female Workers (Muster Roll) (2000)

Maternity benefits were extended to women employed on muster roll (daily wage) by the MCD, using Art. 39 and 42 and CEDAW, giving a beneficial interpretation to the Maternity Benefit Act.

2.1 Restrictive construction

2.1.1 Consequences to be considered

Where two interpretations are possible, the court may prefer the one that avoids unreasonable, unjust or inconvenient consequences (argumentum ab inconvenienti), but cannot use consequences to override clear words.

2.1.2 Presumption against changes in the common law

The legislature does not intend to change the common law more than it expressly says or necessarily implies. Statutes in derogation of common law rights are strictly construed.

2.1.3 Mens rea in statutory offences

  • Presumption that mens rea is required unless excluded expressly or by necessary implication (Sherras v De Rutzen, 1895; Nathulal v State of MP, 1966).
  • Exclusion in public welfare and socio-economic offences (State of Maharashtra v Mayer Hans George, 1965).
CaseSrinivas Mall Bairoliya v King-Emperor (1947)

The Privy Council held that offences under food control orders were not to be read as creating absolute liability for a master's servant's acts without knowledge, unless the statute clearly required it; mens rea is an essential ingredient unless excluded.

2.2 Construction to prevent evasion or abuse

2.2.1 Construction to prevent evasion

Courts interpret statutes to defeat devices for evading them; they look at the substance of a transaction, not its form.

CaseMcDowell and Co v Commercial Tax Officer (1985)

Chinnappa Reddy J said colourable devices cannot be part of tax planning; it is wrong to encourage the belief that it is honourable to avoid tax by dubious methods. (Later, Union of India v Azadi Bachao Andolan, 2003, and Vodafone International Holdings v Union of India, 2012, clarified that legitimate tax planning within the law is permissible; only sham and colourable devices are disregarded.)

2.2.2 Construction to prevent abuse of powers

Statutory powers are construed so that they are exercised for the purpose for which they were conferred, reasonably and in good faith; a power conferred on an authority is not construed to permit arbitrary action (Arts. 14, 21).

2.3 Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseKamal Dev Prasad v Mahesh Forge (2025)

The Employees' Compensation Act is beneficial legislation. The Schedule's percentages of disability are not rigid; compensation may reflect the actual loss of earning capacity (functional disability).

CaseDaivshala v Oriental Insurance Co (2025)

An accident while commuting to or from work can arise 'out of and in the course of employment'. The Court read the Employees' Compensation Act consistently with s.51E of the ESI Act, treated as clarificatory.

Points to remember

  • Beneficial construction of welfare laws: functional disability (2025); commuting accidents covered (2025).
  • Welfare legislation is construed beneficially (American Express workmen; MCD female workers).
  • Restrictive construction considers consequences and presumes against changes in common law.
  • Mens rea presumed in offences unless excluded (Sherras; Nathulal; Srinivas Mall).
  • Courts look at substance to prevent evasion (McDowell), but legitimate tax planning is allowed (Azadi Bachao; Vodafone).

Unit 3

Theories of interpretation: restraint and activism

Interpretation reflects a judge's theory of the judicial role: restraint defers to the legislature's words and choices; activism uses interpretation to advance constitutional values and fill gaps.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Theoretical and ideological approaches to interpretation
  • Judicial restraint
  • Judicial activism
  • Juristic restraint

3.1 Theoretical approaches

  • Textualism: meaning is fixed by the words; the judge's role is to apply them.
  • Intentionalism: find the subjective intention of the legislature.
  • Purposivism: find the objective purpose of the statute and give effect to it.
  • Originalism (for constitutions): meaning as understood at the time of adoption.
  • Living constitutionalism: the meaning evolves with society (Indian courts largely follow this for the Constitution).
  • Dynamic interpretation (Eskridge): statutes must be read in light of present circumstances.
  • Critical and law and economics approaches.

3.2 Judicial restraint

Courts should not substitute their views for those of the legislature or executive, avoid deciding policy questions, presume constitutionality of laws, and decide cases on narrow grounds.

CaseDivisional Manager, Aravali Golf Course v Chander Hass (2008)

The Court cautioned against judicial overreach, stating that judges must exercise restraint and not encroach on the legislative or executive domain; creating posts or directing policy is not the function of courts.

3.3 Judicial activism

Courts expand rights and remedies through creative interpretation, especially of Art. 21, relax standing (PIL), read international law into domestic law, and fill legislative gaps by guidelines.

  • Examples: Maneka Gandhi (1978, fair procedure); Vishaka (1997, sexual harassment guidelines); Puttaswamy (2017, privacy); Navtej Johar (2018); environmental cases (Vellore, M.C. Mehta).
  • Criticisms: lack of democratic legitimacy, institutional competence and accountability; erosion of separation of powers.

3.4 Juristic restraint

Restraint exercised by jurists and courts in developing legal doctrine: adherence to precedent, incremental development, respect for legislative text, avoiding broad pronouncements beyond the case, and self-discipline in using canons of construction (Cardozo's view that the judge legislates only between gaps, "from molar to molecular motions").

Points to remember

  • Approaches: textualism, intentionalism, purposivism, originalism, living constitutionalism.
  • Restraint: defer to legislature, presume constitutionality (Aravali Golf Course).
  • Activism: expand rights and remedies (Maneka, Vishaka, Puttaswamy); criticised for overreach.
  • Juristic restraint: incremental, precedent-respecting development.

Unit 4

Presumptions regarding jurisdiction

Courts presume that the legislature does not take away the jurisdiction of ordinary courts, does not bind the State unless it says so, does not legislate beyond its territory without a nexus, and does not intend to violate international law.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Presumption against ousting established jurisdictions
  • against creating new and enlarging established jurisdictions
  • how far statutes affect the Crown (State)
  • territorial extent of legislation
  • how far statutes conferring rights affect foreigners
  • presumption against violation of international law

4.1 Presumption against ousting established jurisdiction

  • Exclusion of civil courts' jurisdiction is not readily inferred; it must be express or clearly implied (s.9 CPC: civil courts try all civil suits unless barred expressly or impliedly).
CaseDhulabhai v State of Madhya Pradesh (1968)

The Supreme Court laid down principles: where a statute gives finality to orders of special tribunals, civil courts' jurisdiction is excluded if there is an adequate remedy; but civil courts can still examine cases where the provisions of the Act have not been complied with or the tribunal has not acted in conformity with fundamental principles of judicial procedure; challenges to the vires of the Act lie in civil courts.

  • Jurisdiction of High Courts under Arts. 226 and 227 and of the Supreme Court under Art. 32 cannot be ousted by statute (L. Chandra Kumar, 1997).

4.2 Presumption against creating new or enlarging established jurisdiction

Statutes are not construed to create new jurisdiction or enlarge existing jurisdiction unless clearly intended; jurisdiction cannot be conferred by consent of parties.

4.3 How far statutes affect the Crown (State)

  • English rule: the Crown is not bound by a statute unless named expressly or by necessary implication.
CaseSuperintendent and Legal Remembrancer, State of West Bengal v Corporation of Calcutta (1967)

A nine-judge bench overruled Director of Rationing v Corporation of Calcutta (1960) and held that the common law rule that the Crown is not bound by statute unless named was not part of Indian law; the State is bound by a statute unless it is exempted expressly or by necessary implication. The State's market was held liable to obtain a municipal licence.

4.4 Further presumptions

4.4.1 Territorial extent of legislation

  • Parliament may make laws for the whole or any part of India, and no law made by Parliament shall be invalid on the ground of extra-territorial operation (Art. 245(2)). State laws must have a territorial nexus.
  • Even Parliament's extra-territorial laws should have a nexus with India's interests.
CaseGVK Industries Ltd v Income Tax Officer (2011)

A Constitution Bench held that Parliament may make laws for extra-territorial aspects or causes that have an impact on or nexus with India; laws for aspects having no nexus with India would be ultra vires.

CaseState of Bombay v R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla (1957)

A Bombay tax on prize competitions run from Mysore through a newspaper circulated in Bombay was valid, because there was a sufficient territorial nexus: the competitions were conducted largely in Bombay.

4.4.2 How far statutes conferring rights affect foreigners

Statutes conferring rights or imposing duties are presumed to apply to persons within the territory, including foreigners, but rights conferred on "citizens" (Arts. 15, 16, 19) do not extend to foreigners; laws are not presumed to regulate conduct of foreigners outside the territory.

4.4.3 Presumption against violation of international law

Courts interpret domestic law, as far as possible, consistently with international law and treaty obligations (Art. 51(c)).

CaseGramophone Co of India v Birendra Bahadur Pandey (1984)

Rules of international law are incorporated into national law and considered part of it unless in conflict with an Act of Parliament; national courts should construe statutes so as to avoid conflict with international law. The Copyright Act was read consistently with transit rights of land-locked Nepal.

Points to remember

  • Exclusion of civil courts' jurisdiction must be express or clearly implied (Dhulabhai).
  • Writ jurisdiction cannot be ousted (L. Chandra Kumar).
  • The State is bound by statute unless exempted (Superintendent and Legal Remembrancer, 1967).
  • Extra-territorial laws need a nexus (GVK Industries; R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla).
  • Statutes construed consistently with international law (Gramophone Co).

Unit 5

Construction most agreeable to justice and reason; exceptional construction

Where language allows a choice, courts choose the meaning that is just and reasonable. Penal and taxing statutes are construed strictly; statutes are presumed to operate prospectively unless they are procedural or clearly retrospective.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Presumptions against inconvenience, unreasonableness, injustice or absurdity
  • against impairing obligations or permitting advantage from one's own wrong
  • retrospective operation
  • Exceptional construction: modification of language to meet intention
  • equitable construction
  • strict construction of penal laws
  • statutes encroaching on rights or imposing burdens

5.1 Construction most agreeable to justice and reason

5.1.1 and 5.1.2 Presumptions against inconvenience, unreasonableness, injustice or absurdity

If the words are capable of two meanings, the court prefers the one that avoids inconvenience, hardship, injustice or absurdity; it cannot, however, rewrite clear words.

5.1.3 Presumption against impairing obligations or permitting advantage from one's own wrong

Nullus commodum capere potest de injuria sua propria: no one can take advantage of his own wrong. Statutes are not construed to allow a wrongdoer to benefit (Riggs v Palmer, 1889: a murderer could not inherit under the victim's will; Indian statutes now expressly disqualify murderers from inheriting, s.25 HSA).

5.1.4 Retrospective operation of statutes

  • Substantive laws are presumed to be prospective unless expressly or by necessary implication made retrospective.
  • Procedural laws apply to pending proceedings unless they affect vested rights.
  • Declaratory and curative statutes may be retrospective.
  • Penal statutes cannot be retrospective to create offences or enhance punishment (Art. 20(1)); beneficial changes may apply.
  • Taxing statutes can be retrospective if Parliament so provides, subject to Art. 14 reasonableness.
CaseHitendra Vishnu Thakur v State of Maharashtra (1994)

The Court summarised: a statute dealing with substantive rights is prima facie prospective unless made retrospective expressly or by necessary implication; procedural law is generally retrospective unless it affects substantive rights; a right of appeal is a substantive right.

CaseVineeta Sharma v Rakesh Sharma (2020)

The 2005 amendment to s.6 of the Hindu Succession Act conferring coparcenary rights on daughters is retroactive: a daughter is a coparcener by birth, and her rights do not depend on her father being alive on 9 September 2005.

5.2 Exceptional construction

5.2.1 Modification of language to meet the intention

Where literal reading leads to manifest absurdity or defeats the evident purpose, courts may modify, add or omit words (rectifying construction), but only where the intention is clear and the error obvious.

5.2.3 Equitable construction

Historically, English courts extended statutes to cases within their "equity" (spirit) though not their letter. Modern courts use purposive construction instead; equitable construction is not favoured where the words are clear.

5.2.4 Strict construction of penal laws

Penal statutes are construed strictly: if two reasonable constructions are possible, the one favouring the accused is adopted (rule of lenity); no one can be punished unless clearly within the words.

CaseTolaram Relumal v State of Bombay (1954)

The Court held that a penal statute must be strictly construed; where a provision admits of two interpretations, the court must lean towards the one exempting the subject from penalty. Receiving "pugree" (premium) for a lease not yet granted was not within the offence as worded.

5.2.5 Statutes encroaching on rights or imposing burdens (taxing statutes)

  • Taxing statutes are strictly construed: there is no equity about tax, no presumption as to tax, nothing is to be read in or implied (Rowlatt J, Cape Brandy Syndicate v IRC, 1921).
  • Exemption notifications: in case of ambiguity, the benefit goes to the revenue, and the burden of proving entitlement is on the assessee.
CaseCommissioner of Customs v Dilip Kumar and Co (2018)

A Constitution Bench held that an exemption notification must be interpreted strictly; the burden is on the assessee to show that his case comes within its terms; in case of ambiguity, the benefit must go to the State. It overruled Sun Export Corporation (1997) on this point.

CaseCommissioner of Income Tax v Vatika Township (2014)

A Constitution Bench held that a legislation imposing a new burden or liability (here, a surcharge on block assessments) is presumed to be prospective, and the principle of fairness requires that tax obligations are not imposed retrospectively unless clearly intended.

Points to remember

  • Courts prefer just and reasonable meanings; no advantage from one's own wrong.
  • Substantive laws prospective, procedural retrospective (Hitendra Vishnu Thakur); 2005 HSA amendment retroactive (Vineeta Sharma).
  • Rectifying construction only for obvious errors; equitable construction disfavoured.
  • Penal statutes strictly construed in favour of the accused (Tolaram Relumal).
  • Taxing statutes strictly construed; exemption ambiguity benefits revenue (Dilip Kumar); new burdens prospective (Vatika Township).

Unit 6

Subordinate principles: maxims and canons

A set of maxims helps courts read words in their setting: words are known by their companions, general words take colour from specific ones, and the express mention of one thing may exclude another.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Usage and contemporanea expositio
  • Construction imposed by statute
  • Words in bonam partem
  • Change of language
  • Associated words: noscitur a sociis
  • expressio unius
  • Generic words following specific (ejusdem generis)
  • Meaning of particular expressions

6.1 Usage and contemporanea expositio

Contemporanea expositio est optima et fortissima in lege: a contemporaneous exposition is the best and strongest in law. Long usage and contemporaneous official interpretation of an old statute may be relied upon where the words are ambiguous, but cannot override clear words.

CaseDesh Bandhu Gupta and Co v Delhi Stock Exchange Association (1979)

The Court relied on the contemporaneous understanding of a notification by the authorities and those affected to interpret it, holding that contemporanea expositio is a useful aid for old statutes and documents.

6.2 Construction imposed by statute

Where a statute (or the General Clauses Act) prescribes how words are to be understood (definitions, deeming provisions, interpretation clauses), that construction must be followed. Deeming fictions must be carried to their logical conclusion but only for the purpose for which they are created (East End Dwellings Co v Finsbury Borough Council, 1952; State of Bombay v Pandurang Vinayak, 1953).

6.3 Words in bonam partem

Words are construed in their lawful sense (in bonam partem); a statute is presumed not to intend an unlawful purpose. For example, a provision dealing with a "contract" refers to a lawful contract.

6.4 Change of language

A change of language in an amending or later statute, or within the same statute, raises a presumption of a change of intention; the same word in the same statute is ordinarily given the same meaning.

6.5 Associated words and expressio unius

Noscitur a sociis (a word is known by the company it keeps)

Where two or more words susceptible of analogous meaning are coupled together, they take their colour from each other.

CaseState of Bombay v Hospital Mazdoor Sabha (1960)

Applying (and limiting) noscitur a sociis, the Court held that a hospital run by the government was an "industry" under the IDA; the associated words in the definition did not restrict "undertaking" to trade and business in a narrow sense. The maxim is only an aid where meaning is doubtful.

CaseRainbow Steels Ltd v Commissioner of Sales Tax (1981)

The word "old" in "old, discarded, unserviceable or obsolete machinery" was read in the light of its associated words to mean machinery that had become useless for its original purpose, not merely second-hand.

Expressio unius est exclusio alterius

The express mention of one thing implies the exclusion of another. It is an aid only, and must be applied with caution, since the mention may be for abundant caution.

6.6 Generic words following specific words: ejusdem generis

Where general words follow specific words of a distinct category (genus), the general words are limited to things of the same class. Conditions: (1) the specific words form a distinct genus or category; (2) the general words follow the specific ones; (3) the statute shows no different intention.

CaseAmar Chandra Chakraborty v Collector of Excise (1972)

The Court explained the conditions for applying ejusdem generis: the statute contains an enumeration of specific words; the subjects of enumeration constitute a class or category; that class is not exhausted by the enumeration; the general term follows the enumeration; and there is no indication of a different legislative intent.

CaseSiddeshwari Cotton Mills v Union of India (1989)

Processes like "bleaching, mercerising, dyeing, printing, water-proofing, rubberising, shrink-proofing, organdie processing" share the character of bringing about a lasting change in fabric; "any other process" was read ejusdem generis to exclude calendering, which produced only a temporary effect.

6.7 Meaning of particular expressions

  • "Shall" and "may": "shall" is ordinarily mandatory and "may" directory, but context decides (Unit 7).
  • "And" and "or": may be read conjunctively or disjunctively to give effect to intention.
  • "Includes": extends; "means": exhausts; "means and includes": exhaustive.
  • "Notwithstanding": non obstante clause gives overriding effect.
  • "Subject to": makes the provision subordinate to another.
  • Reddendo singula singulis: where several words are followed by several objects, each word refers to its appropriate object.
Exam tip

For ejusdem generis, list the conditions from Amar Chandra Chakraborty and apply them to a problem. For noscitur a sociis, contrast its use in Rainbow Steels with its limits in Hospital Mazdoor Sabha.

Points to remember

  • Contemporanea expositio aids old statutes (Desh Bandhu Gupta).
  • Statutory definitions and deeming fictions bind.
  • Words read in their lawful sense; change of language suggests change of intention.
  • Noscitur a sociis (Rainbow Steels; limits in Hospital Mazdoor Sabha).
  • Expressio unius: express mention excludes others, used with caution.
  • Ejusdem generis: conditions (Amar Chandra Chakraborty; Siddeshwari Cotton Mills).

Unit 7

Intentions attributed to the legislature when it expresses none

Where a statute does not say what happens if a requirement is not followed, courts decide whether the requirement is mandatory or directory, whether a duty is absolute, and what remedy the legislature intended.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Imperative and directory enactments
  • Absolute and qualified duties
  • Impossibility of compliance
  • Waiver
  • Public and private remedies

7.1 Imperative (mandatory) and directory enactments

  • A mandatory provision must be strictly complied with; non-compliance invalidates the act.
  • A directory provision requires substantial compliance; non-compliance does not invalidate the act, though it may attract other consequences.
  • Tests: the language ("shall", negative words, "only"), the scheme and object of the statute, the consequence of non-compliance (whether specified), whether it affects public duties or private rights, and the inconvenience or injustice of treating it as mandatory.
CaseState of Uttar Pradesh v Manbodhan Lal Srivastava (1957)

Art. 320(3)(c), requiring consultation with the Public Service Commission before disciplinary action, was held directory: failure to consult did not invalidate the dismissal, because the Constitution did not provide the consequence and the provision was not meant to confer a right on the civil servant.

CaseMay George v Special Tahsildar (2010)

The Court summarised that the question whether a provision is mandatory or directory depends on the intent of the legislature, not the language alone; the court must consider the whole scope and object of the statute, the consequences, and whether penalty is provided for non-compliance.

  • "Shall" may be read as "may" and vice versa: Bachahan Devi v Nagar Nigam, Gorakhpur (2008).
  • Provisions relating to time limits for public officers are usually directory; those conferring rights or imposing conditions on private persons (for example, notice under s.80 CPC) may be mandatory.

7.2 Absolute and qualified duties

  • An absolute duty must be performed regardless of difficulty (strict liability statutes, safety duties under factory laws).
  • A qualified duty requires the duty-holder to do what is "reasonably practicable" or to use due diligence.

7.3 Impossibility of compliance

Lex non cogit ad impossibilia: the law does not compel the impossible. Impotentia excusat legem: inability excuses the law. Where compliance with a mandatory requirement is impossible without the party's fault, non-compliance may be excused.

CaseRaj Kumar Dey v Tarapada Dey (1987)

Where the last day for filing fell on a day when the office was closed, the act done on the next working day was valid, applying lex non cogit ad impossibilia.

7.4 Waiver

Quilibet potest renunciare juri pro se introducto: everyone may renounce a right introduced for his own benefit. A party may waive a statutory requirement enacted for his own benefit, but not one enacted for public policy or public benefit. Fundamental Rights cannot be waived (Basheshar Nath, 1959).

7.5 Public and private remedies

  • Where a statute creates a right and also provides a specific remedy, the remedy is generally exclusive.
CasePremier Automobiles Ltd v Kamlekar Shantaram Wadke (1976)

Where an industrial dispute relates to the enforcement of a right or obligation created only under the Industrial Disputes Act, the remedy is only under that Act; the civil court's jurisdiction is excluded. If the right exists under general law, the party may choose either remedy.

  • Breach of a statutory duty may give rise to a private civil action for damages if the statute was intended to protect a limited class and does not provide an adequate remedy (the tort of breach of statutory duty).

Points to remember

  • Mandatory vs directory depends on intention, object and consequences (Manbodhan Lal; May George).
  • Absolute duties vs duties qualified by reasonable practicability.
  • Impossibility excuses (Raj Kumar Dey).
  • Waiver of rights for one's own benefit allowed; not of public rights or Fundamental Rights.
  • A right created by statute with its own remedy must be enforced by that remedy (Premier Automobiles).

Unit 8

Stare decisis

India inherited the English doctrine of precedent, but adapted it: the Supreme Court can overrule itself, uses prospective overruling, and has developed techniques to avoid inconvenient precedents.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • The doctrine as inherited
  • Techniques of innovation and subversion of stare decisis
  • Supreme Court's authority to overrule its own decisions (Antulay's case)
  • Advisory jurisdiction and its import on precedent
  • Prospective overruling
  • Objections to judicial review as anti-majoritarian

8.1 The doctrine as inherited

  • Stare decisis et non quieta movere: stand by decided cases and do not disturb settled matters.
  • Art. 141: the law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts within India. High Court decisions bind subordinate courts in the State and are persuasive elsewhere.
  • Hierarchy within the Supreme Court: a smaller bench is bound by a larger bench; a bench of co-equal strength should refer a doubt to a larger bench (Central Board of Dawoodi Bohra Community v State of Maharashtra, 2005).

8.2 Techniques of innovation (or subversion) of stare decisis

  • Distinguishing a precedent on material facts.
  • Treating a statement as obiter dictum.
  • Holding a decision per incuriam (rendered in ignorance of a statute or binding precedent).
  • Sub silentio: a point not argued or considered is not an authority.
  • Overruling by a larger bench.
  • Reading down or confining the precedent to its facts.
CaseA.R. Antulay v R.S. Nayak (1988)

A seven-judge bench recalled earlier directions of a five-judge bench (1984) transferring Antulay's trial to the High Court, holding them per incuriam and violative of his rights under Arts. 14 and 21 (since the statute required trial by a Special Judge), and that the Court could correct its own errors to prevent injustice, even after finality.

8.3 Supreme Court's authority to overrule its own decisions

CaseBengal Immunity Co v State of Bihar (1955)

The Supreme Court held that Art. 141 does not bind the Supreme Court itself; it can depart from its previous decisions if convinced they are erroneous and injurious to public welfare, though it should do so cautiously.

8.4 Advisory jurisdiction and precedent

  • Opinions under Art. 143 are not binding on the President, but are of high persuasive authority and generally followed.
  • Whether they are "law declared" under Art. 141 has been debated; in In re Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal (1991) the Court treated its opinion as binding law; others treat it as persuasive.
  • The 2025 Presidential Reference (assent to Bills) overruled, in effect, the April 2025 two-judge bench decision on timelines, showing that advisory opinions can reshape precedent.

8.5 Prospective overruling in India

CaseI.C. Golak Nath v State of Punjab (1967)

The Court applied the American doctrine of prospective overruling: earlier amendments were valid, but the new rule (Parliament cannot abridge Fundamental Rights) would apply only in future. Limitations: it applies only to constitutional matters, can be applied only by the Supreme Court, and its scope is at the discretion of the Court.

  • Later used in other contexts (for example, Ashok Kumar Gupta v State of UP, 1997; Somaiya Organics v State of UP, 2001), and the Court has applied it to statutory interpretation as well.

8.6 Objections to judicial review as anti-majoritarian

  • Counter-majoritarian difficulty (Alexander Bickel, 1962): unelected judges invalidate decisions of elected representatives.
  • Responses: the Constitution itself is the supreme expression of the people's will; courts protect minorities and fundamental rights from majorities; judicial review is a basic feature (L. Chandra Kumar); the people can amend the Constitution (subject to basic structure).

8.7 Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseRohan Vijay Nahar v State of Maharashtra (2025)

Courts bound by Art. 141 must follow the law declared by the Supreme Court; judicial discipline is part of the rule of law.

CaseRejanish K.V. v K. Deepa (2025)

A larger bench overruled Dheeraj Mor (2020) on eligibility of in-service judges for direct recruitment, showing how a larger bench corrects precedent.

Points to remember

  • Judicial discipline under Art. 141 (Rohan Vijay Nahar, 2025).
  • Art. 141 binds all courts; larger benches bind smaller ones (Dawoodi Bohra).
  • Techniques: distinguishing, obiter, per incuriam, sub silentio, overruling.
  • Antulay (1988): the Court recalled per incuriam directions to prevent injustice.
  • The Supreme Court can overrule itself (Bengal Immunity).
  • Advisory opinions are of high persuasive value.
  • Prospective overruling (Golak Nath).
  • Counter-majoritarian objection and its answers.

Unit 9

Statutory interpretation as an aspect of the judicial process

The rules of interpretation are not statutes but judicial practices. They balance legalism and creativity, and reflect the values judges bring to the text.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Rules of interpretation as judge-made
  • Legalism and literal interpretation
  • Creativity: mischief and golden rules
  • Self-discipline: construction of fiscal and criminal statutes
  • Technical rules on necessary and implied repeal
  • codifying, consolidating and amending statutes
  • Values and interpretation

9.1 Judge-made character of the rules

The canons of construction were developed by courts over centuries; they are aids, not rules of law, and may conflict with one another. The General Clauses Act codifies some, but most remain judicial.

9.2 Legalism and literal interpretation

Legalism values certainty, predictability and separation of powers; the literal rule embodies it. Criticism: words have no fixed meaning; literalism can produce absurd or unjust results.

9.3 Creativity: mischief and golden rules

The mischief and golden rules, and purposive construction, give judges room to adapt the law to its purpose. Lord Denning in Seaford Court Estates v Asher (1949) said a judge should "iron out the creases" when a defect appears, asking how the makers would have dealt with it; the House of Lords in Magor and St Mellons RDC v Newport Corporation (1952) rejected this as "a naked usurpation of the legislative function", but the purposive approach has since gained acceptance.

9.4 Self-discipline: fiscal and criminal statutes

Strict construction of taxing and penal statutes (Unit 5) is an example of judicial self-discipline: courts do not extend burdens or offences by analogy.

9.5 Technical rules

Necessary and implied repeal

  • Express repeal: s.6 of the General Clauses Act preserves rights, liabilities, penalties and proceedings under a repealed Act unless a different intention appears.
  • Implied repeal: not favoured; arises only where two provisions are so inconsistent that both cannot stand together (leges posteriores priores contrarias abrogant). A general later law does not repeal a special earlier law (generalia specialibus non derogant).
CaseMunicipal Council, Palai v T.J. Joseph (1963)

There is a presumption against implied repeal; it can be inferred only where the provisions of the later enactment are so inconsistent with or repugnant to the earlier one that the two cannot stand together.

Codifying, consolidating and amending statutes

  • Codifying statute: states the whole law on a subject (statute and case law); interpreted by its own language, without first referring to the earlier law, unless the language is ambiguous: Bank of England v Vagliano Brothers (1891).
  • Consolidating statute: brings together existing statutory provisions; presumed not to change the law.
  • Amending statute: read with the principal Act as a whole.

9.6 Values and interpretation

Interpretation is guided by constitutional values (equality, liberty, dignity, social justice, secularism, federalism). Courts read statutes to conform to the Constitution (reading down: R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla; Navtej Johar), and use the Directive Principles and international conventions to give content to vague words.

9.7 Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseState of UP v Jai Bir Singh (2026)

A nine-judge bench held that pending disputes under the repealed Industrial Disputes Act remain governed by the Bangalore Water Supply test, while the definition of 'industry' in s.2(p) of the Industrial Relations Code must be read on its own terms. A repealing Code does not carry the old judicial gloss with it.

Points to remember

  • New Codes read on their own terms (Jai Bir Singh, 2026).
  • Interpretive rules are judge-made aids.
  • Legalism (literal rule) vs creativity (mischief, golden, purposive).
  • Self-discipline: strict construction of fiscal and penal laws.
  • Presumption against implied repeal (Palai v Joseph); generalia specialibus non derogant; s.6 General Clauses Act.
  • Codifying statutes read on their own terms (Vagliano).
  • Constitutional values shape interpretation.

Unit 10

Constitutional interpretation

A constitution is a living document meant to endure, so it is interpreted broadly and purposively, with special doctrines to resolve conflicts between legislative lists.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Difference from statutory interpretation (Queen v Burah)
  • Literal interpretation
  • Harmonious construction
  • Reference to Constituent Assembly debates
  • Pith and substance
  • Occupied field
  • Residuary power
  • Repugnancy
  • Amending power
  • Directive Principles as sources

10.1 Difference from statutory interpretation

  • A constitution is the supreme law, framed in broad terms, meant to last for ages and to be adapted to various crises (Marshall CJ, McCulloch v Maryland, 1819: "it is a constitution we are expounding").
  • It must be interpreted liberally and generously, not narrowly and pedantically.
CaseThe Queen v Burah (1878)

The Privy Council held that the Indian legislature, when acting within the limits of its powers, has plenary powers of legislation as large and of the same nature as those of the British Parliament; it is not a mere delegate. Its powers must be ascertained by looking at the terms of the instrument creating it, and if what was done is within the powers expressly conferred, courts cannot enlarge or restrict them by inferences. The case also upheld conditional legislation (leaving the date and area of application to the executive).

10.2 Literal interpretation

Clear words of the Constitution are given effect (A.K. Gopalan, 1950, adopted a literal reading of Art. 21 which was later abandoned in Maneka Gandhi, 1978).

10.3 Harmonious construction

Provisions of the Constitution are read together so that each is given effect.

CaseVenkataramana Devaru v State of Mysore (1958)

Art. 25(2)(b) (throwing open Hindu religious institutions to all classes) and Art. 26(b) (right of a religious denomination to manage its own affairs in matters of religion) were harmonised: the temple had to be opened to all Hindus, but the denomination could exclude the public from certain special ceremonies.

10.4 Reference to Constituent Assembly debates

Initially, courts were reluctant (A.K. Gopalan, 1950: debates are not admissible to construe the Constitution, though they show the background). Later, debates were used extensively as aids to understand the purpose of provisions (Kesavananda, 1973; S.R. Bommai, 1994; Puttaswamy, 2017; Supreme Court AoR Association, 2015).

10.5 Pith and substance

To decide whether a law falls within a legislative entry, the court examines its true nature and character (pith and substance); incidental encroachment on another list does not invalidate it.

CasePrafulla Kumar Mukherjee v Bank of Commerce, Khulna (1947)

A Bengal money-lenders law was in pith and substance a law on money-lending (a provincial subject) and valid, although it incidentally affected promissory notes (a federal subject).

10.6 Occupied field

When Parliament has enacted a law on a Concurrent List matter intending to occupy the whole field, a State law on the same field is void to the extent of repugnancy. Even without direct conflict, a State law is invalid if Parliament intended to cover the entire field.

10.7 Residuary power

  • Art. 248 and Entry 97 of List I vest residuary power in Parliament.
CaseUnion of India v H.S. Dhillon (1971)

By majority, the Court held that a wealth tax on the capital value of assets including agricultural land was within Parliament's competence, either under Entry 86 or the residuary Entry 97 of List I, since the tax was not on agricultural land as such; the residuary power should not be read narrowly.

10.8 Repugnancy

Under Art. 254, a Union law on a Concurrent List subject prevails over an inconsistent State law; a State law reserved for and assented to by the President prevails in that State, subject to later Union law (Zaverbhai Amaidas v State of Bombay, 1954; M. Karunanidhi v Union of India, 1979).

10.9 Amending power

Art. 368 is interpreted subject to the basic structure (Kesavananda, 1973; Minerva Mills, 1980). Amendments are interpreted like other constitutional provisions but tested against basic features.

10.10 Directive Principles as sources of constitutional interpretation

Directive Principles guide the interpretation of Fundamental Rights (Art. 21 expansion through Arts. 39, 41, 45, 47, 48A), the reasonableness of restrictions under Art. 19, and the meaning of statutes (Randhir Singh, 1982; Unni Krishnan, 1993; Mirzapur Moti Kureshi, 2005).

Exam tip

In any constitutional interpretation question, contrast the narrow approach (A.K. Gopalan) with the broad, purposive approach (Maneka Gandhi; Kesavananda), and illustrate the federal doctrines (pith and substance, occupied field, repugnancy, residuary power) with one case each.

Points to remember

  • A constitution is interpreted broadly; the Indian legislature has plenary powers (Queen v Burah).
  • Harmonious construction (Venkataramana Devaru).
  • Constituent Assembly debates are now freely used.
  • Pith and substance (Prafulla Kumar Mukherjee); occupied field and repugnancy (Art. 254).
  • Residuary power with Parliament, broadly read (H.S. Dhillon).
  • Amending power subject to basic structure; Directive Principles guide interpretation.

Part A Short answer questions5 × 4 = 20 marks

Answer any FIVE of the following.

1.Literal rule
Model answer

Words are given their plain, ordinary and grammatical meaning. If the language is clear, it must be applied even if the result is harsh, because the remedy lies with the legislature (Sussex Peerage case, 1844; Nokes v Doncaster Amalgamated Collieries, 1940).

  • In India: Nelson Motis v Union of India (1992).
  • Limits: words take colour from context, and absurdity may justify departure (the golden rule).
2.Mischief rule
Model answer

The rule in Heydon's case (1584) asks four questions:

  1. what was the common law before the Act;
  2. what was the mischief or defect;
  3. what remedy did Parliament provide; and
  4. what was the true reason for the remedy?

The court then suppresses the mischief and advances the remedy.

  • Bengal Immunity Co v State of Bihar (1955) applied it to Art. 286.
  • Smith v Hughes (1960): soliciting from balconies was treated as soliciting "in a street".
3.Golden rule
Model answer

The literal meaning is followed unless it leads to absurdity, repugnancy or inconsistency. In that case, the meaning may be modified to the extent needed to avoid the absurdity (Grey v Pearson, 1857; Adler v George, 1964, where "in the vicinity of" was read to include "in").

In India: Lee Kun Hee v State of UP (2012), and State of Punjab v Quiser Jehan Begum (1963) on "knowledge" of an award.

4.Ejusdem generis
Model answer

Where general words follow specific words of the same class (genus), the general words are limited to things of the same kind. Conditions:

  • the specific words must form a distinct genus; and
  • there must be no contrary intention.

Examples:

  • Powell v Kempton Park Racecourse (1899).
  • Kavalappara Kottarathil Kochuni v State of Madras (1960).
  • Rajasthan SEB v Mohan Lal (1967) refused to apply the rule to "other authorities" in Art. 12, because the preceding words formed no single genus.
5.Noscitur a sociis
Model answer

A word is known by the company it keeps. A doubtful word takes its meaning from the words around it, which is wider than ejusdem generis. Examples:

  • Foster v Diphwys Casson (1887).
  • Pengelly v Bell Brick (1964).
  • State of Bombay v Hospital Mazdoor Sabha (1960) declined to narrow "industry" by applying this rule.
6.Reading down
Model answer

Where a provision is capable of two interpretations, one of which would make it unconstitutional, the court adopts the narrower interpretation that keeps it valid. This rests on the presumption of constitutionality.

  • Kedar Nath Singh v State of Bihar (1962) read sedition down to incitement to violence.
  • Independent Thought v Union of India (2017) read down the marital rape exception for minors.

The court cannot rewrite the statute.

7.Harmonious construction
Model answer

Where two provisions appear to conflict, they are to be construed so as to give effect to both, avoiding a head-on clash.

  • Venkataramana Devaru v State of Mysore (1958): Art. 25(2)(b), temple entry, was harmonised with Art. 26(b), denominational rights.
  • Calcutta Gas Co v State of WB (1962).
  • In re Kerala Education Bill (1958): Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

The rule is based on the presumption that the legislature does not intend contradiction.

8.Casus omissus
Model answer

A case omitted by the legislature cannot be supplied by the court. Judges interpret; they do not legislate (Hansraj Gupta v Dehra Dun-Mussoorie Electric Tramway, 1933; Padmasundara Rao v State of TN, 2002).

  • An omission may be supplied only in rare cases of clear necessity, apparent from the statute itself.
  • Lord Denning's "ironing out the creases" (Seaford Court Estates v Asher, 1949) is the liberal exception.
9.Beneficial construction
Model answer

Welfare and social legislation is construed liberally, in favour of the class it seeks to protect, where two views are possible. Examples:

  • labour laws: Kamal Dev Prasad v Mahesh Forge (2025), functional disability under the Employees' Compensation Act;
  • Daivshala v Oriental Insurance (2025), commuting accidents;
  • maternity benefit: Hamsaanandini Nanduri (2026); and
  • senior citizens: Urmila Dixit (2025).

Beneficial construction cannot override clear language.

Answer any TWO of the following.

10.Compare the literal, golden and mischief rules of interpretation. Which approach do Indian courts prefer?
Model answer

Literal rule

  • Content: plain meaning; the court gives effect to the words even if the result is harsh (Sussex Peerage, 1844).
  • Merits: certainty, and respect for legislative supremacy.
  • Demerits: absurdity and injustice. Examples: Whiteley v Chappell (1868), where impersonating "a person entitled to vote" did not cover a dead voter; and Fisher v Bell (1961), where a display was held an invitation to treat.

Golden rule

  • Content: a modification of the literal rule to avoid absurdity (Grey v Pearson, 1857; R v Sigsworth, 1935, where a murderer could not inherit from his victim).
  • Merits: avoids absurd results.
  • Demerits: "absurdity" is subjective.

Mischief rule

  • Content: Heydon's case (1584): identify the mischief and the remedy, and interpret to suppress the mischief.
  • Merits: gives effect to purpose.
  • Demerits: reliance on historical context, with uncertainty about the intent.

Purposive approach

The modern development of the mischief rule: Pepper v Hart (1993), which allows reference to Hansard, and the purposive approach in the EU and the UK.

Indian approach

  • The literal rule is primary when the language is clear (Nelson Motis, 1992; Union of India v Hansoli Devi, 2002).
  • Purposive interpretation applies to remedial, welfare and constitutional provisions:
  • Bengal Immunity (1955);
  • Reserve Bank of India v Peerless (1987), "text and context";
  • Abhiram Singh v C.D. Commachen (2017), purposive reading of "his religion".
  • Taxing and penal statutes are construed strictly (Commissioner of Customs v Dilip Kumar, 2018, five judges: ambiguity in an exemption notification goes to the Revenue).
  • New labour Codes are read on their own terms, not through old judicial glosses (Jai Bir Singh, 2026).

Conclusion

Indian courts start from the text but read it in context and with purpose. Text and context together give meaning. The choice of rule depends on the nature of the statute.

11.Discuss the internal and external aids to interpretation.
Model answer

Internal aids (within the statute)

  1. Long title: shows the scope and purpose of the Act (Manohar Lal v State of Punjab, 1961).
  2. Preamble: a key to the mind of the legislature where the language is ambiguous (Kesavananda for the Constitution's Preamble; Burrakur Coal Co v Union of India, 1961).
  3. Headings: may be referred to when ambiguous (Bhinka v Charan Singh, 1959).
  4. Marginal notes: generally not used, though used for the Constitution (Bengal Immunity, 1955).
  5. Definitions: "means" is exhaustive; "includes" is extensive.
  6. Illustrations: help in interpretation but cannot curtail the section (Shambhu Nath Mehra v State of Ajmer, 1956).
  7. Provisos: carve out exceptions (CIT v Indo Mercantile Bank, 1959).
  8. Explanations: clarify, and may widen the provision.
  9. Schedules, punctuation (a minor role) and non-obstante clauses.

External aids

  1. Parliamentary history: Statement of Objects and Reasons, debates, Constituent Assembly debates and committee reports, used to understand the context and mischief, not to control clear words (R.S. Nayak v A.R. Antulay, 1984; Kalpana Mehta v Union of India, 2018).
  2. Historical facts and surrounding circumstances.
  3. Social, political and economic developments, and science: updating construction (State v S.J. Choudhary, 1996).
  4. Reference to other statutes: in pari materia statutes (Daivshala, 2025, reading the EC Act with the ESI Act).
  5. Dictionaries.
  6. Judicial decisions, including foreign decisions: persuasive.
  7. International conventions: to read domestic law consistently (Vishaka, 1997).
  8. Textbooks and the General Clauses Act, 1897.
  9. Executive construction and usage: contemporanea expositio.

Conclusion

Aids help ascertain legislative intent. They are used to resolve ambiguity, and never to override clear statutory language.

12."Penal statutes are to be strictly construed, while taxing statutes are to be construed in favour of the subject." Discuss.
Model answer

Penal statutes

  • Rule of strict construction: no one can be punished unless the case falls clearly within the words. If two reasonable constructions are possible, the one favouring the accused is adopted (the rule of lenity) (Tolaram Relumal v State of Bombay, 1954).
  • Mens rea presumption: Nathulal v State of MP (1966).
  • Art. 20(1): no ex post facto criminal laws.
  • Limits: strict construction does not mean ignoring the purpose. Social welfare penal statutes, such as food adulteration and dowry laws, are interpreted to suppress the mischief (Kisan Trimbak Kothula v State of Maharashtra, 1977).
  • Courts do not create offences: creating offences is legislative (Ashwini Kumar Upadhyay, 2026).

Taxing statutes

  • "In a taxing Act, one has to look merely at what is clearly said. There is no room for any intendment" (Cape Brandy Syndicate v IRC, 1921). Tax is not imposed by implication.
  • Ambiguity in a charging provision goes in favour of the subject (CIT v Vegetable Products, 1973).
  • Exemption notifications are strictly construed against the assessee; the burden is on the assessee (Commissioner of Customs v Dilip Kumar, 2018, five judges, overruling Sun Export).
  • Machinery provisions are construed to make the charge effective.
  • Substance over form and anti-avoidance: McDowell (1985); Azadi Bachao Andolan (2003); Vodafone (2012). Under the GAAR, the Court has denied treaty benefits to structures lacking commercial substance (Tiger Global, 2026).

Common rationale

Both kinds of statute impose burdens on the subject, so the legislature must speak clearly.

Conclusion

The maxims hold good, subject to purposive reading in welfare penal laws, strict construction of tax exemptions, and anti-avoidance principles.

13.Explain the principles of constitutional interpretation followed by the Supreme Court.
Model answer

The Constitution as a living document

It must be interpreted broadly and liberally, not in a narrow, pedantic way (A.K. Gopalan versus Maneka Gandhi; the "living tree" approach).

Principles

  1. Harmonious construction: Parts III and IV (Minerva Mills, 1980); Arts. 25 and 26.
  2. Pith and substance, and colourable legislation: for federal entries.
  3. Doctrine of eclipse and severability (R.M.D.C. v Union of India, 1957).
  4. Presumption of constitutionality and reading down.
  5. Progressive and purposive interpretation: reading in unenumerated rights under Art. 21, such as privacy (Puttaswamy, 2017).
  6. Basic structure: a limit on amendment (Kesavananda, 1973).
  7. Transformative constitutionalism: Navtej Singh Johar (2018); Joseph Shine (2018).

  10. Use of foreign and international law.

  12. Silences and gaps: when the Constitution is silent, the Court will not supply rigid timelines in the guise of interpretation. Advisory opinions under Art. 143

Whether they bind as precedent under Art. 141 is debated, but they carry high authority and are generally followed (In re Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal, 1991).

Conclusion

Constitutional interpretation balances text, structure, history and evolving values. The Court treats the Constitution as an organic document to be read to secure its goals.

14.A statute defines "vehicle" as including "cars, buses, lorries and other conveyances". The question is whether a bicycle is covered by a ban on "vehicles" in a park. Apply the rules of interpretation.
Model answer

Issue: The scope of the general words "other conveyances".

Law:

  • Ejusdem generis: general words following a list of specific items of one genus are limited to that genus (Powell v Kempton Park, 1899).
  • The purposive approach: what was the mischief, for example safety and noise in the park?

Application: Cars, buses and lorries form a genus of motor vehicles. Under ejusdem generis, "other conveyances" would mean motorised conveyances, which excludes a bicycle. If the purpose is pedestrian safety generally, a court might read the provision more widely. Hart's "vehicles in the park" example illustrates the penumbra of uncertainty.

Conclusion: On the usual rule, a bicycle is not covered, unless the statute's purpose clearly shows an intention to ban all conveyances.

15.A penal statute punishes "any person who sells adulterated food". X stores adulterated food for sale but has not yet sold any. Is he guilty?
Model answer

Issue: Can "sells" be extended to "stores for sale" in a penal statute?

Law:

  • Penal statutes are strictly construed; ambiguity goes to the accused (Tolaram Relumal, 1954).
  • Courts cannot supply a casus omissus.
  • Where the legislature intended to cover storage, it says so; the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 covers storage and distribution expressly.

Application: "Sells" does not plainly cover storage. Extending it would create an offence by interpretation.

Conclusion: X is not guilty under this provision as framed, unless another provision covers storage or attempt.

16.A worker is injured in a road accident while travelling from home to his factory. The employer's insurer denies compensation under the Employees' Compensation Act, arguing that the accident did not arise "out of and in the course of employment". Apply the rules of interpretation.
Model answer

Issue: Should the phrase be read narrowly or beneficially?

Law:

  • Welfare legislation is construed beneficially (Kamal Dev Prasad v Mahesh Forge, 2025).
  • Daivshala v Oriental Insurance (2025): an accident while commuting to or from work can arise out of and in the course of employment. The EC Act was read consistently with s.51E of the ESI Act (statutes in pari materia), treated as clarificatory.

Application: The worker was commuting to his place of work, and the causal connection with employment can be established.

Conclusion: Applying beneficial and in pari materia construction, the worker is entitled to compensation.

17.Article 25(2)(b) empowers the State to throw open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes of Hindus. Article 26(b) gives every religious denomination the right to manage its own affairs in matters of religion. A denominational temple bars non-members. How should the conflict be resolved?
Model answer

Issue: The conflict between Arts. 25(2)(b) and 26(b).

Law: Sri Venkataramana Devaru v State of Mysore (1958): by harmonious construction, Art. 26(b) is subject to Art. 25(2)(b). A denominational temple must be thrown open to all Hindus. However, the denomination may exclude the public from certain special religious services where only members may participate, so long as the temple is not closed to the public as a whole. The Sabarimala reference (nine judges, reserved May 2026) is re-examining related questions.

Application: A total ban on non-member Hindus violates Art. 25(2)(b). Limited, religiously necessary restrictions on special rituals may be valid.

Conclusion: Using harmonious construction, the temple must admit all Hindus to general worship, while retaining denominational control over special rites.
