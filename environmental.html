Unit 1

Meaning of environment and pollution

Indian law defines environment broadly, as the web of relationships among water, air, land, living creatures and property, and treats pollution as anything that harms that web.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Definitions in the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and the Water Act, 1974
  • Forests under the Indian Forest Act, 1927 and the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980
  • Environment in the Constitution
  • Causative factors of pollution

Statutory definitions

Statutes.2(a), Environment (Protection) Act, 1986

"Environment" includes water, air and land and the inter-relationship which exists among and between water, air and land, and human beings, other living creatures, plants, micro-organism and property.

Statutes.2(b) and (c), EPA

"Environmental pollutant" means any solid, liquid or gaseous substance present in such concentration as may be, or tend to be, injurious to environment. "Environmental pollution" means the presence in the environment of any environmental pollutant.

  • s.2(e) defines "hazardous substance" as any substance or preparation which, by reason of its chemical or physico-chemical properties or handling, is liable to cause harm to human beings, other living creatures, plants, micro-organisms, property or the environment.
Statutes.2(e), Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974

"Pollution" means such contamination of water or such alteration of the physical, chemical or biological properties of water or such discharge of any sewage or trade effluent or of any other liquid, gaseous or solid substance into water as may, or is likely to, create a nuisance or render such water harmful or injurious to public health or safety, or to domestic, commercial, industrial, agricultural or other legitimate uses, or to the life and health of animals or plants or aquatic organisms.

The Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981 similarly defines "air pollutant" (including noise) and "air pollution" (s.2(a), (b)).

Forests

  • The Indian Forest Act, 1927 does not define "forest"; it provides for reserved forests, village forests and protected forests and regulates timber transit.
  • The Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 restricts de-reservation of forests and use of forest land for non-forest purposes without prior approval of the Central Government.
CaseT.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v Union of India (1996)

The Supreme Court held that "forest" must be understood in its dictionary sense, covering all statutorily recognised forests, whether designated reserved, protected or otherwise, and also any area recorded as forest in government records, irrespective of ownership. The Forest (Conservation) Act applies to all such forests. This continuing mandamus case has produced hundreds of orders on forest governance.

Current law

The Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act, 2023 renamed the 1980 Act the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam, 1980 and limited its application mainly to land notified as forest or recorded as forest in government records on or after 25 October 1980, with exemptions for certain strategic and linear projects. Petitions argue this narrows Godavarman's dictionary meaning; the Supreme Court has directed in interim orders that the broad definition continue to be followed pending decision.

CaseSubhash Kumar v State of Bihar (1991)

The right to life under Art. 21 includes the right to enjoyment of pollution-free water and air. A citizen may approach the Court under Art. 32 to remove pollution of water or air that impairs quality of life (though the petition, filed for personal motives, was dismissed).

1.2 Causative factors of pollution

  • Population growth and poverty: pressure on land, water and forests; lack of sanitation.
  • Industrialisation: effluents, emissions, hazardous waste, accidents (Bhopal, 1984).
  • Urbanisation: sewage, solid waste, vehicular emissions, construction dust.
  • Agriculture: chemical fertilisers and pesticides, stubble burning.
  • Mining and deforestation: soil erosion, loss of biodiversity, siltation.
  • Energy production: thermal power, large dams.
  • Consumerism and technology: plastics, e-waste.
  • Weak enforcement: under-staffed pollution control boards and low penalties.
Exam tip

Begin any environmental law answer with the s.2(a) EPA definition and Art. 48A and 51A(g); then link to Art. 21 through Subhash Kumar.

Points to remember

  • EPA defines environment as the inter-relationship of water, air, land, living beings and property.
  • The Water and Air Acts define pollution by its harmful effects.
  • "Forest" has its dictionary meaning (Godavarman); the 2023 amendment and its challenge must be noted.
  • Arts. 48A and 51A(g) were added in 1976; Art. 21 includes a pollution-free environment (Subhash Kumar).
  • Pollution results from population, industry, urbanisation, agriculture, mining and weak enforcement.

Unit 2

Subject matter, types and functions of environmental laws

Environmental laws fall into three groups: protective laws that directly prevent harm, planning laws that decide how resources are used and shared, and administrative laws that create the machinery.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Subject-matter of environmental laws
  • Primary protective laws for human beings (water, air, noise, nuclear radiation, toxic substances) and for non-human beings (wild life, marine life, forests, minor forest produce, restrictions on trade)
  • Primary planning laws for production (land use, irrigation, industries, mining, grazing land, catchment areas, wetlands, estuaries) and distribution (land ceiling, town planning, slums, housing, recreational areas, parks, sanctuaries, biospheres)

Subject-matter

Environmental law regulates human interaction with the natural world: control of pollution, conservation of natural resources, protection of living species and their habitats, land-use planning, management of hazardous substances, and fair distribution of environmental benefits and burdens.

3.2 Primary planning laws

For production

  • Land use: State town and country planning Acts; land revenue codes.
  • Irrigation: State irrigation Acts; River Boards Act, 1956; Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956.
  • Industries: Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951; Environmental Impact Assessment Notification, 2006 (prior environmental clearance for listed projects).
  • Mining: Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957; mining plans and mine closure.
  • Grazing land, catchment areas: State laws protecting common lands; watershed programmes.
  • Wetlands: Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules, 2017; Ramsar Convention, 1971.
  • Estuaries: Coastal Regulation Zone Notification, 2019.

For distribution

  • Land ceiling: agricultural land ceiling laws; Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act, 1976 (repealed in 1999 at the Centre).
  • Town planning and zoning, slums (slum clearance and improvement Acts), housing schemes.
  • Recreational areas and parks: municipal and development authority laws; the public trust doctrine prevents diversion of parks.
  • Sanctuaries, national parks, conservation and community reserves: Chapter IV of the Wild Life Act.
  • Biosphere reserves: designated under UNESCO's Man and Biosphere programme; administered through executive schemes.
CaseM.I. Builders Pvt Ltd v Radhey Shyam Sahu (1999)

Permission to build an underground shopping complex beneath a historic park in Lucknow was held illegal; the park was a public amenity held in trust, and the Court ordered demolition.

3.3 Functions of environmental laws

  • Prevention: standards, consents and prohibitions.
  • Control and abatement: monitoring, inspection, closure orders.
  • Conservation: protected areas, forest conservation.
  • Remedy and compensation: liability rules, relief funds.
  • Participation and information: public hearings in EIA, access to information.
Exam tip

The syllabus classification (protective, planning, secondary and tertiary laws) follows Chhatrapati Singh's scheme. Answer with a table under each heading and one statute per item.

Points to remember

  • Protective laws for humans: Water, Air, Noise Rules, nuclear liability (now SHANTI Act), toxic substances.
  • Protective laws for non-humans: Wild Life Act (amended 2022), CRZ, forest laws, Forest Rights Act, Biological Diversity Act.
  • Planning laws govern production (land use, industries, mining, wetlands) and distribution (ceiling, housing, parks, sanctuaries).
  • Parks and public amenities are held in public trust (M.I. Builders).

Unit 3

Secondary laws: administration and institutions

Secondary laws create and empower the agencies that make the primary laws work: the pollution control boards, the Ministry, and the courts.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Administration and functioning of Pollution Control Boards, Water Boards, Factories Act, Forest Act, land reform and industries Acts
  • Administration within the Ministry of Environment and Forests
  • Collection, dissemination and publication of data on hazardous materials, endangering industries, pollution levels and safety measures
  • Role of lower courts including forest courts

4.1 Pollution Control Boards

  • Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) and State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs) were constituted under the Water Act (ss.3, 4) and have the same functions under the Air Act.
  • CPCB functions (s.16, Water Act): advise the Central Government, coordinate SPCBs, lay down standards for streams and wells, plan nationwide programmes, collect and publish data, and carry out research.
  • SPCB functions (s.17): plan programmes for prevention of pollution in the State, inspect sewage and effluent treatment, lay down effluent standards, and grant consent.
  • Consent mechanism: no person shall establish or operate an industry or process likely to discharge sewage or trade effluent into a stream, well, sewer or on land without the previous consent of the SPCB (s.25, Water Act; s.21, Air Act). Consent to establish and consent to operate.
  • Powers: entry and inspection (s.23), taking samples (s.21), directions including closure and stoppage of electricity or water supply (s.33A, Water Act; s.31A, Air Act), and applications to courts to restrain pollution (s.33).
Current law

The Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023 and the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Amendment Act, 2024 decriminalised many offences under the EPA, Air Act and Water Act, replacing imprisonment for several contraventions with monetary penalties imposed by adjudicating officers, and allowed the Central Government to exempt certain categories of industries from the consent requirement.

Other administrative laws

  • Factories Act, 1948 (now subsumed in the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020, in force from 21 November 2025): hazardous processes, site appraisal committees, disclosure of information to workers and the public, and emergency plans (introduced after Bhopal).
  • Forest Act: forest officers, forest settlement officers, and powers to seize and confiscate.
  • Land reform and development Acts, industries Acts: licensing and location policies.

4.2 Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC)

  • The nodal agency for environmental policy, created as a Department in 1980 and a Ministry in 1985.
  • Exercises power under s.3 of the EPA to take all measures it deems necessary to protect the environment: standards for emissions and discharges, restriction of areas where industries may operate, procedures and safeguards for handling hazardous substances, and appointment of authorities under s.3(3) (for example, the Central Ground Water Authority, the Commission for Air Quality Management).
  • s.5 EPA: power to give directions, including closure, prohibition or regulation of any industry, and stoppage of supply of electricity or water.
  • Issues environmental clearances under the EIA Notification, 2006 through expert appraisal committees.
CaseAlembic Pharmaceuticals Ltd v Rohit Prajapati (2020)

The Supreme Court held that the concept of ex post facto environmental clearance is alien to environmental law; prior clearance is mandatory, though on the facts it imposed compensation instead of closure on industries already operating. (See the 2025–26 Vanashakti rulings at the end of this unit.)

4.3 Collection, dissemination and publication of data

  • The boards must collect and disseminate information relating to pollution (s.16(2)(i), s.17(1)(c), Water Act).
  • The Manufacture, Storage and Import of Hazardous Chemicals Rules, 1989 require information on major accident hazards and on-site and off-site emergency plans.
  • Hazardous industries must disclose information to workers and the public (provisions inserted in the Factories Act after Bhopal, carried into the OSH Code).
  • The Right to Information Act, 2005 and the public hearing in the EIA process give citizens access to data.
CaseResearch Foundation for Science, Technology and Natural Resource Policy v Union of India (2005)

In a case on import of hazardous waste, the Court held that the right to information and community participation for protection of the environment and human health is part of Art. 21.

4.4 Role of lower courts, including forest courts

  • Magistrates try offences under the Water, Air and Environment Acts (on complaints by the boards or by any person after 60 days' notice: s.19, EPA; s.49, Water Act) and forest offences under the Indian Forest Act.
  • Public nuisance: magistrates can order removal of nuisances under s.152 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (earlier s.133 CrPC) (Municipal Council, Ratlam v Vardhichand, 1980).
  • Forest courts and special courts: some States have special forest courts or authorised officers for confiscation of forest produce and vehicles; appeals lie to designated authorities and courts.
  • Civil courts' jurisdiction over matters within the National Green Tribunal's jurisdiction is barred (s.29, NGT Act).
Exam tip

Learn the consent scheme (ss.25 and 26 Water Act; s.21 Air Act), the closure power (s.33A, s.31A) and s.3 and s.5 of the EPA. Add the 2023 and 2024 decriminalisation changes to show you are current.

Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseVanashakti v Union of India (2025)

In May 2025, a two-judge bench struck down the 2017 notification and a 2021 office memorandum that allowed ex post facto environmental clearance.

CaseCREDAI v Vanashakti (2025)

In November 2025, a three-judge bench recalled the May 2025 judgment by a 2:1 majority, holding that ex post facto clearance may be granted in exceptional cases on payment of penalties.

CaseVanashakti v Union of India (2026)

In July 2026, a bench led by the Chief Justice reaffirmed that prior environmental clearance is the rule and projects without it cannot generally be regularised. The 2017 notification survives only as a narrow, one-time window.

Current law

Current law. Prior environmental clearance remains mandatory. Ex post facto clearance is an exception, allowed only narrowly and with penalties. Cite all three Vanashakti stages in an answer.

Points to remember

  • Ex post facto EC: struck down (May 2025), recalled 2:1 (CREDAI, Nov 2025), allowed only narrowly (Vanashakti, July 2026).
  • CPCB and SPCBs set standards, grant consent, monitor and direct closure.
  • MoEFCC acts under ss.3 and 5 of the EPA; prior environmental clearance is mandatory (Alembic).
  • 2023 and 2024 amendments replaced many prison terms with penalties.
  • Information and participation are part of Art. 21 (Research Foundation).
  • Magistrates and forest courts enforce environmental offences; NGT bars civil courts.

In the Cauvery water dispute, the Supreme Court held that inter-State rivers are national assets and no State can claim exclusive ownership; it modified the tribunal's allocation and directed a scheme for implementation.

5.1 (contd.) Property, Directive Principles and resources

  • Arts. 31A, 31B and 31C protected agrarian reform and resource redistribution laws from challenge under Arts. 14 and 19 (see Constitutional Law I).
  • Art. 39(b) and (c): the ownership and control of the material resources of the community should be distributed to subserve the common good; operation of the economic system should not result in concentration of wealth.
CaseProperty Owners Association v State of Maharashtra (2024)

A nine-judge bench held (by majority) that not every private resource is a "material resource of the community" under Art. 39(b); natural resources such as forests, ponds, minerals and spectrum, held in public trust, clearly are, but other private property qualifies only on a case-specific inquiry.

5.2 Rights-based constitutional provisions

  • Art. 14: allocation of natural resources must be fair and non-arbitrary. In Centre for Public Interest Litigation v Union of India (2012) (2G spectrum), the Court said natural resources are vested in the State as trustee and must be distributed fairly; auction is generally preferable. In Natural Resources Allocation, In re (2012), the Court advised that auction is not a constitutional mandate for all resources.
  • Art. 19(1)(g): trade must yield to environmental protection; reasonable restrictions under Art. 19(6).
  • Art. 21: right to a healthy environment, clean water and air.
  • Art. 32: environmental PIL.
  • Arts. 38, 39, 42, 47: social order, health, humane conditions of work, public health.
  • Arts. 48A, 49, 51A(g): protection of environment, monuments and duty of citizens.
  • Art. 51(c): respect for international law and treaty obligations, the basis for domestic application of international environmental principles.
CaseM.K. Ranjitsinh v Union of India (2024)

In a case on protection of the Great Indian Bustard from overhead power lines, the Supreme Court recognised that people have a right against the adverse effects of climate change, flowing from Arts. 14 and 21, and modified its earlier blanket direction to underground transmission lines, balancing renewable energy needs.

Exam tip

Remember Art. 253 (treaty implementation) as the constitutional basis for the Air Act and EPA, and Art. 252 (State consent) as the basis for the Water Act.

Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseT.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v Union of India (2025)

Land recorded as forest in government records is forest land, whatever its ownership.

CaseState of Karnataka v Gandhi Jeevan Collective Farming Society (2026)

Forest land cannot be diverted to agriculture; about 134 acres were ordered to be restored as forest.

CaseNaveen Solanki v Rail Land Development Authority (2026)

Land earmarked for development in a Master Plan does not become 'deemed forest' merely because trees have grown on it.

Points to remember

  • Recorded forest land is forest (Godavarman, 2025); no diversion to agriculture (Gandhi Jeevan, 2026).
  • Natural resources are divided across the three lists; forests and wild life moved to the Concurrent List in 1976.
  • Water Act under Art. 252; Air Act and EPA under Art. 253.
  • Inter-State rivers are national assets (Cauvery, 2018).
  • Natural resources are held in trust and must be allocated fairly (2G case).
  • A right against adverse effects of climate change was recognised in 2024 (M.K. Ranjitsinh).

Unit 5

International parameters of environment

International environmental law began at Stockholm in 1972 and matured at Rio in 1992. India's own laws and courts have absorbed its key principles.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Stockholm Declaration and its impact
  • Rio Summit
  • UNEP
  • State responsibility for environmental pollution
  • North-South perspective

6.1 Stockholm Declaration (1972)

  • The UN Conference on the Human Environment at Stockholm (June 1972) adopted a Declaration of 26 principles and an Action Plan.
  • Principle 1: man has the fundamental right to freedom, equality and adequate conditions of life, in an environment of a quality that permits a life of dignity, and bears a solemn responsibility to protect and improve the environment for present and future generations.
  • Principle 21: States have the sovereign right to exploit their own resources, and the responsibility to ensure that activities within their jurisdiction do not cause damage to the environment of other States.
  • Impact on India: Indira Gandhi addressed the conference ("poverty is the greatest polluter"); India enacted the Water Act (1974), the 42nd Amendment (Arts. 48A, 51A(g)), the Air Act (1981) and the EPA (1986), the last two under Art. 253.

6.2 Rio Summit (1992)

The UN Conference on Environment and Development (the Earth Summit) produced:

  • the Rio Declaration (27 principles), including Principle 3 (intergenerational equity), Principle 4 (environment integral to development), Principle 7 (common but differentiated responsibilities), Principle 10 (public participation and access to information), Principle 15 (precautionary approach), Principle 16 (polluter pays) and Principle 17 (environmental impact assessment);
  • Agenda 21, a programme of action for sustainable development;
  • the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (later the Kyoto Protocol, 1997, and the Paris Agreement, 2015);
  • the Convention on Biological Diversity (implemented in India by the Biological Diversity Act, 2002);
  • the Forest Principles.

6.3 United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP)

Established in 1972 after Stockholm, headquartered in Nairobi. It coordinates UN environmental activities, monitors the global environment, promotes development of international environmental law (for example, the Montreal Protocol on ozone-depleting substances, 1987; the Basel Convention on hazardous wastes, 1989), and assists developing countries. The UN Environment Assembly (since 2012) is its governing body with universal membership.

6.4 State responsibility for environmental pollution

CaseTrail Smelter Arbitration (United States v Canada) (1938, 1941)

Fumes from a smelter at Trail in British Columbia damaged property in Washington State. The tribunal held that no State has the right to use or permit the use of its territory in such a manner as to cause injury by fumes in or to the territory of another, when the case is of serious consequence and established by clear and convincing evidence. Canada was held liable to pay compensation.

  • Corfu Channel case (1949): every State's obligation not knowingly to allow its territory to be used for acts contrary to the rights of other States.
  • Pulp Mills on the River Uruguay (Argentina v Uruguay) (2010): the ICJ held that environmental impact assessment for activities with transboundary risk is a requirement under general international law.
  • Advisory Opinion on Climate Change (ICJ, 2025): the ICJ advised that States have obligations under international law to protect the climate system, including due diligence to prevent significant harm, and that breach may entail State responsibility.

6.5 North-South perspective

  • Developed countries (North) caused most historical emissions and pollution; developing countries (South) need room for development and poverty eradication.
  • Common but differentiated responsibilities (Rio Principle 7; UNFCCC): all States share responsibility, but developed countries must take the lead and provide finance and technology.
  • Disputes over climate finance, technology transfer, trade-related environmental measures (such as carbon border taxes) and the right to development continue in negotiations.
  • India's position: per capita emissions and historical responsibility should guide burden sharing; it has committed to net-zero emissions by 2070.
Exam tip

Link each Rio principle to an Indian case: precautionary and polluter pays (Vellore Citizens), public participation (EIA public hearings), intergenerational equity (Godavarman, Kamal Nath).

Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseObligations of States in respect of Climate Change (ICJ Advisory Opinion, 2025)

On 23 July 2025, the International Court of Justice unanimously advised that States have binding obligations under treaties and customary international law to prevent significant harm to the climate system and to cooperate in good faith. Breach can be an internationally wrongful act.

Points to remember

  • ICJ climate advisory opinion (2025): binding duty to prevent significant climate harm.
  • Stockholm (1972): Principles 1 and 21; led to India's Water, Air and Environment Acts.
  • Rio (1992): Rio Declaration, Agenda 21, UNFCCC, CBD; precautionary, polluter pays, CBDR.
  • UNEP coordinates environmental activities.
  • State responsibility: Trail Smelter; ICJ climate advisory opinion (2025).
  • North-South: common but differentiated responsibilities.

Unit 6

Legal strategies of regulation

The law uses many tools: punishment, civil liability, standards, economic incentives and specialised tribunals. The National Green Tribunal now sits at the centre.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Deterrence through criminal, strict, absolute and vicarious liability
  • Principles of calculating penalties and economic sanctions
  • Standardisation
  • Managerial strategies: tortious liability, estoppel, incentives through non-taxation and deductions, recovery through insurance, sureties and bonds
  • Environmental courts and tribunals

7.1 Deterrence through liability

  • Criminal liability: offences under the Water Act (ss.41 to 45A), Air Act (ss.37 to 39), EPA (s.15), Wild Life Act (s.51) and Forest Acts. After the 2023 and 2024 amendments, many EPA, Air and Water Act contraventions carry monetary penalties imposed by adjudicating officers rather than imprisonment; wildlife and forest offences remain criminal.
  • Strict liability: Rylands v Fletcher (1868).
  • Absolute liability: M.C. Mehta v Union of India (1987, Oleum gas); Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v Union of India (1996).
  • Vicarious liability: offences by companies (s.16 EPA; s.47 Water Act; s.40 Air Act), making every person in charge of the business liable unless he proves lack of knowledge or due diligence; offences by government departments (s.17 EPA).

7.2 Penalties and economic sanctions

  • Polluter pays principle: the polluter must pay the cost of remedying the damage and compensating victims, not merely of preventing pollution.
CaseVellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v Union of India (1996)

Tanneries in Tamil Nadu discharged untreated effluent into the Palar river. The Court held that the precautionary principle and the polluter pays principle are part of the environmental law of the country, as part of sustainable development; it directed the polluting tanneries to pay compensation and set up an authority under s.3(3) EPA.

  • Penalties should be deterrent: compensation correlated to the magnitude and capacity of the enterprise (M.C. Mehta, 1987); exemplary damages (M.C. Mehta v Kamal Nath, 2000); environmental compensation calculated by the CPCB and NGT based on the cost of restoration and the scale of the polluter.
  • Economic instruments: pollution cess and fees, compensatory afforestation funds (Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act, 2016), carbon credit trading scheme (Energy Conservation Act amendments, 2022), extended producer responsibility for plastics, e-waste and batteries.

7.3 Standardisation

  • Standards for ambient air quality (National Ambient Air Quality Standards, 2009), effluent and emission standards (Schedules to the Environment (Protection) Rules, 1986), noise standards (Noise Rules, 2000), fuel and vehicle standards (Bharat Stage norms), product standards (BIS).
  • Methods: technology-based standards (best available technology), ambient quality-based standards, and performance standards; monitoring through continuous emission monitoring systems.

7.4 Managerial strategies

  • Tortious liability: nuisance, negligence, trespass; class actions.
  • Estoppel: the State cannot resile from conditions it imposed in clearances; promissory estoppel cannot be pleaded to defeat environmental protection, since there is no estoppel against public interest or statute.
  • Incentives: tax deductions and accelerated depreciation for pollution control equipment and renewable energy; exemptions for clean technology.
  • Insurance, sureties and bonds: compulsory insurance for hazardous substances (Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991, with an Environment Relief Fund); bank guarantees required by pollution control boards as a condition of consent; mine closure and reclamation bonds.

7.5 Environmental courts and tribunals

  • The National Environment Tribunal Act, 1995 and National Environment Appellate Authority Act, 1997 were not effectively implemented.
  • The Supreme Court in A.P. Pollution Control Board v M.V. Nayudu (1999) recommended environmental courts with expert members.
  • The National Green Tribunal Act, 2010 established the NGT.

Unit 7

Rights in common law and natural rights theories

Environmental justice asks whose rights count: forest communities with customary rights, people whose livelihood depends on nature, future generations, and nature itself.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Basis of occupancy, usufruct and group or collective rights of indigenous communities in national and international law
  • Natural rights theories in environmental issues: right to life, livelihood, to reside, to development, rights of future generations
  • Bio-ethics, organisational and social ethics, environmental ethics

Unit 8: Rights of indigenous communities

Common law basis

  • Occupancy: rights based on long and continuous possession of land.
  • Usufruct: the right to use and enjoy the produce of land or resources belonging to another without damaging the substance (grazing, collection of firewood and minor forest produce, fishing).
  • Collective or group rights: rights held by communities as a whole over commons (village forests, pastures, water bodies), often recognised by custom.

National law

  • Fifth and Sixth Schedules of the Constitution: protection of Scheduled Areas and tribal autonomy.
  • Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA): gram sabhas empowered over community resources, minor forest produce and land alienation.
  • Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006: recognises individual and community forest rights (s.3), including rights over minor forest produce, grazing, habitat rights of particularly vulnerable tribal groups, and the right to protect and manage community forest resources; the gram sabha initiates the process (s.6).
CaseSamatha v State of Andhra Pradesh (1997)

Leasing tribal land in Scheduled Areas to non-tribal private mining companies was held void, as it violated the Fifth Schedule and the AP Scheduled Areas Land Transfer Regulation; tribals have a right to land and to share in mineral wealth.

CaseOrissa Mining Corporation v Ministry of Environment and Forests (2013)

In the Niyamgiri bauxite mining case, the Court held that the gram sabhas of the affected Dongria Kondh villages must decide whether the mining would affect their religious, cultural and community rights under the Forest Rights Act. The gram sabhas rejected the project.

International law

  • ILO Convention No. 169 (1989) on Indigenous and Tribal Peoples (India has not ratified it; it ratified the earlier Convention No. 107).
  • UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007): rights to lands, territories and resources, and free, prior and informed consent.
  • Convention on Biological Diversity, Art. 8(j): respect for traditional knowledge; the Nagoya Protocol (2010) on access and benefit-sharing.

9.6 Ethics

  • Environmental ethics: anthropocentrism (nature valued for human benefit) versus ecocentrism (nature has intrinsic value). Indian courts have moved towards an ecocentric approach.
CaseCentre for Environmental Law, WWF-India v Union of India (2013)

(Later, in Animal Welfare Board of India v Union of India (2023), a Constitution Bench upheld State amendments permitting jallikattu with safeguards.)

  • Gram sabha consent is decisive (Niyamgiri); tribal land cannot be leased to private miners (Samatha).
  • Rights to life, livelihood, residence, development and of future generations shape environmental law.

Unit 8

Multinational corporate liability and judicial activism

Big polluters and big lenders often escape national law. Indian courts responded with absolute liability, continuing mandamus and new principles, making the judiciary the most active environmental regulator.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Multinational corporate liabilities
  • Legal responsibilities of multinational funding agencies
  • Judicial activism and the environment

Unit 10: Multinational corporations

  • Problems: limited liability and corporate veil, parent-subsidiary separation, forum shopping, weak host-State regulation, difficulty of enforcing judgments abroad.
  • Bhopal: Union Carbide Corporation (US parent) and Union Carbide India Ltd. The Union of India first sued in New York; the US court dismissed the case on forum non conveniens (In re Union Carbide Corporation Gas Plant Disaster at Bhopal, 1986), sending the case to India. The 1989 settlement of US $470 million was upheld in Union Carbide Corporation v Union of India (1991, review) and the curative petition seeking more was dismissed in 2023.
  • Principles: absolute liability of enterprises engaged in hazardous activity (M.C. Mehta, 1987), with compensation correlated to the size of the enterprise; possibility of lifting the corporate veil; enterprise liability.
  • Soft law: the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises; the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (2011): "Protect, Respect and Remedy"; India's National Guidelines on Responsible Business Conduct (2019).
CaseVedanta Ltd v State of Tamil Nadu (2020)

The Madras High Court upheld the permanent closure of the Sterlite copper smelter at Thoothukudi for persistent pollution, and the Supreme Court in 2024 refused to interfere, holding that the health of residents and the environment prevail.

Multinational funding agencies

  • The World Bank, the Asian Development Bank and others finance large projects (dams, mines, power plants). Their safeguard policies require environmental and social impact assessment, resettlement and consultation.
  • The World Bank Inspection Panel (1993) lets affected people complain of non-compliance. The Bank withdrew from the Sardar Sarovar project in 1993 after the Morse Committee report.
  • Funding agencies have generally claimed immunity from national courts; in Jam v International Finance Corporation (2019), the US Supreme Court held that the IFC does not enjoy absolute immunity and can be sued for commercial activities (here, a power plant in Gujarat that harmed fishing communities).

Unit 11: Judicial activism and the environment

Indian courts have:

  1. Expanded rights: Art. 21 includes a clean environment (Subhash Kumar), right to information on environment (Research Foundation), and right against climate change effects (M.K. Ranjitsinh).
  2. Created principles: absolute liability (M.C. Mehta, 1987), polluter pays and precautionary principles (Vellore), public trust (Kamal Nath), intergenerational equity (Godavarman), burden on the developer (Nayudu), sustainable development.
  3. Used new procedures: epistolary jurisdiction, expert committees, continuing mandamus (Godavarman since 1995; M.C. Mehta vehicular pollution cases), amicus curiae, the Central Empowered Committee for forests.
  4. Issued policy-type directions: CNG for Delhi buses (M.C. Mehta v Union of India, 1998 to 2002), closure of polluting industries around the Taj (M.C. Mehta v Union of India, Taj Trapezium, 1997), relocation of industries out of Delhi, Ganga cleaning (M.C. Mehta, tanneries, 1987), firecracker restrictions, stubble burning.
  5. Built institutions: environmental authorities under s.3(3) EPA; recommended the NGT.

Criticism

  • Courts take over executive functions and policy-making without expertise or accountability.
  • Directions are difficult to implement and monitor; continuing mandamus cases run for decades.
  • Livelihoods of workers are sometimes affected without adequate rehabilitation.
  • Defence: courts step in where the executive fails to enforce the law; access to justice for the voiceless.
Exam tip

Close an essay on judicial activism with a balanced paragraph: the courts filled a vacuum left by weak enforcement, but the long-term solution is strong regulators and the NGT, with courts acting as guardians of principle.

Points to remember

  • MNC liability: Bhopal shows limits of corporate structure and forum; absolute liability and enterprise liability respond.
  • Soft law: OECD Guidelines and UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights.
  • Funding agencies: safeguard policies, Inspection Panel; IFC not absolutely immune (Jam, 2019).
  • Judicial activism created rights, principles, procedures and institutions; criticised for overreach.

Answer any FIVE of the following.

1.Polluter pays principle
Model answer

The person who causes pollution must bear the cost of remedying it. This covers not only compensation to victims, but also restoring the environment.

  • Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v Union of India (1996): the Bichhri case, involving H-acid sludge.
  • Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v Union of India (1996): the principle is part of the law of the land.
  • It is applied by the NGT under s.20 of the NGT Act, 2010, including turnover-based environmental compensation where the method is rational (Rhythm County, 2026).
2.Precautionary principle
Model answer

Where there are threats of serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty should not be used as a reason to postpone measures to prevent environmental degradation (Rio Principle 15).

In India (Vellore Citizens, 1996; A.P. Pollution Control Board v M.V. Nayudu, 1999):

  • the State must anticipate, prevent and attack the causes of degradation; and
  • the onus of proof is on the developer to show that the activity is environmentally benign.
3.Public trust doctrine
Model answer

Natural resources such as air, sea, waters, forests and shores are held by the State as trustee for the public. They cannot be converted to private ownership or used inconsistently with public use.

  • M.C. Mehta v Kamal Nath (1997): a lease of protected forest land on a river bank to a motel was quashed.
  • Fomento Resorts v Minguel Martins (2009).
  • Centre for PIL v Union of India (2012, 2G).

The doctrine also underlies Art. 21 and Arts. 48A and 51A(g).

4.Sustainable development
Model answer

Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (Brundtland Report, 1987). Its components are:

  • intergenerational equity;
  • the precautionary principle;
  • polluter pays;
  • use and conservation of natural resources; and
  • environmental impact assessment.

India adopted the concept in Vellore Citizens (1996). In Narmada Bachao Andolan (2000), the Court balanced development with rehabilitation.

5.Environmental Impact Assessment
Model answer

EIA is a process to identify and evaluate the likely environmental effects of a project before it is approved. It is governed by the EIA Notification, 2006 under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986. The stages are:

  1. screening (Category A or B);
  2. scoping (terms of reference);
  3. public consultation; and
  4. appraisal by the expert appraisal committee.

Then follows the decision on clearance.

  • Prior environmental clearance is mandatory (Common Cause v Union of India, 2017; Alembic, 2020).
  • On ex post facto clearance, see the Vanashakti rulings: May 2025, recalled in CREDAI (Nov 2025), and clarified in July 2026 as a narrow exception.
6.Jurisdiction of the National Green Tribunal
Model answer

Under the NGT Act, 2010:

  • Original jurisdiction (s.14): civil cases involving a substantial question relating to the environment under the seven Schedule I Acts (Water, Water Cess, Forest Conservation, Air, Environment Protection, Public Liability Insurance and Biological Diversity).
  • Relief and compensation (s.15): limitation of five years.
  • Appellate jurisdiction (s.16).
  • It applies sustainable development, precautionary and polluter pays principles (s.20), and no-fault liability (s.17(3)).
  • Appeals lie to the Supreme Court within 90 days (s.22).
  • Suo motu power was recognised in Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai v Ankita Sinha (2021).
7.Stockholm Conference, 1972
Model answer

The UN Conference on the Human Environment was the first global environmental conference. It produced:

  • the Stockholm Declaration (26 principles, including the right to an environment of quality, Principle 1, and State responsibility for transboundary harm, Principle 21);
  • an Action Plan; and
  • the creation of UNEP.

India's response included the 42nd Amendment (Arts. 48A and 51A(g)), the Water Act (1974) and the Air Act (1981, s.2 referring to Stockholm).

8.Articles 48A and 51A(g)
Model answer
  • Art. 48A (Directive Principle): the State shall endeavour to protect and improve the environment and safeguard forests and wildlife.
  • Art. 51A(g) (Fundamental Duty): every citizen shall protect and improve the natural environment, including forests, lakes, rivers and wildlife, and have compassion for living creatures.

Both were added by the 42nd Amendment (1976). The courts read them with Art. 21 to create a right to a healthy environment (Subhash Kumar, 1991; M.K. Ranjitsinh, 2024, on climate change).

9.Forest land under T.N. Godavarman
Model answer

T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v Union of India (1996 onwards) is a continuing mandamus. It held that "forest" in the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 must be understood in its dictionary sense, regardless of ownership. Land recorded as forest in government records is forest land (reaffirmed in 2025).

  • Forest land cannot be diverted to agriculture (Gandhi Jeevan Collective Farming Society, 2026).
  • Land merely covered by trees in a Master Plan development area is not deemed forest (Naveen Solanki, 2026).
  • The 1980 Act was renamed the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam in 2023.

11.Explain the mechanism for prevention and control of water and air pollution under the Water Act, 1974 and the Air Act, 1981.
Model answer

  • Boards: the Central Pollution Control Board (s.3) and State Pollution Control Boards (s.4).
  • Functions (ss.16 and 17):
  • advise government;
  • plan comprehensive programmes;
  • lay down standards;

  • sampling (s.21);
  • entry and inspection (s.23);
  • emergency measures (s.32);
  • court orders restraining pollution (s.33); and
  • directions, including closure and stoppage of electricity or water (s.33A).
  • Appeals (s.28).
  • Offences (ss.41 to 49). The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Amendment Act, 2024 decriminalised many minor offences, replacing imprisonment with monetary penalties imposed by adjudicating officers.

  • Boards act under the Air Act as well.
  • Air pollution control areas (s.19).
  • Emission standards for vehicles (s.20).

12.Discuss the evolution of international environmental law from Stockholm to the present.
Model answer

Stockholm 1972

  • The Declaration on the Human Environment and UNEP.
  • Principle 21: sovereignty over resources, and responsibility not to cause transboundary harm (drawn from Trail Smelter, 1941).

Brundtland Report (1987)

Introduced sustainable development.

Rio 1992 (Earth Summit)

  • The Rio Declaration (27 principles):
  • precaution (Principle 15);
  • polluter pays (16);
  • EIA (17);
  • common but differentiated responsibilities (7).
  • Agenda 21, the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change, the Convention on Biological Diversity and the Forest Principles.

Climate regime

  • Kyoto Protocol (1997): binding targets for developed countries.
  • Paris Agreement (2015):
  • nationally determined contributions;
  • the goal of well below 2°C and efforts towards 1.5°C;
  • "progression" of ambition.

Other conventions

  • Vienna Convention and Montreal Protocol (ozone, 1985 and 1987).
  • Basel Convention (hazardous waste, 1989).
  • Ramsar (wetlands), CITES and the Nagoya Protocol.
  • The BBNJ (high seas biodiversity) Agreement, 2023.

ICJ advisory opinion (23 July 2025)

The ICJ unanimously advised that States have binding obligations under treaties and customary law:

  • to prevent significant harm to the climate system;
  • to exercise due diligence; and
  • to cooperate in good faith.

Breach may engage State responsibility. The customary obligations apply to all States, even non-parties to the climate treaties.

India's implementation

  • Arts. 48A, 51A(g) and 253.
  • The Air Act (Stockholm), the Environment Protection Act and the Biological Diversity Act (CBD).
  • Courts adopting Rio principles.

Conclusion

International environmental law has moved from soft declarations to binding treaties and judicial affirmation of State duties. India absorbed these principles through legislation and judicial activism.

13.Explain the powers of the Central Government under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.
Model answer

Background

The Act was passed after the Bhopal gas tragedy (1984) under Art. 253, implementing the Stockholm decisions. It is an umbrella law.

Powers

  1. General power (s.3): to take all measures necessary to protect and improve the environment, including:
  • coordinating State action;
  • planning national programmes;
  • laying down standards of environmental quality and of emissions;
  • restricting industries in certain areas;
  • procedures for accidents; and
  • inspection and research.

It may constitute authorities (s.3(3)), such as the CGWA and the Central Empowered Committee.

  1. Directions (s.5): to any person, officer or authority, including the closure, prohibition or regulation of any industry, and stoppage of electricity or water.
  2. Rules (s.6): for example, the Hazardous Waste Rules, Coastal Regulation Zone Notification, EIA Notification 2006, Plastic Waste Rules, E-waste Rules and Noise Rules.
  3. Prohibitions:
  • emitting pollutants beyond standards (s.7);
  • handling hazardous substances without following procedure (s.8).
  1. Entry, inspection and sampling (ss.10 and 11).
  2. Penalties: the Jan Vishwas Act, 2023 replaced imprisonment for many contraventions with monetary penalties imposed by adjudicating officers, with appeals to the NGT.
  3. Citizen suits (s.19): a person may complain after giving 60 days' notice.
  4. Bar of civil court jurisdiction (s.22).

Notable instruments

  • EIA Notification 2006 (prior clearance).
  • CRZ Notification 2019.
  • Eco-sensitive zones around protected areas (Godavarman, 2022 and 2023).

Conclusion

The Act gives the Central Government wide regulatory and directive powers. Its effectiveness depends on enforcement and on judicial oversight.

Part C Problems2 × 10 = 20 marks

Answer any TWO of the following.

14.A cement company starts construction of a plant without prior environmental clearance under the EIA Notification, 2006. After a complaint, it applies for ex post facto clearance and offers to pay a penalty. Advise the authorities.
Model answer

Issue: Can ex post facto environmental clearance be granted?

Law:

  • Common Cause (2017) and Alembic Pharmaceuticals v Rohit Prajapati (2020): ex post facto clearance is alien to environmental law; prior clearance is mandatory.
  • Vanashakti v Union of India (May 2025) struck down the 2017 notification and the 2021 office memorandum permitting it.
  • CREDAI v Vanashakti (Nov 2025, 2:1) recalled that judgment, permitting such clearance in exceptional cases on penalty.
  • Vanashakti (July 2026) reaffirmed that prior clearance is the rule, and projects without it cannot generally be regularised. The 2017 window operates only narrowly and is time-bound.

Application: The company deliberately began construction without clearance. Unless its case falls within the narrow exceptional window, regularisation is not available, and the work must stop.

Conclusion: The authorities should stop construction and take penal and compensatory action. They should consider fresh appraisal only if the narrow exception applies, with heavy penalties and restoration conditions.

15.Tanneries on a river bank discharge untreated effluents, polluting drinking water and agricultural land downstream. Villagers file a public interest litigation. What principles will the court apply?
Model answer

Issue: The liability of polluting industries and the remedies available.

Law:

  • Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v Union of India (1996): the precautionary and polluter pays principles are part of Indian law. The Court ordered compensation, restoration, and the closure of units not installing treatment plants.
  • M.C. Mehta v Union of India (Ganga tanneries, 1987): closure of units without primary treatment.
  • Water Act ss.25 and 33A; Environment Protection Act s.5.

Application: Untreated discharge violates consent conditions and Art. 21. The industries must bear the cost of compensation and of restoring the environment.

Conclusion: The court will:

  • direct the industries to install effluent treatment plants within a fixed time, failing which they will be closed;
  • fix compensation for victims and for ecological restoration; and
  • direct monitoring by the Pollution Control Board.

The NGT may also award compensation under s.15 of the NGT Act.

16.The State government leases part of a protected forest on a river bank to a private company to build a resort. The company diverts the river's course to protect the resort. Is the lease valid?
Model answer

Issue: Can the State transfer natural resources held in public trust to a private party for commercial use?

Law:

  • M.C. Mehta v Kamal Nath (1997): the public trust doctrine. The State, as trustee of natural resources, cannot transfer ecologically fragile land for private commercial use. The lease was quashed, the company was directed to pay restoration costs, and exemplary damages were imposed later.
  • Forest (Conservation) Act s.2 (now the Van Adhiniyam): prior Central approval is needed for non-forest use or leasing of forest land.

Application: The lease of forest land for a resort, without Central approval, together with the diversion of the river, breaches the public trust and forest law.

Conclusion: The lease is invalid. The court will quash it, order restoration of the river and forest at the company's cost, and impose compensation under the polluter pays principle.

17.A landlord rents his premises to a dyeing unit. The unit discharges untreated effluent. The Pollution Control Board proceeds against both the landlord and the tenant. The landlord argues that he had no control over the unit. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Is a landlord liable for pollution caused by a tenant's industry?

Law:

  • Gujarat Pollution Control Board v Jagmohan (2026): a landlord is not accountable for a tenant's violations merely because he owns the premises.
  • Liability attaches to the occupier who carries on the activity and controls the discharge.
  • The polluter pays principle targets the polluter.

Application: The tenant operated the unit and controlled the effluent. The landlord's ownership alone does not make him the polluter, unless he participated in, permitted or profited from the polluting activity knowingly.

Conclusion: Proceedings against the landlord should be dropped, unless there is evidence of his participation. The tenant is liable for penalties, closure and compensation.
