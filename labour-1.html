Unit 1

Historical perspectives of labour

Labour law emerged to correct the inequality between employer and worker. It moved the employment relation from free contract to statutory protection.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Labour through the ages: slave labour, guild system, caste-based division, feudal labour
  • Colonial labour law and policy
  • Labour-capital conflicts
  • International labour standards
  • From laissez faire to welfare State: exploitation to protection, contract to statute

1.1 Labour through the ages

  • Slave labour: workers owned as property (ancient Greece and Rome; Indian dasa); no rights.
  • Guild system: medieval associations of craftsmen and merchants (in India, shrenis) regulating training, prices and quality.
  • Caste-based division of labour: occupations tied to birth, with forced and unpaid labour (begar) imposed on lower castes.
  • Feudal labour: serfs and tenants bound to land; bonded labour persisted into modern India.

1.2 Colonial labour law and policy

  • Early laws protected employers: the Workmen's Breach of Contract Act, 1859 made breach of contract by workers a criminal offence; indentured labour was exported to plantations abroad; the Assam Labour and Emigration Act regulated tea garden labour on harsh terms.
  • Gradual protective laws: Factories Act, 1881 (child labour); Workmen's Compensation Act, 1923; Trade Unions Act, 1926 (after Buckingham Mill case, 1920, where union leaders were restrained by injunction); Trade Disputes Act, 1929; Royal Commission on Labour (Whitley Commission, 1929 to 1931).
  • Post-independence: Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946, Minimum Wages Act, 1948, Factories Act, 1948, ESI Act, 1948, EPF Act, 1952.

1.3 Labour-capital conflicts

Exploitation, profit motive, poor bargaining power of workers, poor working conditions, unorganised and surplus labour, extreme division of labour and lack of alternative employment produce conflict. Labour law aims to balance this through collective bargaining, minimum standards, social security and dispute resolution.

1.4 International labour standards

  • The International Labour Organisation (1919), with tripartite structure (governments, employers, workers), adopts conventions and recommendations. India is a founding member.
  • Core (fundamental) conventions: freedom of association (No. 87) and collective bargaining (No. 98) (India has not ratified these two); forced labour (Nos. 29, 105); equal remuneration (No. 100); discrimination (No. 111); minimum age (No. 138); worst forms of child labour (No. 182); occupational safety and health (Nos. 155, 187, added in 2022).
  • Implementation in India: through legislation, the Constitution (Arts. 23, 24, 39, 41, 42, 43, 43A) and judicial decisions.

1.5 From laissez faire to welfare State

  • Under laissez faire, employment was a private contract; the State did not interfere ("hire and fire").
  • The welfare State intervenes: minimum wages, hours, safety, social security, collective bargaining, protection against arbitrary dismissal. Status (protected by statute) replaces contract.
  • Second National Commission on Labour (2002) recommended consolidation of central labour laws into four or five codes. This led to the Code on Wages, 2019, the Industrial Relations Code, 2020, the Code on Social Security, 2020, and the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020, all in force from 21 November 2025, consolidating 29 central laws.

Points to remember

  • Labour evolved from slavery, guilds, caste and feudal service to protected employment.
  • Colonial law favoured employers; protective laws grew after 1920.
  • ILO standards guide Indian law; India has not ratified Conventions 87 and 98.
  • The welfare State moved labour from contract to statute.
  • Four Labour Codes replaced 29 central laws from 21 November 2025.

Unit 2

Trade unionism and legal control of trade unions

Trade unions give workers collective strength. The law registers and protects them, gives them immunities for trade disputes, and now provides for recognition of negotiating unions.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Labour movement as a counter to exploitation
  • History of trade union movement in India
  • Right to form trade unions as part of freedom of association
  • Registration, amalgamation, rights and liabilities, dissolution
  • Problems: multiplicity, politicisation, intra- and inter-union rivalry, outside leadership, closed shop and union shop, recognition
  • Trade Unions Act, 1926 and reforms

2.1 and 2.2 Labour movement and its history in India

  • Early welfare associations (Bombay Mill Hands Association, 1890, led by N.M. Lokhande).
  • Madras Labour Union (1918), led by B.P. Wadia, the first organised trade union.
  • All India Trade Union Congress (1920).
  • After independence, unions became linked to political parties (INTUC, AITUC, HMS, CITU, BMS), leading to multiplicity and rivalry.

Right to form unions

Art. 19(1)(c) guarantees the right to form associations or unions, subject to reasonable restrictions (Art. 19(4)).

CaseAll India Bank Employees' Association v National Industrial Tribunal (1962)

The right to form unions does not carry with it a fundamental right to strike, to bargain collectively, or to achieve every object of the union.

CaseB.R. Singh v Union of India (1989)

The right to strike is not a fundamental right, but it is a legal right recognised by labour statutes, subject to restrictions; it is an important weapon of collective bargaining.

2.3 Registration, rights, liabilities, amalgamation and dissolution

Under the Trade Unions Act, 1926

  • Any seven or more members could apply for registration (s.4), subject to at least 10% or 100 workers (whichever less) in the establishment (2001 amendment).
  • A registered union is a body corporate (s.13), with a general fund (for listed objects) and a separate political fund (voluntary contributions).
  • Immunities: from criminal conspiracy for agreements to further trade union objects (s.17); from civil suits for acts in furtherance of a trade dispute (s.18); enforceability of agreements in restraint of trade (s.19).
  • Amalgamation (s.24) and dissolution (s.27).

Under the Industrial Relations Code, 2020 (Chapter III)

  • Registration on application by at least 10% of workers or 100 workers, whichever is less, with a minimum of seven members; a registered union is a body corporate.
  • Membership ordinarily limited to persons engaged in the industry, with a limit on outsiders as office-bearers (in the organised sector, not more than one-third or five, whichever less, can be outsiders; the unorganised sector allows a higher proportion).
  • Immunities from criminal conspiracy and from civil suits for acts in contemplation or furtherance of an industrial dispute continue.
  • Central and State trade unions may be recognised by the appropriate government.
  • Disputes concerning registered unions (internal disputes, between union and members) are referred to the Industrial Tribunal.
  • Provisions for amalgamation, change of name, cancellation of registration and dissolution.

2.4 Problems of trade unionism

  • Multiplicity of unions in one establishment, weakening bargaining.
  • Over-politicisation: unions as wings of political parties.
  • Intra-union and inter-union rivalry.
  • Outside leadership: politicians and professional leaders, not workers, lead unions.
  • Closed shop (only union members may be employed) and union shop (employees must join within a period): not recognised in Indian law, as they conflict with the freedom not to associate (Damyanti Naranga, 1971).
  • Recognition: the 1926 Act had no provision for compulsory recognition; some State laws (Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh) and the Code of Discipline (1958) provided for it.

Recognition under the IR Code: negotiating union and council

  • Where only one union exists, it is recognised as the sole negotiating union.
  • Where more than one union exists, the union with the support of 51% or more of the workers on the muster roll is recognised as the sole negotiating union.
  • If no union has 51%, a negotiating council is formed of unions each having at least 20% of the workers, with one representative for each 20%.
  • The negotiating union or council negotiates on matters such as standing orders, wages and service conditions.
Exam tip

Compare the 1926 Act and the IR Code in a table: registration thresholds, outsiders, immunities, and the new recognition provisions (51% and 20%).

Points to remember

  • Trade unions began with the Madras Labour Union (1918) and AITUC (1920).
  • Art. 19(1)(c) protects forming unions; no fundamental right to strike (All India Bank Employees; B.R. Singh).
  • Registration requires 10% or 100 workers and at least seven members; unions are bodies corporate with immunities.
  • Problems: multiplicity, politicisation, rivalry, outside leadership.
  • IR Code introduces sole negotiating union (51%) or negotiating council (20% each).

Unit 3

Collective bargaining

Collective bargaining replaces individual contracts with negotiated agreements between employers and organised workers, backed by the possibility of strikes and lockouts.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Concept
  • International norms
  • Conditions precedent
  • Merits and demerits
  • Bargaining process: negotiation
  • pressurisation: strike, lockout, go-slow, work to rule, gherao
  • Structure: plant, industry and national levels
  • Duration and enforcement of bipartite agreements
  • Reforms

3.1 Concept

Collective bargaining is the process of negotiation between employers and organised workers (through unions) to determine wages and conditions of employment, and the settlement of disputes through agreements. ILO Convention No. 98 encourages voluntary negotiation.

CaseKarnal Leather Karamchari Sangathan v Liberty Footwear Co (1989)

The Court described collective bargaining as a technique by which disputes about conditions of employment are resolved amicably by agreement rather than by coercion; the Industrial Disputes Act promotes it as the preferred method of industrial peace.

3.2 Conditions precedent, merits and demerits

  • Preconditions: strong, representative unions; recognition of unions by employers; good faith on both sides; willingness to compromise; a legal framework supporting agreements.
  • Merits: industrial democracy; settlements suited to the enterprise; flexibility; less litigation.
  • Demerits: unequal bargaining power where unions are weak; multiplicity of unions; strikes and lockouts disrupt production; excludes unorganised workers.

3.3 The bargaining process

Negotiation

Charter of demands, negotiation, settlement (bipartite), or failure leading to conciliation.

Pressurisation

FormMeaning and legal position
------
StrikeCessation of work by a body of persons acting in combination, or a concerted refusal to continue to work. Under the IR Code, it includes concerted casual leave on a given day by 50% or more workers
LockoutTemporary closing of a place of employment, suspension of work, or refusal by an employer to continue to employ workers
Go-slowDeliberate slowing of work while drawing full wages; regarded as misconduct, not a protected strike (Bharat Sugar Mills v Jai Singh, 1961)
Work to ruleWorkers follow rules strictly to reduce output; legal status depends on circumstances
GheraoPhysical confinement of management to press demands; illegal, as it involves wrongful confinement (Jay Engineering Works v State of West Bengal, Calcutta HC, 1968)

3.4 Structure of bargaining

  • Plant level: between a single employer and its union (most common in India).
  • Industry level: between employers' associations and federations of unions in an industry (textiles, banking: bipartite settlements between the Indian Banks' Association and bank unions).
  • National level: for public sector industries (steel, coal) through joint committees.

3.5 Duration and enforcement of bipartite agreements

  • Settlement (s.2(p), IDA): a settlement arrived at in conciliation, or by written agreement between employer and workmen otherwise than in conciliation (signed and copied to the government and conciliation officer).
  • Binding effect (s.18, IDA): a bipartite settlement binds only the parties to it; a settlement in conciliation binds all parties to the dispute, including all present and future workmen of the establishment.
  • Duration (s.19): as agreed, or six months if not specified, and continues until terminated by two months' notice.
  • Under the IR Code: similar rules on settlements and their binding effect; a settlement remains in force for the period agreed (or as the Code provides where none is agreed) and continues until terminated by notice.
CaseITC Ltd Workers' Welfare Association v Management of ITC Ltd (2002)

A settlement arrived at in the course of conciliation proceedings, with a recognised majority union, is binding on all workers, including members of a minority union that did not sign it, unless shown to be unfair or arrived at by fraud or mala fides.

3.6 Reforms

  • Statutory recognition of unions (now in the IR Code), compulsory bargaining in good faith, and promoting industry-wide bargaining.
  • Grievance redressal committees (IR Code, establishments with 20 or more workers) and works committees (100 or more) promote bipartite dialogue.

Points to remember

  • Collective bargaining is the preferred method of settling disputes (Karnal Leather).
  • Preconditions: strong, recognised unions and good faith.
  • Pressure tactics: strike and lockout are lawful within limits; go-slow and gherao are not protected.
  • Bargaining at plant, industry and national levels.
  • Settlements in conciliation bind all workmen; bipartite settlements bind parties (s.18 IDA; ITC Workers' Welfare Association).

Unit 4

State regulation of industrial relations

The State intervenes in industrial relations to secure social justice and industrial peace, through conciliation, arbitration and adjudication, and by laying down standards.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Theoretical foundations: social justice, welfare, public interest, productivity, industrial peace, price control
  • Methods: recognition of mutual arrangements, assistance to bipartite settlements (conciliation, voluntary arbitration, standing orders), State machinery (reference, adjudication, awards, judicial review), standards on lay-off, strike, lockout, retrenchment, closure and transfer
  • The conceptual conundrum: industry, industrial dispute, workman
  • Unfair labour practices

4.1 Theoretical foundations

Social justice (Preamble; Arts. 38, 39, 43), labour welfare, public interest in uninterrupted production, productivity, industrial peace, economic development, and control of prices. The judiciary has treated industrial adjudication as a means of social justice, not mere contract enforcement.

4.2 Methods of regulation

Recognition of mutual arrangements and assistance to bipartite settlements

  • Conciliation: conciliation officers and boards help parties reach a settlement (ss.4, 5, 12, 13 IDA; IR Code provides conciliation officers).
  • Voluntary arbitration (s.10A IDA; s.42 IR Code): parties may agree in writing to refer a dispute to an arbitrator; the award is binding.
  • Standing orders: certified conditions of service (Unit 6).

State prescription of machinery: adjudication

  • Reference (s.10 IDA): the appropriate government may refer a dispute to a Labour Court, Industrial Tribunal or National Tribunal. Reference was discretionary and administrative, criticised for political overtones.
  • IR Code: an aggrieved party may apply directly to the Industrial Tribunal if conciliation fails, after the conciliation officer's failure report or the lapse of the conciliation period, within the prescribed time; the government retains power to refer certain disputes to the National Industrial Tribunal.
  • How tribunals differ from courts: they can create new obligations, modify contracts in the interest of social justice, and are not bound by strict rules of evidence.
  • Award: binding on parties (s.18 IDA); enforceable; subject to judicial review under Arts. 226, 227 and 136.

State prescription of standards

Lay-off, strike, lockout, retrenchment, closure and transfer of undertakings (Unit 9).

4.3 The conceptual conundrum: industry, industrial dispute, workman

Industry

CaseBangalore Water Supply and Sewerage Board v A. Rajappa (1978)

A seven-judge bench (Krishna Iyer J) laid down the "triple test": where there is (1) systematic activity, (2) organised by co-operation between employer and employee, (3) for the production or distribution of goods and services calculated to satisfy human wants and wishes, prima facie there is an industry. Profit motive and capital are irrelevant. Hospitals, educational institutions, clubs and professions with organised activity can be industries. Only sovereign functions strictly understood are excluded.

  • The correctness of Bangalore Water Supply was referred to a larger bench in State of UP v Jai Bir Singh (2005). The reference was decided in 2026 (see below).
CaseState of UP v Jai Bir Singh (2026)

A nine-judge bench answered the 2005 reference. Disputes pending under the repealed Industrial Disputes Act continue to be governed by the Bangalore Water Supply triple test. The definition of 'industry' in s.2(p) of the Industrial Relations Code, 2020 must be applied on its own terms. A lead opinion reformulated the test for the future without overruling Bangalore Water Supply; the other judges saw no need to do so.

Current law

Current law. Bangalore Water Supply still governs pending ID Act disputes; for new disputes, apply the IR Code definition and its exclusions (charitable, sovereign, domestic).

  • IR Code definition: "industry" means any systematic activity carried on by co-operation between an employer and workers for production, supply or distribution of goods or services with a view to satisfy human wants (whether or not with capital or profit motive), but excludes institutions owned or managed by organisations wholly or substantially engaged in charitable, social or philanthropic service, sovereign functions (defence, research, atomic energy, space), domestic service, and other activities notified.

Industrial dispute

  • Any dispute or difference between employers and employers, employers and workmen, or workmen and workmen, connected with employment or non-employment, terms of employment or conditions of labour of any person (s.2(k) IDA).
  • An individual dispute becomes an industrial dispute if espoused by a union or substantial number of workmen; but dismissal, discharge, retrenchment or termination of an individual workman is deemed an industrial dispute (s.2A, 1965).
CaseWorkmen of Dimakuchi Tea Estate v Management of Dimakuchi Tea Estate (1958)

"Any person" in the definition of industrial dispute must be read in the context: the workmen raising the dispute must have a direct and substantial interest in the employment of that person. A dispute about a member of the staff (a doctor) who was not a workman was not an industrial dispute.

Workman

  • Any person employed in an industry to do manual, unskilled, skilled, technical, operational, clerical or supervisory work for hire or reward (s.2(s) IDA), excluding persons employed mainly in managerial or administrative capacity, and supervisory staff drawing above a wage ceiling.
  • IR Code defines "worker" on similar lines, raising the wage ceiling for supervisory staff (₹18,000 per month or as notified), and also includes working journalists and sales promotion employees.
CaseH.R. Adyanthaya v Sandoz (India) Ltd (1994)

A Constitution Bench held that medical representatives were not "workmen" under the IDA as it then stood, since their work was not manual, clerical, technical or supervisory; the dominant nature of the work decides.

4.4 Unfair labour practices

  • Introduced in 1982 (Fifth Schedule, IDA; ss.25T, 25U): practices by employers (interfering with union formation, victimisation, discharge for union activities, refusal to bargain collectively, recruiting strike-breakers) and by workers and unions (coercion, illegal strikes, go-slow, gherao, violence).
  • IR Code: prohibits unfair labour practices listed in the Second Schedule, with penalties.

Points to remember

  • Theoretical basis: social justice, welfare, industrial peace.
  • Conciliation, voluntary arbitration and adjudication; IR Code allows direct approach to the Tribunal.
  • Industry: triple test (Bangalore Water Supply) still governs pending ID Act cases (Jai Bir Singh, nine judges, 2026); IR Code s.2(p) read on its own terms, excluding charitable, sovereign and domestic activities.
  • Industrial dispute requires substantial interest (Dimakuchi); individual dismissals deemed disputes (s.2A).
  • Workman depends on dominant nature of work (H.R. Adyanthaya).
  • Unfair labour practices prohibited (Fifth Schedule IDA; Second Schedule IR Code).

Unit 5

Discipline in industry: restraints on managerial prerogative

The employer's power to discipline is now subject to fairness: a worker can be punished for misconduct only after a fair domestic inquiry, and only within limits during pending disputes.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Doctrine of hire and fire
  • Fairness in disciplinary process: misconduct, charge sheet, domestic enquiry, notice, evidence, cross-examination, representation, unbiased inquiry officer, reasoned decision
  • Prior permission and post-facto approval during pendency of proceedings (s.33 IDA)

5.1 Doctrine of hire and fire

Under common law, an employer could dismiss a worker at will on notice or payment in lieu. Indian labour law limits this through standing orders, the requirement of a fair inquiry, s.11A IDA (tribunal's power to modify punishment), protection during pending proceedings (s.33), and retrenchment conditions.

5.2 Fairness in the disciplinary process

Misconduct

Defined in the certified or model standing orders: wilful insubordination, theft, fraud, dishonesty, habitual absence, habitual late attendance, riotous behaviour, taking bribes, striking illegally, neglect of work. Misconduct outside the workplace may be punished if it affects the employer's business or reputation.

The right to know: charge sheet

The charge sheet must state the charges clearly with particulars, so the worker can answer them.

The right to defend: domestic enquiry

Essentials of a fair inquiry:

  1. Notice of the inquiry, with time, date and place.
  2. Opportunity to examine documents relied upon.
  3. Evidence led by the management in the worker's presence.
  4. Right to cross-examine management witnesses.
  5. Right to lead defence evidence and examine witnesses.
  6. Representation by a co-worker (and by a lawyer only if the rules permit or the management is represented by a legally trained person).
  7. An unbiased inquiry officer.
  8. A reasoned finding based on evidence.
  9. Proportionate punishment.
CaseDelhi Cloth and General Mills v Ludh Budh Singh (1972)

If the employer does not hold an inquiry, or the inquiry is defective, the employer may justify the action by leading evidence before the tribunal; the tribunal then decides on that evidence. The employer must seek this opportunity at the appropriate stage.

CaseWorkmen of Firestone Tyre and Rubber Co v Management (1973)

Explaining s.11A IDA (1971), the Court held that the tribunal now has power to reappraise the evidence, decide whether misconduct is proved, and interfere with the punishment if it is disproportionate, substituting a lesser punishment.

5.2.4 Prior permission and post-facto approval: s.33 IDA

  • During the pendency of conciliation or adjudication proceedings, the employer shall not, in regard to any matter connected with the dispute, alter conditions of service to the prejudice of workmen, or discharge or punish them for misconduct connected with the dispute, without the express permission of the authority (s.33(1)).
  • For matters not connected with the dispute, the employer may discharge or dismiss for misconduct, but must pay one month's wages and apply for approval of the action (s.33(2)(b)).
  • Protected workmen (union office-bearers) need express permission (s.33(3)).
CaseJaipur Zila Sahakari Bhoomi Vikas Bank v Ram Gopal Sharma (2002)

A Constitution Bench held that if the employer dismisses a workman under s.33(2)(b) without paying one month's wages or without applying for approval, or approval is refused, the dismissal is void ab initio, and the workman is deemed to continue in service.

  • Under the IR Code, a corresponding provision bars changes in conditions of service and discharge or punishment during pending proceedings without permission or approval, on similar lines.
Exam tip

Discipline questions are answered as a checklist: misconduct defined, charge sheet, inquiry with natural justice, unbiased officer, reasoned finding, proportionate punishment, then s.11A review and s.33 protection.

Points to remember

  • Hire and fire is now limited by statute and natural justice.
  • Fair domestic inquiry: notice, documents, cross-examination, defence, unbiased officer, reasoned finding.
  • Defective inquiry can be cured by evidence before the tribunal (Ludh Budh Singh).
  • s.11A lets tribunals modify disproportionate punishments (Firestone).
  • s.33: permission or approval during pendency; dismissal without approval is void (Jaipur Zila Sahakari).

Unit 6

Law relating to service conditions: standing orders

Standing orders codify conditions of service so that workers know their rights and duties, and employers cannot change them unilaterally.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946: scope and object
  • Model standing orders
  • Matters in the Schedule
  • Draft, certification and modification
  • Interpretation
  • Rule-making
  • Misconduct, disciplinary action and punishment

Scope and object

  • The Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946 required employers in industrial establishments with 100 or more workmen (reduced by some States) to define conditions of employment precisely and make them known to workmen.
  • Matters in the Schedule: classification of workmen (permanent, temporary, probationer, badli, apprentice); shift working, hours and holidays; attendance and late coming; leave; termination; suspension or dismissal for misconduct and acts constituting misconduct; grievance redressal.

Certification and modification

  • Employer submits draft standing orders to the Certifying Officer (s.3) within six months.
  • Copy sent to the union or workmen; objections heard; certifying officer decides whether the draft is fair and reasonable (s.4).
  • Appeal to the appellate authority (s.6).
  • Modification after six months, by application (s.10).
  • Until certified, model standing orders apply (s.12A).
  • Interpretation disputes may be referred to the Labour Court (s.13A).
CaseWestern India Match Co v Workmen (1973)

Certified standing orders have statutory force and are binding on employer and workmen; a contract of employment inconsistent with them to the workman's detriment is not valid.

Under the IR Code (Chapter IV)

  • Applies to industrial establishments with 300 or more workers (raised from 100), though a State may lower the threshold.
  • The Central Government frames model standing orders; employers may adopt them with suitable changes; if the employer adopts the model, it is deemed certified.
  • Matters include classification of workers, working hours, holidays, paydays, shift working, attendance, leave, termination, suspension or dismissal for misconduct, grievance redressal.
  • Disciplinary process: suspension pending inquiry with subsistence allowance (50% of wages for the first 90 days, 75% thereafter); inquiry to be completed ordinarily within 90 days.

Concept of misconduct and punishment

Misconduct includes acts listed in standing orders; punishments range from warning, fine, suspension, withholding increments to discharge and dismissal. Punishment must be proportionate and follow a fair inquiry.

Exam tip

Note the key change: standing orders now apply under the IR Code to establishments with 300 or more workers (previously 100), with deemed certification on adoption of the model.

Points to remember

  • Standing orders define conditions of service and misconduct.
  • Certified after hearing objections; statutory force (Western India Match).
  • Model standing orders apply until certification.
  • IR Code: threshold of 300 workers; model standing orders and deemed certification; subsistence allowance during suspension.

Unit 7

Industrial disputes: settlement machinery and adjudication

When negotiation fails, the law provides conciliation and adjudication, restricts strikes and lockouts, and regulates lay-off, retrenchment and closure.

What the syllabus lists for this unit

Under the IR Code

  • Grievance Redressal Committee (20 or more workers), Works Committee (100 or more).
  • Conciliation officers; Industrial Tribunals with a judicial member and an administrative member; National Industrial Tribunal.
  • Labour Courts are abolished; their functions go to the Industrial Tribunals.
  • Voluntary arbitration by written agreement.

Reference and voluntary arbitration (s.10, s.10A IDA)

  • Government reference is an administrative act; courts may review refusal to refer if based on irrelevant considerations (Telco Convoy Drivers Mazdoor Sangh v State of Bihar, 1989: the government cannot decide the merits of the dispute while refusing reference).
  • Voluntary arbitration under s.10A; award binding.

Award: binding nature and judicial review

  • Awards bind the parties and their successors (s.18(3)); remain in force for one year, extendable (s.19).
  • s.11A: power to modify discharge or dismissal.
  • Judicial review by High Courts (Arts. 226, 227) and the Supreme Court (Art. 136), on grounds of jurisdiction, error of law, perversity and natural justice.

Statutory limits on strikes and lockouts (ss.22 to 31)

  • Public utility services (s.22): no strike without notice within six weeks before striking, within fourteen days of giving notice, before the expiry of the date in the notice, or during conciliation and seven days after.
  • All industries (s.23): no strike or lockout during conciliation before a board, adjudication, arbitration, or during the operation of a settlement or award on matters covered.
  • Illegal strikes and lockouts (s.24) and penalties (ss.26 to 28).
  • IR Code: the notice requirements (60 days' notice; no strike within 14 days of notice; none during conciliation and seven days after; none during tribunal or arbitration proceedings and 60 days after) now apply to all industrial establishments, not only public utility services. Concerted casual leave by 50% or more workers is a strike.
CaseCrompton Greaves Ltd v Workmen (1978)

Where a strike is legal and justified, workers are entitled to wages for the strike period. A strike may be illegal but justified or legal but unjustified; wages depend on both legality and justification.

  • Fixed-term employment is recognised, with benefits on par with permanent workers.

Unit 8

Recommendations of the Second National Commission on Labour on industrial disputes

The Second National Commission on Labour (2002) proposed a single, simplified law on industrial relations, more flexibility for employers, and stronger protection through recognition and social security. Most of its ideas shape the IR Code.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Recommendations of the Second National Commission on Labour on industrial relations and industrial disputes

The Commission

Set up in 1999 under the chairmanship of Ravindra Varma; report submitted in June 2002.

Key recommendations on industrial relations

  • Consolidation of central labour laws into a few codes: a Labour Management Relations law combining the Industrial Disputes Act, Trade Unions Act and Standing Orders Act.
  • Recognition of a negotiating agent: a union with 66% support as sole negotiating agent, or a negotiating college with proportional representation of unions with at least 25%. (The IR Code adopted 51% and 20%.)
  • Labour Relations Commissions at the national and State levels, and Lok Adalats and arbitration to reduce adjudication delays.
  • Strikes: secret ballot before a strike (strike decision by majority); "go-slow" and "work to rule" to be treated as misconduct.
  • Flexibility: no permission needed for lay-off or retrenchment in establishments below 300 workers; prior permission for closure only above 300, with higher compensation (adopted in substance in the IR Code).
  • Social security for unorganised workers, and a separate law for small enterprises.
  • Grievance redressal committees in establishments of 20 or more workers.

Evaluation

  • Employers welcomed flexibility; unions criticised dilution of job security and restrictions on strikes.
  • The IR Code adopted many proposals: unified law, recognition, Grievance Redressal Committees, higher threshold for Chapter X, re-skilling fund, fixed-term employment, and extended strike notice to all establishments.

Points to remember

  • Second National Commission on Labour (Ravindra Varma, 2002) recommended consolidation into codes.
  • Proposed recognition of negotiating agents, Labour Relations Commissions, and flexibility thresholds of 300.
  • The IR Code implements many of these recommendations.

Part A Short answer questions5 × 4 = 20 marks

Answer any FIVE of the following.

1.Registration of trade unions
Model answer
  • Under the Trade Unions Act, 1926 (s.4), any seven or more members may apply for registration. Since 2001, a union must have at least 10% or 100 workmen, whichever is less, of the establishment.
  • The Registrar registers the union if the rules and particulars comply (ss.5 to 8).
  • A registered union becomes a body corporate (s.13), with immunities.
  • Under the Industrial Relations Code, 2020, the same thresholds apply, and the Code adds recognition of a "negotiating union" or a negotiating council (s.14).
2.Immunities of registered trade unions
Model answer
  • Criminal conspiracy (s.17): no liability for an agreement among members to further the union's lawful objects, unless it is an agreement to commit an offence.
  • Civil immunity (s.18): no suit lies in respect of acts in contemplation or furtherance of a trade dispute, merely because they induce breach of contract or interfere with trade, business or employment.
  • Enforceability of agreements (s.19).

The immunities do not cover violence, criminal acts or damage to property (Rohtas Industries v Rohtas Industries Staff Union, 1976). The IR Code continues them.

3.Collective bargaining
Model answer

Collective bargaining is negotiation between an employer and organised workers, through their union, on wages and working conditions, leading to a settlement.

  • It is recognised under ILO Conventions 87 and 98.
  • In India, settlements under s.18 of the ID Act bind the parties. Those reached in conciliation bind all workmen.
  • The IR Code introduces a negotiating union (51% membership) or a negotiating council.
  • The right to collective bargaining is not a fundamental right (All India Bank Employees' Association v NITB, 1962), but it is promoted by statute.
4.Standing orders
Model answer

Standing orders are rules governing service conditions, such as classification, hours, leave, misconduct and termination. They are made under the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946 for establishments with 100 or more workmen, and are certified by the certifying officer.

  • Certified standing orders have statutory force and bind the parties (Bagalkot Cement v R.K. Pathan, 1963).
  • Under the IR Code (Chapter IV), the threshold is 300 or more workers, and the model standing orders apply unless the employer adopts its own.
5.Definition of "workman"
Model answer

Under s.2(s) of the ID Act, a "workman" is any person employed to do manual, unskilled, skilled, technical, operational, clerical or supervisory work for hire or reward. It excludes:

  • persons in managerial or administrative capacity;
  • supervisory staff drawing over ₹10,000 per month; and
  • persons under the Army, Air Force or Navy Acts, and the police.
  • H.R. Adyanthaya v Sandoz (1994): medical representatives are not workmen.
  • The IR Code (s.2(zr)) includes working journalists and sales promotion employees. Its supervisory salary ceiling is ₹18,000.
6.Lay-off
Model answer

Lay-off is the failure, refusal or inability of an employer, owing to shortage of coal, power or raw materials, accumulation of stock, breakdown of machinery or natural calamity, to give employment to a workman on the muster rolls who has not been retrenched (s.2(kkk), ID Act).

  • Compensation is 50% of basic wages plus dearness allowance (s.25C).
  • Prior permission is needed in large establishments: 100 or more workmen under Chapter VB of the ID Act, and 300 or more under Chapter X of the IR Code.
7.Conditions precedent to retrenchment
Model answer

Section 25F of the ID Act requires, for a workman with one year's continuous service:

  • one month's notice in writing with reasons, or wages in lieu;
  • retrenchment compensation of 15 days' average pay for every completed year; and
  • notice to the appropriate government.
  • Non-compliance makes the retrenchment void ab initio (State Bank of India v N. Sundara Money, 1976).
  • "Last come, first go" applies (s.25G).
  • Retrenched workmen must be preferred for re-employment (s.25H).
  • The IR Code adds contributions to a Worker Re-skilling Fund.
8.Strikes in public utility services
Model answer

Under s.22 of the ID Act, no person employed in a public utility service may go on strike:

  • without giving the employer six weeks' notice;
  • within 14 days of giving the notice;
  • before the expiry of the date specified in the notice; or
  • during conciliation proceedings, and for seven days after they conclude.
  • An illegal strike is punishable (s.26).
  • Under the IR Code (s.62), these conditions apply to all industrial establishments: 60 days' notice, and no strike within 14 days or during conciliation or tribunal proceedings.
9.Unfair labour practices
Model answer

Section 25T read with the Fifth Schedule of the ID Act (now the Second Schedule of the IR Code) prohibits unfair labour practices. On the employer's side, these include:

  • interfering with union rights;
  • discharging workers for union activity;
  • victimisation;
  • recruiting strike-breakers; and
  • refusing to bargain.

On the union's side, they include coercion and violence. The penalty is under s.25U.

In Maharashtra, the MRTU and PULP Act, 1971 provides a separate code.

10.Discuss the meaning of "industry" under the Industrial Disputes Act and the Industrial Relations Code, with reference to Jai Bir Singh.
Model answer

Section 2(j), ID Act

"Industry" means any business, trade, undertaking, manufacture or calling of employers, and includes any calling, service, employment, handicraft, or industrial occupation or avocation of workmen.

Early cases

  • D.N. Banerji (1953): municipal activities.
  • Hospital Mazdoor Sabha (1960): hospitals.
  • Safdarjung Hospital (1970): restrictive, holding that non-profit hospitals were not industries.

Bangalore Water Supply and Sewerage Board v A. Rajappa (1978, seven judges)

The triple test: where there is:

  1. systematic activity;
  2. organised by co-operation between employer and employee; and
  3. for the production and/or distribution of goods and services calculated to satisfy human wants and wishes,

then prima facie there is an industry.

The dominant nature test applies:

  • profit motive and capital are irrelevant;
  • hospitals, educational institutions, clubs and professions are covered;
  • only sovereign functions, strictly understood, and charitable activities run by volunteers are excluded.

Response

  • The 1982 amendment to s.2(j) sought to exclude hospitals, educational institutions, charitable bodies and sovereign functions, but was never brought into force.
  • State of UP v Jai Bir Singh (2005): correctness was referred to a larger bench.

Industrial Relations Code, 2020 (s.2(p))

It codifies the test (systematic activity, co-operation and satisfaction of human wants), but excludes:

  • institutions owned or managed by organisations wholly or substantially engaged in charitable, social or philanthropic service;
  • sovereign functions, including defence, atomic energy and space;
  • domestic service; and
  • other notified activities.

Jai Bir Singh decided (2026, nine judges)

  • Disputes pending under the repealed ID Act remain governed by the Bangalore Water Supply test.
  • The IR Code definition must be applied on its own terms.
  • A lead opinion reformulated the test for the future, without overruling Bangalore Water. Other judges saw no need to reformulate.

Conclusion

For past and pending cases, Bangalore Water governs. New disputes turn on s.2(p) of the IR Code and its express exclusions, which significantly narrow the old position.

11.Explain the machinery for settlement of industrial disputes under the Industrial Disputes Act and the IR Code.
Model answer

Bipartite machinery

  • Works committees (s.3, ID Act): in establishments with 100 or more workmen, to promote amity.
  • Grievance redressal committees (s.9C): for 20 or more workmen. The IR Code keeps works committees (s.3) and grievance redressal committees (s.4).

Conciliation

  • Conciliation officers (s.4) and Boards of Conciliation (s.5).
  • Conciliation is mandatory in public utility disputes.
  • The report goes to the government, and a settlement in conciliation binds all.

Courts of inquiry (s.6)

These investigate matters (omitted in the IR Code).

Adjudication

  • Labour Courts (s.7, Second Schedule): dismissal, standing orders and similar matters.
  • Industrial Tribunals (s.7A, Third Schedule): wages, hours, retrenchment and closure.
  • National Tribunals (s.7B): national importance.
  • Reference by the appropriate government (s.10): the government may refer an existing or apprehended dispute. Its refusal is reviewable (Telco Convoy Drivers Mazdoor Sangh, 1989). A prior written demand is not a precondition (Premium Transmission, 2026).
  • Individual disputes (s.2A): may be raised directly before the Labour Court after 45 days.
  • Section 11A: the power to modify punishment.
  • Awards (s.17A): enforceable 30 days after publication.

Voluntary arbitration (s.10A)

IR Code changes

  • Industrial Tribunals with judicial and administrative members, and National Industrial Tribunals (ss.44 to 46).
  • Labour Courts are abolished.
  • Conciliation is time-bound, and parties may approach the Tribunal directly after it fails. The government's reference role is reduced.

Conclusion

The machinery moves from conciliation to adjudication. The IR Code streamlines it by allowing direct access to tribunals and reducing government gatekeeping.

12.Discuss the statutory regulation of strikes and lock-outs in India.
Model answer

Definitions

  • Strike (s.2(q)): cessation of work by a body of persons acting in combination, or a concerted refusal to work. It includes "go-slow" by judicial interpretation. The IR Code adds "mass casual leave" by 50% or more of workers.
  • Lock-out (s.2(l)): temporary closing of a place of employment, or suspension or refusal of work by the employer.

Right to strike

It is not a fundamental right (All India Bank Employees' Association, 1962; T.K. Rangarajan v Government of TN, 2003). It is a statutory right subject to restrictions.

Restrictions

  • Public utility services (s.22): six weeks' notice, a 14-day gap, and no strike during conciliation.
  • All industries (s.23): no strike during conciliation before a Board, or during adjudication or arbitration proceedings, or during the operation of a settlement or award on matters it covers.
  • Prohibition orders (s.10(3)): on reference.
  • IR Code (s.62): 60 days' notice for all establishments, and no strike during proceedings.

Illegal strikes and lock-outs (s.24)

These are strikes in contravention of ss.22 and 23 or prohibitory orders. Penalties apply (ss.26 to 28), and financial aid to illegal strikes is penalised.

Wages for the strike period

  • Illegal and unjustified strike: no wages.
  • Legal and justified strike: wages may be awarded (Crompton Greaves v Workmen, 1978; Syndicate Bank v K. Umesh Nayak, 1994).
  • "No work, no pay" applies to an unjustified strike.
  • Participation in an illegal strike can be misconduct, but punishment must be proportionate.

Government employees

Their strikes are regulated by conduct rules and essential services laws.

Conclusion

Indian law recognises the strike as a weapon of collective bargaining but regulates it heavily to protect public interest and industrial peace. The IR Code extends notice requirements to all establishments.

13.What are the requirements of a valid domestic inquiry for misconduct? Explain the power of the Labour Court under section 11A.
Model answer

Misconduct

Misconduct is defined in standing orders. It includes wilful insubordination, theft, fraud, habitual absence, riotous behaviour and striking illegally.

Stages of a domestic inquiry

  1. Charge-sheet: specific charges, with particulars and an opportunity to reply.
  2. Inquiry officer: an impartial person.
  3. Notice of hearing: to the workman, with the right to a co-worker's assistance (representation by a lawyer only if the employer is represented by one, or under the rules).
  4. Evidence: the employer's witnesses are examined in the workman's presence, cross-examination is allowed, and the workman may lead defence evidence.
  5. Findings: reasoned, based on evidence, and supported by material. The standard is preponderance of probability.
  6. Show-cause notice on punishment: under the rules, with a copy of the findings (ECIL v B. Karunakar, 1993).
  7. Order of punishment: by the competent authority, proportionate to the misconduct.

Defects

Non-supply of documents, bias, denial of cross-examination and perverse findings vitiate the inquiry. The employer may then justify the action by leading evidence before the tribunal (Workmen of Firestone Tyre v Management, 1973).

Section 11A

Introduced in 1971 after Firestone. Where an order of discharge or dismissal is referred, the Labour Court or Tribunal may:

  • re-appreciate the evidence;
  • decide whether the misconduct is proved; and
  • if satisfied that the discharge or dismissal is not justified, set it aside and direct reinstatement on terms, or award a lesser punishment.

The tribunal must rely only on the material on record. The IR Code continues this power (s.57).

Criminal acquittal

A criminal acquittal does not automatically bar departmental action, but dismissal on identical facts after a discharge on merits may be impermissible (Ex Sqn Ldr R. Sood, 2026). Termination for suppression of antecedents is not automatic (Shatrughn Yadav, 2026).

Conclusion

A fair domestic inquiry is the core of industrial discipline. Section 11A gives tribunals appellate-like powers to correct disproportionate punishment.

14.A hospital run by a charitable trust, which charges fees and employs 200 staff, dismisses a nurse. The trust argues that it is not an "industry". Decide under (a) the ID Act, for a dispute raised in 2024 and pending, and (b) the IR Code, for a new dispute.
Model answer

Issue: Is a charitable hospital an "industry"?

Law:

  • Bangalore Water Supply (1978): hospitals are industries if they satisfy the triple test. Only charities run by volunteers, where services are given free, may be excluded.
  • Jai Bir Singh (2026, nine judges): Bangalore Water governs disputes pending under the repealed ID Act. The IR Code definition is applied on its own terms.
  • Section 2(p) of the IR Code excludes institutions owned or managed by organisations wholly or substantially engaged in charitable, social or philanthropic service.

Application:

  • (a) The hospital is a systematic, organised activity serving human wants, with paid staff and fees, so it is an industry, and the pending reference is maintainable.
  • (b) Under the IR Code, if the trust is wholly or substantially charitable, it falls within the exclusion. Charging fees and employing paid staff may suggest it is not substantially charitable, which is a question of fact.

Conclusion:

  • (a) Under the ID Act, the dispute is maintainable.
  • (b) Under the IR Code, maintainability depends on proof of the trust's substantially charitable character.
15.A workman is dismissed for alleged theft without any domestic inquiry. The Labour Court finds the charge proved on evidence led before it, but feels that dismissal is too harsh for a first lapse involving ₹500. What can it do?
Model answer

Issue: The effect of the absence of an inquiry, and the power to modify punishment.

Law:

  • If no inquiry is held, or it is defective, the employer may justify the action by leading evidence before the tribunal (Firestone Tyre, 1973).
  • Under s.11A ID Act (s.57, IR Code), the tribunal may substitute a lesser punishment if dismissal is not justified.
  • Theft is serious misconduct, but proportionality and the employee's past record are relevant.

Application: The misconduct is proved before the court. Considering the small amount and the clean record, the court may find dismissal disproportionate.

Conclusion: The Labour Court may set aside the dismissal and impose a lesser penalty, such as withholding increments or reinstatement without back wages. Courts are cautious, however, where loss of confidence in a cashier or in a position of trust is involved.

16.A factory with 150 workmen retrenches 20 workmen without notice or compensation, citing losses. Advise the workmen.
Model answer

Issue: Is retrenchment without complying with s.25F valid?

Law:

  • Under s.25F of the ID Act (s.70 of the IR Code), conditions precedent apply: one month's notice or pay in lieu, 15 days' average pay per completed year, and notice to the government.
  • Non-compliance makes the retrenchment void ab initio (State Bank of India v N. Sundara Money, 1976; Anoop Sharma v Executive Engineer, 2010).
  • Prior permission (Chapter VB of the ID Act) is required only for 100 or more workmen. Under the IR Code (Chapter X), it applies only for 300 or more, so it is not needed here under the IR Code.
  • "Last come, first go" applies (s.25G).

Application: No notice was given and no compensation paid.

Conclusion: The retrenchment is void. The workmen may raise a dispute and seek reinstatement with continuity of service. Back wages are at the tribunal's discretion.

17.Workers in a power-generating company go on strike without giving notice, demanding a bonus. The company deducts eight days' wages and charge-sheets the union leaders. Advise the union.
Model answer

Issue: Is the strike legal, and are wages payable?

Law:

  • Electricity supply is a public utility service. Section 22 of the ID Act requires six weeks' notice; s.62 of the IR Code requires 60 days' notice for all establishments.
  • A strike in breach is illegal (s.24), and participants may be penalised (s.26).
  • Wages are not payable for an illegal strike (Crompton Greaves, 1978; Syndicate Bank v Umesh Nayak, 1994).
  • Leaders who instigate an illegal strike may face disciplinary action, after a fair inquiry and with proportionate punishment.

Application: The workers struck without notice, so the strike is illegal.

Conclusion:

  • The deduction of wages is valid.
  • Disciplinary action may proceed after a fair inquiry.
  • The union should raise the bonus demand as an industrial dispute through conciliation.
