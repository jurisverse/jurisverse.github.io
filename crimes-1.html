General: crime, the State and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita

A crime is conduct the State forbids on pain of punishment because it harms society. The BNS, which replaced the IPC on 1 July 2024, is India's general penal code.

  • State's power to declare acts or omissions crimes
  • State's responsibility to detect, control and punish crime
  • Crime distinguished from other wrongs
  • Short title, commencement and application of the BNS
  • Salient features of the BNS

1.1 Conception of crime

There is no single definition. Common approaches:

  • Legal: a crime is an act or omission prohibited by law and punishable by the State (Kenny: wrongs whose sanction is punitive and not remissible by any private person).
  • Blackstone: an act committed or omitted in violation of a public law forbidding or commanding it.
  • Social: conduct that offends the collective conscience or threatens social order.
  • Procedural: conduct that leads to criminal proceedings and possible punishment.

Under the BNS, "offence" means a thing made punishable by the Sanhita (s.2(24)); illegal, act and omission are defined in s.2.

1.2 State's power to declare crimes

  • Parliament and State legislatures declare acts criminal under Entry 1 of List III (Concurrent List): "Criminal law, including all matters included in the Indian Penal Code at the commencement of this Constitution".
  • The power is limited by Fundamental Rights: Art. 14 (no arbitrary classification), Art. 19 (reasonable restrictions only), Art. 20 (no retrospective offences, no double jeopardy, no self-incrimination) and Art. 21 (fair procedure and substantive fairness). Courts have struck down vague or arbitrary offences: s.66A of the IT Act (Shreya Singhal, 2015), s.303 IPC mandatory death sentence (Mithu, 1983), s.497 IPC adultery (Joseph Shine, 2018), and read down s.377 IPC (Navtej Johar, 2018).

1.3 State's responsibility to detect, control and punish crime

  • The State maintains police, prosecution, courts and prisons.
  • Procedure is now governed by the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS) and evidence by the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA), both also in force from 1 July 2024.
  • The State also owes duties to victims: compensation (s.395 and the victim compensation scheme under s.396 BNSS) and a fair investigation.

1.5 Short title, commencement and application

Statutes.1, BNS

(1) This Act may be called the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023. (2) It shall come into force on such date as the Central Government may notify. (3) Every person shall be liable to punishment under this Sanhita for every act or omission contrary to its provisions of which he is guilty within India. (4) Any person liable by any law in force in India to be tried for an offence committed beyond India shall be dealt with according to this Sanhita as if the act had been committed within India. (5) The provisions also apply to any offence committed by any citizen of India outside India, by any person on any ship or aircraft registered in India, and by any person in any place outside India committing an offence targeting a computer resource located in India.

  • The BNS was notified to come into force on 1 July 2024, repealing the Indian Penal Code, 1860 (s.358). Offences committed before that date continue to be governed by the IPC (s.358(2)).
  • Intra-territorial jurisdiction: every person, citizen or foreigner, for offences within India. Exceptions: the President and Governors (Art. 361), foreign sovereigns, ambassadors and diplomats, alien enemies in war, and foreign armed forces and warships with permission.
  • Extra-territorial jurisdiction: Indian citizens anywhere; persons on Indian ships or aircraft; and offences targeting computer resources in India.
CaseMobarik Ali Ahmed v State of Bombay (1957)

A Pakistani national in Karachi made false representations to a firm in Bombay by letters, telegrams and phone calls and obtained money. He was held liable for cheating under the IPC though he was physically outside India, because the offence was committed in Bombay where the representations were received and acted upon.

1.6 Salient features of the BNS

  • Structure: 358 sections in 20 chapters (the IPC had 511 sections in 23 chapters).
  • Reorganisation: offences against women and children brought together in Chapter V, placed before offences against the human body; offences against the State and public tranquillity reorganised.
  • New offences: organised crime (s.111); petty organised crime (s.112); terrorist act (s.113); murder by a mob on grounds of race, caste, community and similar grounds (s.103(2)); snatching (s.304); acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India (s.152, replacing sedition); sexual intercourse by deceitful means including a false promise to marry (s.69); hiring or employing a child to commit an offence (s.95).
  • Omitted offences: sedition (s.124A IPC) as such; adultery (s.497 IPC, already struck down); unnatural offences (s.377 IPC); attempt to commit suicide as a general offence (s.309 IPC); thug (ss.310, 311 IPC).
  • New punishment: community service (s.4(f)).
  • Gender: "gender" includes transgender (s.2(10)); several provisions are made gender-neutral (for example, offences against children).
  • Higher fines and mandatory minimum sentences for some offences; extra-territorial reach for offences targeting Indian computer resources.
  • Many sections are re-numbered without change of substance, so IPC case law continues to apply.

Points to remember

  • Crime is a public wrong punished by the State; tort and breach of contract are private wrongs.
  • Criminal law is in the Concurrent List; Fundamental Rights limit what can be made a crime.
  • BNS in force from 1 July 2024; 358 sections; repealed the IPC (s.358).
  • Jurisdiction is intra-territorial and extra-territorial (s.1(3) to (5)); Mobarik Ali Ahmed.
  • New offences: organised crime, terrorist act, mob lynching, snatching, s.152; community service added; sedition, adultery, s.377 and s.309 dropped.

Elements of criminal liability

Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea: an act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty. Liability normally needs a human being (or a company), a guilty act, a guilty mind and injury.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Author of crime: natural person, fit subject for punishment, companies and corporations
  • Mens rea
  • Importance of mens rea
  • Recent trend of strict liability in socio-economic offences
  • Injury to another

The four elements

  1. A human being (or legal person) under a legal obligation and capable of being punished.
  2. Mens rea: a guilty mind (intention, knowledge, recklessness, negligence, or a specified state such as dishonesty).
  3. Actus reus: a voluntary act or illegal omission, with its circumstances and consequences.
  4. Injury to another person or to society. Under s.2(14) BNS (s.44 IPC), "injury" means any harm illegally caused to any person in body, mind, reputation or property.

2.1 Author of crime: natural persons and corporations

  • "Person" includes any company or association or body of persons, whether incorporated or not (s.2(26) BNS; s.11 IPC).
  • Children below seven are incapable of crime (s.20); between seven and twelve, liability depends on maturity of understanding (s.21) (Unit 5).
  • Animals and inanimate objects cannot commit crimes in modern law.

Corporate criminal liability

Earlier courts held that a company could not be prosecuted for offences carrying mandatory imprisonment, because it cannot be imprisoned.

CaseStandard Chartered Bank v Directorate of Enforcement (2005)

A Constitution Bench held that a company can be prosecuted and convicted for an offence carrying mandatory imprisonment and fine; the court will impose the fine on the company. It cannot escape liability merely because imprisonment cannot be imposed.

CaseIridium India Telecom Ltd v Motorola Inc (2011)

A company can be held liable for offences requiring mens rea (here cheating). The mens rea of the persons who are its "directing mind and will" (alter ego) is attributed to the company.

  • But directors are not automatically vicariously liable for a company's offence under the general penal law, unless the statute so provides (Sunil Bharti Mittal v CBI, 2015).

2.2 and 2.3 Mens rea and its importance

  • The maxim actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea comes from English common law.
  • The IPC and BNS do not use the term "mens rea"; instead, each offence states the required mental element: "intentionally", "knowingly", "voluntarily", "dishonestly" (s.2(7)), "fraudulently" (s.2(9)), "rashly or negligently", "reason to believe" (s.2(29)), "corruptly", "maliciously".
  • The General Exceptions (ss.14 to 44) also reflect the need for a guilty mind: mistake of fact, accident, infancy, insanity, involuntary intoxication.

CaseNathulal v State of Madhya Pradesh (1966)

A food-grain dealer stored grain beyond the licensed quantity while his licence application was pending, honestly believing he would get it. The Supreme Court held that mens rea is an essential ingredient of a criminal offence unless the statute expressly or by necessary implication excludes it. He was acquitted.

CaseR v Prince (1875)

Prince took a girl under sixteen out of her father's custody, honestly and reasonably believing she was eighteen. He was convicted, because the statute was read as not requiring knowledge of her age; the act of taking a girl from her father was itself wrong. Compare R v Tolson (1889), where a woman who remarried believing on reasonable grounds that her husband was dead was acquitted of bigamy: an honest and reasonable mistake of fact negatived mens rea.

CaseSherras v De Rutzen (1895)

Wright J held that there is a presumption that mens rea is an essential ingredient of every offence; it may be displaced by the words of the statute or its subject-matter.

2.4 Strict liability in socio-economic offences

In public welfare and socio-economic legislation (food adulteration, foreign exchange, customs, environmental protection, drugs), statutes often dispense with mens rea, because:

  • the harm to society is great and widespread;
  • proof of the mental state is very difficult;
  • penalties are usually moderate;
  • businesses can be expected to take care.
CaseState of Maharashtra v Mayer Hans George (1965)

A German smuggler passing through Bombay on a flight from Zurich to Manila carried gold in breach of an RBI notification published only days earlier, of which he claimed ignorance. The Supreme Court (majority) convicted him under the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act and the Sea Customs Act. Mens rea was excluded by necessary implication, given the object of the statute; ignorance of the notification was no defence. Subba Rao J dissented, holding mens rea should not be excluded.

The Santhanam Committee (1964) and the Law Commission's 47th Report (1972) recommended stricter liability for socio-economic offences. Such offences often place a reverse burden on the accused, for example presumptions under the NDPS Act (ss.35, 54).

2.5 Actus reus and injury to another

  • Actus reus includes the act, its circumstances and its consequences. An "act" includes a series of acts (s.2(1)); "omission" includes a series of omissions; an illegal omission can be an offence where there is a legal duty to act.
  • Voluntarily (s.2(33) BNS, s.39 IPC): a person causes an effect voluntarily when he causes it by means he intended, or by means which he knew or had reason to believe were likely to cause it.
  • Causation: the act must be the legal cause of the result; an intervening independent act may break the chain.
  • Injury: harm illegally caused to body, mind, reputation or property. Offences against the State or public tranquillity injure society even without an individual victim.
Exam tip

For mens rea essays, show three things: (1) the BNS uses specific words instead of "mens rea"; (2) courts presume mens rea unless excluded (Nathulal; Sherras v De Rutzen); (3) socio-economic laws may exclude it (Mayer Hans George), with reasons for and against.

Points to remember

  • Liability needs a human or legal person, actus reus, mens rea and injury.
  • Companies can be prosecuted even for offences with mandatory imprisonment (Standard Chartered) and can have mens rea through their directing mind (Iridium).
  • The BNS states mental elements through words such as intentionally, knowingly, dishonestly, fraudulently.
  • Mens rea is presumed unless excluded expressly or by necessary implication (Nathulal).
  • Socio-economic offences often impose strict liability (Mayer Hans George).

Group liability

When several people act together, the law may hold each of them liable for what the group does: through common intention, common object, abetment, conspiracy and organised crime.

3.1 Why stringent provisions for groups

Combinations of persons are more dangerous than individuals: they increase the likelihood of the crime, make resistance harder, and threaten public peace. So the law punishes agreement, assembly and participation, sometimes before the main crime occurs.

3.2 Common intention: s.3(5) BNS (s.34 IPC)

Statutes.3(5), BNS

When a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each of such persons is liable for that act in the same manner as if it were done by him alone.

  • It is a rule of evidence and liability, not a separate offence; it must be read with a substantive offence.
  • Elements: (1) a criminal act; (2) done by several persons; (3) in furtherance of the common intention of all; (4) participation by each accused in some form.
CaseBarendra Kumar Ghosh v King-Emperor (1925)

In the Shankaritola post office robbery, several men attacked the postmaster; one fired and killed him. Barendra stood outside the door. The Privy Council held him liable for murder: "they also serve who only stand and wait". Participation need not be in the actual killing.

CaseMahbub Shah v Emperor (1945)

The Privy Council held that common intention implies a pre-arranged plan and a prior meeting of minds; it is different from similar intention. Mahbub Shah was acquitted of murder because the fatal shot was fired by another person on the spur of the moment, not shown to be in furtherance of a pre-arranged plan.

CasePandurang v State of Hyderabad (1955)

The Supreme Court held that common intention may develop on the spot, but there must be a prior meeting of minds; several persons acting with the same or similar intention independently are not liable under this rule.

3.3 Abetment: ss.45 to 60 BNS (ss.107 to 120 IPC)

Statutes.45, BNS

A person abets the doing of a thing who (a) instigates any person to do that thing; or (b) engages with one or more other persons in any conspiracy for the doing of that thing, if an act or illegal omission takes place in pursuance of that conspiracy; or (c) intentionally aids, by any act or illegal omission, the doing of that thing.

Three forms:

  1. Instigation: goading, urging, provoking or encouraging. Wilful misrepresentation or concealment of a material fact is also instigation (Explanation 1).
  2. Conspiracy: agreement plus an act in pursuance.
  3. Intentional aiding: facilitating the commission of the act.
CaseRamesh Kumar v State of Chhattisgarh (2001)

Words uttered in a fit of anger or emotion, without intending the consequences, do not amount to instigation. Instigation requires goading or urging forward with intent.

3.3.2 Mere abetment is punishable

  • Abetment is punishable even if the act abetted is not committed, or the person abetted is not capable of committing it (s.46, Explanations).
  • Abetment of abetment is an offence.
  • Punishments depend on whether the offence is committed (s.49: same punishment as the offence), and on the gravity of the offence abetted (for example, ss.55, 56 for offences punishable with death, life imprisonment or imprisonment).
  • Abetment in India of offences outside India (s.47) and abetment outside India of offences in India (s.48) are covered.

3.4 Unlawful assembly: ss.189 and 190 BNS (ss.141 to 149 IPC)

Statutes.189(1), BNS

An assembly of five or more persons is an unlawful assembly if the common object of the persons composing it is (a) to overawe by criminal force the Central or State Government or Legislature or any public servant; (b) to resist the execution of any law or legal process; (c) to commit any mischief, criminal trespass or other offence; (d) by criminal force, to take possession of property or deprive any person of an incorporeal right; or (e) by criminal force to compel any person to do what he is not legally bound to do.

3.4.1 Basis of liability: common object

Statutes.190, BNS

If an offence is committed by any member of an unlawful assembly in prosecution of the common object, or such as the members knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object, every person who, at the time of the offence, is a member of that assembly is guilty of that offence.

3.5 Organised crime: s.111 BNS (new)

Statutes.111(1), BNS

Any continuing unlawful activity including kidnapping, robbery, vehicle theft, extortion, land grabbing, contract killing, economic offence, cyber-crimes, trafficking in persons, drugs, weapons or illicit goods or services, human trafficking for prostitution or ransom, by any person or a group of persons acting in concert, singly or jointly, either as a member of an organised crime syndicate or on behalf of such syndicate, by use of violence, threat of violence, intimidation, coercion, or by any other unlawful means to obtain direct or indirect material benefit including a financial benefit, constitutes organised crime.

  • "Continuing unlawful activity" means activity prohibited by law, a cognizable offence punishable with three years or more, undertaken by a person or as a member of a syndicate, in respect of which more than one charge-sheet has been filed in the preceding ten years and cognizance taken.
  • Punishment: if the offence results in death, death or life imprisonment and fine not less than ₹10 lakh; otherwise, imprisonment not less than five years extending to life.
  • Petty organised crime (s.112): theft, snatching, cheating, unauthorised sale of tickets, betting, sale of public examination papers and similar crimes by groups or gangs, punishable with one to seven years.
  • Modelled on State laws such as the Maharashtra Control of Organised Crime Act, 1999 (MCOCA).

3.5 Criminal conspiracy: s.61 BNS (ss.120A, 120B IPC)

Statutes.61(1), BNS

When two or more persons agree with the common object to do, or cause to be done, (a) an illegal act; or (b) an act which is not illegal by illegal means, such an agreement is designated a criminal conspiracy. Provided that no agreement except an agreement to commit an offence shall amount to a criminal conspiracy unless some act besides the agreement is done by one or more parties in pursuance thereof.

  • The essence is the agreement. For conspiracy to commit an offence, no overt act is needed; for other illegal acts, an overt act is required.
  • Punishment (s.61(2)): for conspiracy to commit an offence punishable with death, life imprisonment or rigorous imprisonment of two years or more, as if the conspirator abetted the offence; in other cases, up to six months, fine or both.
CaseTopandas v State of Bombay (1955)

Two or more persons are needed for a conspiracy. Where four persons were charged and three were acquitted, the fourth could not be convicted of conspiracy with them alone.

CaseKehar Singh v State (Delhi Administration) (1988)

In the Indira Gandhi assassination case, the Court held that conspiracy is generally proved by circumstantial evidence; the agreement need not be proved by direct evidence, and conspirators need not know all details or all members.

CaseState (NCT of Delhi) v Navjot Sandhu (2005)

In the Parliament attack case, the Court explained that conspiracy can be inferred from circumstances and conduct, but suspicion is not enough; the circumstances must point unerringly to an agreement.

3.6 Rioting: s.191 BNS (ss.146, 147 IPC)

Whenever force or violence is used by an unlawful assembly or any member of it in prosecution of the common object, every member is guilty of rioting (s.191(1)). Punishment is up to two years, or up to five years if armed with a deadly weapon (s.191(2), (3)). Affray (s.194): two or more persons fighting in a public place and disturbing the public peace.

  • Cyber offences are mainly under the IT Act; BNS covers cyber stalking, cheating, forgery of electronic records and organised cyber-crime.

Stages of crime

A crime passes through four stages: intention, preparation, attempt and commission. The law generally punishes only the last two.

4.1 Mere intention is not punishable

The law does not punish thoughts alone. Intention is hard to prove and the person may change his mind. Exceptions where agreement or intent combined with minimal conduct is punished: criminal conspiracy (s.61), and sedition-type offences (s.152).

4.2 Preparation

Preparation is generally not punishable because it is difficult to prove that preparations were made to commit a crime, and the person may still abandon the plan (locus poenitentiae).

These are offences of a grave nature (against the State) or of a peculiar kind (possession offences, where possession itself shows the criminal purpose).

4.2.1 Terrorist act: s.113 BNS (new)

Statutes.113(1), BNS

Whoever does any act with the intent to threaten or likely to threaten the unity, integrity, sovereignty, security or economic security of India, or with the intent to strike terror or likely to strike terror in the people or any section of the people in India or in any foreign country, by using bombs, dynamite or other explosive substances, firearms, lethal weapons, poisonous or noxious gases, chemicals, biological, radioactive or nuclear substances or other means, causing death, injury, damage to property, disruption of essential supplies, damage to currency or monetary stability, or overawing any public functionary, commits a terrorist act.

  • Punishment: death or life imprisonment without parole if death results; otherwise, five years to life.
  • The BNS also punishes conspiracy, attempt, abetment, advocating, incitement and knowingly facilitating a terrorist act or "any act preparatory to" it (s.113(3)), and organising or recruiting for terrorist camps (s.113(4)). This is a clear example of preparation being punished.
  • An officer not below the rank of Superintendent of Police decides whether to register a case under the BNS or under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 (Explanation to s.113).

4.3 Attempt

Statutes.62, BNS (s.511 IPC)

Whoever attempts to commit an offence punishable by this Sanhita with imprisonment for life or imprisonment, or to cause such an offence to be committed, and in such attempt does any act towards the commission of the offence, shall, where no express provision is made for the punishment of such attempt, be punished with imprisonment of any description provided for the offence, for a term which may extend to one-half of the imprisonment for life or one-half of the longest term provided for that offence, or with fine provided for the offence, or with both.

CaseAbhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar (1961)

The accused applied to Patna University for permission to sit for the MA exam as a private candidate, supported by forged certificates of being a graduate and a headmaster. Admit card was issued but the fraud was discovered. The Supreme Court held this was an attempt to cheat, not mere preparation. Preparation was complete when he prepared the application; the attempt began when he sent it to the University. An attempt need not be the penultimate act; it is an act done towards the commission after preparation, during the course of committing the offence.

CaseState of Maharashtra v Mohd. Yakub (1980)

The accused brought silver bars in a truck to a lonely spot near the sea coast at night, waiting for a launch, and the sound of a boat's engine was heard. Held: attempt to export silver illegally, not preparation. Sarkaria J said an attempt begins where preparation ends, and is a direct movement towards commission; it need not be the last act.

CaseAman Kumar v State of Haryana (2004)

The Court distinguished attempt to rape from indecent assault: to constitute attempt, the accused must have gone beyond preparation with determination to commit the offence at all events, notwithstanding resistance.

CaseR v Taylor (1859)

An accused who lit a match intending to set fire to a haystack, but blew it out when he saw he was observed, was held guilty only of preparation in England; the test of locus poenitentiae applied.

Unit 5

Factors negativing guilty intention: general exceptions

The General Exceptions (ss.14 to 44 BNS) describe situations in which an act that would otherwise be an offence is excused or justified, because the guilty mind is absent or the act is lawful.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Mental incapacity: minority, insanity (cognitive impairment, emotional imbalance), medical and legal insanity
  • Involuntary intoxication
  • Private defence: justification and limits, when it extends to causing death (body and property)
  • Necessity
  • Mistake of fact

Burden of proof

Under s.108 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (s.105 of the Evidence Act), the burden of proving that the case falls within a General Exception is on the accused, and the court shall presume its absence. But the accused need only establish it on a preponderance of probabilities, not beyond reasonable doubt.

5.1 Mental incapacity

5.1.1 Minority (doli incapax)

Statutes.20, BNS (s.82 IPC)

Nothing is an offence which is done by a child under seven years of age.

Statutes.21, BNS (s.83 IPC)

Nothing is an offence which is done by a child above seven years of age and under twelve, who has not attained sufficient maturity of understanding to judge the nature and consequences of his conduct on that occasion.

  • Below seven: conclusive presumption of incapacity.
  • Seven to twelve: rebuttable; the prosecution must show maturity of understanding from conduct (for example, concealing the act).
  • Children in conflict with law (below 18) are dealt with under the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, which allows children of 16 to 18 accused of heinous offences to be tried as adults after preliminary assessment.

5.1.2 and 5.1.3 Insanity (unsoundness of mind)

Statutes.22, BNS (s.84 IPC)

Nothing is an offence which is done by a person who, at the time of doing it, by reason of unsoundness of mind, is incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that he is doing what is either wrong or contrary to law.

The section is based on the McNaghten Rules (1843). Daniel McNaghten, under a delusion of persecution, shot and killed Edward Drummond, secretary to the British Prime Minister, believing him to be Peel. He was acquitted, causing public outcry. The House of Lords put questions to the judges, whose answers became the rules:

  1. Every person is presumed sane until the contrary is proved.
  2. To establish insanity, it must be clearly proved that at the time of the act the accused was labouring under such a defect of reason, from disease of the mind, as not to know the nature and quality of the act, or, if he did know it, that he did not know he was doing what was wrong.
  3. A person under a partial delusion is judged as if the facts in his delusion were real.

Medical and legal insanity

  • Medical insanity: any mental illness recognised by psychiatry.
  • Legal insanity: only that unsoundness which destroys the cognitive faculty (knowledge of the nature of the act, or of its wrongness or illegality) at the time of the act. Emotional imbalance, uncontrollable impulse, or loss of self-control alone is not enough.
CaseDahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker v State of Gujarat (1964)

The Supreme Court held that the accused must prove legal insanity at the time of the offence on a preponderance of probabilities. Even if he fails to prove it fully, if the evidence raises a reasonable doubt about mens rea, he is entitled to acquittal, because the general burden of proving mens rea stays on the prosecution.

CaseSurendra Mishra v State of Jharkhand (2011)

Every person suffering from a mental disease is not exempted. The accused must prove legal insanity, not merely medical insanity, at the time of the act; abnormal behaviour or treatment for mental illness is not enough.

CaseRatan Lal v State of Madhya Pradesh (1971)

The accused set fire to a haystack and, when asked why, said he burnt it; a psychiatrist had found him a lunatic. He was acquitted under s.84, since he was incapable of knowing the nature of the act at the time.

Criticism: the McNaghten test ignores volitional insanity (irresistible impulse). Other tests: the irresistible impulse test, the Durham "product" test (US, 1954), and the diminished responsibility test (UK Homicide Act, 1957).

5.2 Intoxication

Statutes.23, BNS (s.85 IPC)

Nothing is an offence which is done by a person who, at the time of doing it, is, by reason of intoxication, incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that he is doing what is either wrong or contrary to law; provided that the thing which intoxicated him was administered to him without his knowledge or against his will.

Statutes.24, BNS (s.86 IPC)

In cases where an act is not an offence unless done with a particular knowledge or intent, a person who does the act in a state of intoxication shall be liable to be dealt with as if he had the same knowledge as he would have had if he had not been intoxicated, unless the thing which intoxicated him was administered to him without his knowledge or against his will.

  • Only involuntary intoxication is a complete defence.
  • Voluntary intoxication: the law presumes the knowledge of a sober man, but not intention. So intention may be negatived by evidence of extreme intoxication.
CaseBasdev v State of PEPSU (1956)

A retired army officer, drunk at a wedding, shot a boy who would not move aside. The Court held that voluntary drunkenness is no excuse; he is presumed to have the knowledge of a sober man. Intention may be inferred from the circumstances, and since he was not so drunk as to be incapable of forming intent, he was guilty of murder.

CaseDirector of Public Prosecutions v Beard (1920)

Drunkenness is a defence only if it renders the accused incapable of forming the specific intent essential to the crime; evidence of drunkenness falling short of this does not rebut the presumption that a man intends the natural consequences of his acts.

5.3 Private defence: ss.34 to 44 BNS (ss.96 to 106 IPC)

Statutes.34, BNS (s.96 IPC)

Nothing is an offence which is done in the exercise of the right of private defence.

Statutes.35, BNS (s.97 IPC)

Every person has a right, subject to the restrictions in s.37, to defend (a) his own body, and the body of any other person, against any offence affecting the human body; (b) the property, movable or immovable, of himself or of any other person, against an act which is an offence falling under theft, robbery, mischief or criminal trespass, or an attempt to commit such offences.

Justification and limits

  • The right is defensive, not punitive or retaliatory; it arises only against an offence.
  • It is available against acts of persons of unsound mind, children and intoxicated persons too (s.36).
  • Restrictions (s.37, s.99 IPC): no right (a) against acts of public servants acting in good faith under colour of office, unless reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt; (b) where there is time to have recourse to public authorities; (c) the harm inflicted must not be more than necessary for defence.
  • Commencement and continuance: for the body, it begins as soon as a reasonable apprehension of danger arises and continues as long as the apprehension continues (s.40); for property, specified in s.43.

5.3.1 When private defence extends to causing death

Statutes.38, BNS (s.100 IPC)

The right of private defence of the body extends to voluntarily causing death or any other harm to the assailant if the offence occasioning the exercise of the right is of any of the following descriptions: (a) an assault reasonably causing apprehension of death; (b) an assault reasonably causing apprehension of grievous hurt; (c) an assault with intention of committing rape; (d) an assault with intention of gratifying unnatural lust; (e) an assault with intention of kidnapping or abducting; (f) an assault with intention of wrongfully confining a person, in circumstances causing reasonable apprehension that he will be unable to have recourse to public authorities for release; (g) an act of throwing or administering acid, or attempt to do so, causing reasonable apprehension of grievous hurt.

Statutes.41, BNS (s.103 IPC)

The right of private defence of property extends to causing death if the offence is (a) robbery; (b) house-breaking after sunset and before sunrise; (c) mischief by fire or any explosive substance committed on any building, tent or vessel used as a human dwelling or as a place for custody of property; (d) theft, mischief or house-trespass in circumstances reasonably causing apprehension that death or grievous hurt will be the consequence if the right is not exercised.

  • In other cases, the right extends only to causing harm other than death (ss.39, 42).
  • s.44: in defending against a deadly assault, the defender may run the risk of harming an innocent person, if there is no other way.
CaseDarshan Singh v State of Punjab (2010)

The Supreme Court laid down principles: self-preservation is a basic instinct; the right is available when there is reasonable apprehension; a person under threat cannot be expected to weigh with "golden scales" the precise force needed; the accused need not prove the right beyond reasonable doubt; and the right is not available to an aggressor.

CaseDeo Narain v State of Uttar Pradesh (1973)

The right of private defence arises as soon as a reasonable apprehension of danger arises; one need not wait to be actually injured first.

5.4 Necessity

Statutes.19, BNS (s.81 IPC)

Nothing is an offence merely by reason of its being done with the knowledge that it is likely to cause harm, if it be done without any criminal intention to cause harm, and in good faith for the purpose of preventing or avoiding other harm to person or property.

Illustration: a captain who, to avoid a collision with a crowded boat, steers into a smaller boat with fewer people, acting in good faith, commits no offence.

CaseR v Dudley and Stephens (1884)

Shipwrecked sailors killed and ate the cabin boy to survive. Held: necessity is no defence to murder; one cannot take an innocent life to save one's own. Their death sentences were commuted.

5.5 Mistake of fact

Statutes.14, BNS (s.76 IPC)

Nothing is an offence which is done by a person who is, or who by reason of a mistake of fact and not by reason of a mistake of law in good faith believes himself to be, bound by law to do it.

Statutes.17, BNS (s.79 IPC)

Nothing is an offence which is done by any person who is justified by law, or who by reason of a mistake of fact and not by reason of a mistake of law in good faith believes himself to be justified by law, in doing it.

  • The mistake must be of fact, not of law (ignorantia juris non excusat); it must be in good faith, meaning with due care and attention (s.2(11)).
CaseChirangi v State of Nagpur (1952)

Under a temporary delusion, the accused killed his son with an axe, believing he was attacking a tiger. He was acquitted under s.79 IPC (now s.17 BNS), as he acted under a mistake of fact in good faith believing he was justified.

CaseR v Tolson (1889)

An honest and reasonable belief in the death of her husband was a good defence to bigamy.

Unit 6

Types of punishment

The BNS provides six kinds of punishment. Death is reserved for the rarest of rare cases; community service is the newest addition.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Death: social relevance of capital punishment and alternatives
  • Imprisonment: life, rigorous, simple
  • Forfeiture of property
  • Fine
  • Community service
Statutes.4, BNS (s.53 IPC)

The punishments to which offenders are liable under the provisions of this Sanhita are: (a) death; (b) imprisonment for life; (c) imprisonment, which is of two descriptions, namely rigorous (with hard labour) and simple; (d) forfeiture of property; (e) fine; (f) community service.

6.1 Death

Offences punishable with death under the BNS include: murder (s.103); murder by a life convict (s.104); murder by a mob on grounds of race, caste and similar grounds (s.103(2)); rape causing death or persistent vegetative state (s.66); gang rape of a woman under 18 (s.70(2)); rape of a girl under 12 (s.65(2)); repeat offenders of rape (s.71); waging war (s.147); abetment of mutiny actually committed (s.160); giving false evidence leading to conviction and execution of an innocent person (s.230(2)); kidnapping for ransom (s.140(2)); dacoity with murder (s.310(3)); organised crime resulting in death (s.111(2)(a)); terrorist act resulting in death (s.113(2)(a)); abetment of suicide of a child or person of unsound mind (s.107).

Constitutional validity and the "rarest of rare" doctrine

CaseJagmohan Singh v State of Uttar Pradesh (1973)

The death penalty was held not violative of Arts. 14, 19 and 21; the discretion of the judge in sentencing is guided by judicial principles.

CaseBachan Singh v State of Punjab (1980)

A Constitution Bench (4:1) upheld the death penalty for murder, but held it should be imposed only in the "rarest of rare cases", when the alternative option is unquestionably foreclosed. Life imprisonment is the rule, death the exception. Courts must weigh aggravating and mitigating circumstances relating to both the crime and the criminal. Bhagwati J dissented.

CaseMachhi Singh v State of Punjab (1983)

The Court gave guidance on the rarest of rare cases by reference to the manner of commission, motive, anti-social or abhorrent nature of the crime, magnitude, and personality of the victim.

CaseMithu v State of Punjab (1983)

Section 303 IPC, which made death the mandatory punishment for murder by a life convict, was struck down as violating Arts. 14 and 21, because it took away judicial discretion. (BNS s.104 now provides death or life imprisonment for the remainder of natural life, not a mandatory death sentence.)

CaseManoj v State of Madhya Pradesh (2022)

The Court required the trial court to collect material on mitigating circumstances (psychological evaluation, background, conduct in prison) before considering a death sentence.

CaseShatrughan Chauhan v Union of India (2014)

Unexplained and inordinate delay in deciding mercy petitions, solitary confinement, and mental illness of the convict are grounds for commuting the death sentence to life imprisonment.

6.1.2 Alternatives to capital punishment

  • Imprisonment for life meaning the remainder of natural life.
  • Special category of life imprisonment without remission for a fixed long term (for example, 20, 25 or 30 years), or for the whole of life: Swamy Shraddananda v State of Karnataka (2008), upheld by a Constitution Bench in Union of India v V. Sriharan (2016).
  • The BNS itself provides "imprisonment for life, which shall mean imprisonment for the remainder of that person's natural life" for many offences.

6.2 Imprisonment

  • Imprisonment for life: means imprisonment for the whole of the remaining natural life, unless commuted or remitted by the appropriate government: Gopal Vinayak Godse v State of Maharashtra (1961). For calculating fractions of terms, life imprisonment is reckoned as equivalent to 20 years (s.6 BNS; s.57 IPC).
  • Rigorous imprisonment: with hard labour (for example, agricultural work, carpentry).
  • Simple imprisonment: without hard labour; some offences are punishable only with simple imprisonment (for example, defamation, s.356(2)).
  • Commutation by the appropriate government is governed by s.5 BNS and ss.474 to 477 BNSS.

6.3 Forfeiture of property

Forfeiture is ordered for offences such as committing depredation on territories of such a power (s.154) and receiving property taken by such war or depredation (s.155). General forfeiture of all property (once in s.62 IPC) was abolished in 1921. Other laws provide for confiscation: the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002, the NDPS Act, 1985 and the BNSS (attachment and forfeiture of proceeds of crime, s.107 BNSS).

6.4 Fine

  • Where no sum is expressed, the amount of fine is unlimited but must not be excessive (s.8(1) BNS).
  • In default of payment of fine, imprisonment may be ordered, not exceeding one-fourth of the maximum term for the offence (s.8(2) to (6)); for offences punishable with fine only, default imprisonment is simple, up to two months, four months, one year or as specified according to the amount.
  • The BNS has raised fines for many offences.

6.5 Community service

  • A new punishment in s.4(f) BNS. The Explanation to s.23 BNSS defines it as work which the court may order a convict to perform as a form of punishment that benefits the community, for which he is not entitled to any remuneration.
  • Offences for which community service is provided include: public servant unlawfully engaging in trade (s.202); non-appearance in response to a proclamation (s.209); attempt to commit suicide to compel or restrain exercise of lawful power (s.226); theft of property worth less than ₹5,000 by a first-time offender who returns or restores the property (s.303(2) proviso); misconduct in public by a drunken person (s.355); and defamation (s.356(2)).
  • It aims at reformation and reduces prison overcrowding for petty offences.

  • Six punishments under s.4 BNS: death, life imprisonment, rigorous or simple imprisonment, forfeiture, fine, community service.
  • Death only in the rarest of rare cases (Bachan Singh); mandatory death unconstitutional (Mithu).
  • Life imprisonment means the whole of natural life (Gopal Godse); reckoned as 20 years for fractions.
  • Special life sentences without remission are an alternative to death (Swamy Shraddananda; V. Sriharan).
  • Community service is new; applies to petty offences such as first-time theft below ₹5,000 with restitution.

7.1.1 Culpable homicide: s.100 BNS (s.299 IPC)

Statutes.100, BNS

Whoever causes death by doing an act with the intention of causing death, or with the intention of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, or with the knowledge that he is likely by such act to cause death, commits the offence of culpable homicide.

Explanations: (1) a person who causes bodily injury to another who is labouring under a disorder or infirmity, and thereby accelerates death, is deemed to have caused death; (2) where death is caused by bodily injury, the person who caused it is deemed to have caused death though recourse to proper remedies might have prevented it; (3) causing death of a child in the mother's womb is not homicide, but causing death of a living child, any part of which has been brought forth, may be.

7.1.2 Murder: s.101 BNS (s.300 IPC)

Statutes.101, BNS

Except in the cases hereinafter excepted, culpable homicide is murder (a) if the act by which death is caused is done with the intention of causing death; or (b) if it is done with the intention of causing such bodily injury as the offender knows to be likely to cause the death of the person to whom the harm is caused; or (c) if it is done with the intention of causing bodily injury to any person and the bodily injury intended to be inflicted is sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death; or (d) if the person committing the act knows that it is so imminently dangerous that it must, in all probability, cause death or such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, and commits such act without any excuse for incurring the risk of causing death or such injury.

Punishment for murder (s.103(1)): death or imprisonment for life, and fine. Mob lynching (s.103(2)): when a group of five or more persons acting in concert commits murder on the ground of race, caste or community, sex, place of birth, language, personal belief or any other similar ground, each member is punished with death or life imprisonment and fine.

7.1.2.1 Distinction between culpable homicide and murder

Melvill J in Reg v Govinda (1876) set the two sections side by side:

Culpable homicide (s.100)Murder (s.101)
------
(a) Intention of causing death(a) Intention of causing death
(b) Intention of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death(b) Intention of causing such bodily injury as the offender knows to be likely to cause death of the particular person harmed; (c) intention of causing bodily injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death
(c) Knowledge that the act is likely to cause death(d) Knowledge that the act is so imminently dangerous that it must in all probability cause death

The key is the degree of probability of death: "likely" (culpable homicide) versus "sufficient in the ordinary course of nature" or "in all probability" (murder).

CaseReg v Govinda (1876)

The accused knocked his wife down, put a knee on her chest and struck her two or three times on the face with his fist, causing extravasation of blood on the brain; she died. Melvill J held that the injury was likely to cause death but not sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death, and convicted him of culpable homicide not amounting to murder.

CaseState of Andhra Pradesh v Rayavarapu Punnayya (1976)

The Supreme Court explained that culpable homicide is the genus and murder its species. There are three degrees: culpable homicide of the first degree (murder, s.300); of the second degree (punishable under the first part of s.304); and of the third degree (the lowest, punishable under the second part of s.304). It is the degree of probability of death that decides.

7.2 The mental element in murder: clause (c)

CaseVirsa Singh v State of Punjab (1958)

The accused thrust a spear into the deceased's abdomen, causing an injury that was sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death. Vivian Bose J laid down four steps for clause thirdly of s.300: the prosecution must prove (1) that a bodily injury is present; (2) the nature of the injury; (3) that there was an intention to inflict that particular injury, that is, it was not accidental or unintentional, or some other kind of injury was intended; (4) that the injury of the type described is sufficient to cause death in the ordinary course of nature. Once these are proved, it is murder, whether or not the accused intended to kill.

Clause (d) covers acts of imminent danger without excuse, for example firing into a crowd or driving a car at high speed into people on a footpath knowing it will in all probability kill.

7.3 Exceptions: when culpable homicide is not murder

Culpable homicide is not murder in the five Exceptions to s.101, punishable under s.105 (s.304 IPC): imprisonment for life or 5 to 10 years and fine (if done with intent), or up to 10 years or fine or both (if done with knowledge only).

7.3.1 Grave and sudden provocation (Exception 1)

The offender, deprived of the power of self-control by grave and sudden provocation, causes the death of the person who gave the provocation, or of any other person by mistake or accident. Provisos: the provocation must not be sought or voluntarily provoked; not given by anything done in obedience to law or by a public servant lawfully exercising powers; nor by a lawful act of private defence.

CaseK.M. Nanavati v State of Maharashtra (1962)

A naval commander learned from his wife that she was having an affair with Ahuja. He took his revolver from his ship, went to Ahuja's flat and shot him dead some three hours later. The Court held that the provocation was not "sudden", since he had time to cool down; the test is whether a reasonable man of the same class of society, placed in the situation, would be so provoked as to lose self-control. He was convicted of murder. The Court also recognised that words and gestures may, under certain circumstances, amount to grave and sudden provocation, and that the mental background created by previous acts may be considered.

7.3.2 Exceeding the right of private defence (Exception 2)

The offender, in good faith exercising the right of private defence, exceeds the power given by law and causes death without premeditation and without intention of doing more harm than necessary.

7.3.3 Public servant exceeding legitimate force (Exception 3)

A public servant, or a person aiding a public servant, acting for the advancement of public justice, exceeds his lawful powers and causes death by an act he in good faith believes lawful and necessary, without ill-will.

7.3.4 Death in a sudden fight (Exception 4)

Without premeditation, in a sudden fight in the heat of passion upon a sudden quarrel, without the offender having taken undue advantage or acted in a cruel or unusual manner. It is immaterial which party offered the provocation or committed the first assault.

7.3.5 Consent of the deceased and euthanasia (Exception 5)

The person whose death is caused, being above eighteen years of age, suffers death or takes the risk of death with his own consent.

Euthanasia:

  • Active euthanasia (a positive act to end life, such as a lethal injection) is murder or culpable homicide; consent at most brings it within Exception 5.
  • Passive euthanasia (withdrawing or withholding life support) is permitted under court-approved safeguards.
CaseAruna Ramchandra Shanbaug v Union of India (2011)

A nurse in a persistent vegetative state for 37 years after a sexual assault. The Court rejected active euthanasia but permitted passive euthanasia with the approval of the High Court in each case.

CaseCommon Cause v Union of India (2018)

A Constitution Bench held that the right to die with dignity is part of Art. 21; it recognised advance medical directives (living wills) and laid down guidelines for passive euthanasia, which were simplified by the Court in January 2023.

7.3.6 Death of a person other than the person intended

Statutes.102, BNS (s.301 IPC)

If a person, by doing anything which he intends or knows to be likely to cause death, commits culpable homicide by causing the death of any person whose death he neither intends nor knows himself to be likely to cause, the culpable homicide is of the description of which it would have been if he had caused the death of the person whose death he intended or knew himself likely to cause.

This is the doctrine of transferred malice: A aims at B but kills C; A is guilty of murdering C if killing B would have been murder.

7.4 Rash or negligent act causing death: s.106 BNS (s.304A IPC)

Statutes.106(1), BNS

Whoever causes death of any person by doing any rash or negligent act not amounting to culpable homicide shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to five years, and also with fine; and if such act is done by a registered medical practitioner while performing a medical procedure, he shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to two years, and also with fine.

Statutes.106(2), BNS

Whoever causes death of any person by rash and negligent driving of vehicle not amounting to culpable homicide, and escapes without reporting it to a police officer or a Magistrate soon after the incident, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description of a term which may extend to ten years, and shall also be liable to fine.

  • The IPC maximum was two years. The BNS raised it to five years generally, and created a separate, lower ceiling for doctors.
  • s.106(2) (hit and run) has not been brought into force. After a nationwide transporters' strike, the government announced in January 2024 that it would not be implemented without further consultation, and it was excluded from the commencement notification of 1 July 2024. The Gauhati High Court (December 2024) directed police not to register cases under it. Until it is notified, such cases fall under s.106(1).
  • Rashness means acting with consciousness of the risk but hoping no harm will follow; negligence means failure to take care that a reasonable person would take.
CaseJacob Mathew v State of Punjab (2005)

For criminal liability of a doctor under s.304A, negligence must be gross or of a very high degree; mere lack of care or error of judgment is not enough.

CaseAlister Anthony Pareira v State of Maharashtra (2012)

The accused, driving drunk at high speed, ran over people sleeping on a footpath in Mumbai, killing seven. The Court upheld his conviction for culpable homicide not amounting to murder (s.304 Part II), not merely s.304A, since he had knowledge that his act was likely to cause death.

7.5 Hurt and grievous hurt

Statutes.114, BNS (s.319 IPC)

Whoever causes bodily pain, disease or infirmity to any person is said to cause hurt.

Statutes.116, BNS (s.320 IPC)

The following kinds of hurt only are designated as grievous: (a) emasculation; (b) permanent privation of the sight of either eye; (c) permanent privation of the hearing of either ear; (d) privation of any member or joint; (e) destruction or permanent impairing of the powers of any member or joint; (f) permanent disfiguration of the head or face; (g) fracture or dislocation of a bone or tooth; (h) any hurt which endangers life or which causes the sufferer to be, during the space of fifteen days, in severe bodily pain, or unable to follow his ordinary pursuits.

  • The IPC period in clause (h) was twenty days; the BNS reduced it to fifteen days.
  • Voluntarily causing hurt (s.115); voluntarily causing grievous hurt (s.117); by dangerous weapons or means (s.118, ss.324 and 326 IPC); grievous hurt causing permanent disability or persistent vegetative state (s.117(3)); grievous hurt by a group of five or more on grounds of race, caste and similar grounds (s.117(4)).

7.6 Assault and criminal force

  • Force (s.128): causing motion, change of motion or cessation of motion to another, or to any substance in contact with his body or senses.
  • Criminal force (s.129): intentional use of force without consent, to commit an offence or intending or knowing it is likely to cause injury, fear or annoyance.
  • Assault (s.130): a gesture or preparation intending or knowing it likely to cause any person present to apprehend that criminal force is about to be used against him. Mere words do not amount to assault, but words may give meaning to gestures.
  • Punishment (s.131): up to three months, or fine up to ₹1,000, or both, except on grave and sudden provocation.

7.7 Wrongful restraint, wrongful confinement and kidnapping

  • Wrongful restraint (s.126, s.339 IPC): voluntarily obstructing a person so as to prevent him from proceeding in any direction in which he has a right to proceed.
  • Wrongful confinement (s.127, s.340 IPC): wrongfully restraining a person so as to prevent him from proceeding beyond certain circumscribing limits.

Kidnapping: s.137 BNS (ss.359 to 361 IPC)

Statutes.137(1), BNS

Kidnapping is of two kinds: (a) kidnapping from India: conveying any person beyond the limits of India without the consent of that person or of some person legally authorised to consent on his behalf; (b) kidnapping from lawful guardianship: taking or enticing any child or any person of unsound mind out of the keeping of the lawful guardian of such child or person, without the consent of such guardian.

  • Under the BNS, "child" means a person below eighteen (s.2(3)), so the age is uniform for both sexes; the IPC had 16 for boys and 18 for girls.
  • Punishment: up to seven years and fine (s.137(2)).
  • Aggravated forms: kidnapping or maiming a child for begging (s.139); kidnapping or abducting in order to murder, or for ransom (s.140); importation of a girl or boy from a foreign country (s.141).
CaseS. Varadarajan v State of Madras (1965)

A girl nearly eighteen, of her own accord, left her father's house and joined the accused, who married her. The Court held that "taking" requires some active part by the accused in causing the minor to leave; where the minor voluntarily leaves the guardian's protection and the accused merely facilitates it, it is not kidnapping.

CaseState of Haryana v Raja Ram (1973)

The accused, through a go-between, persuaded a fourteen-year-old girl to leave her father's house. Held: kidnapping. Persuasion by the accused creating willingness in the minor to leave is "enticing"; the consent of the minor is immaterial.

7.8 Abduction: s.138 BNS (s.362 IPC)

Whoever by force compels, or by any deceitful means induces, any person to go from any place, is said to abduct that person. Abduction is punishable only when done with a particular intent (for example, s.140 for murder or ransom; s.87 to compel a woman to marry).

7.9 Acid attack: s.124 BNS (ss.326A and 326B IPC)

Statutes.124(1), BNS

Whoever causes permanent or partial damage or deformity to, or burns or maims or disfigures or disables, any part of the body of a person, or causes grievous hurt, by throwing acid on or administering acid to that person, or by using any other means, with the intention of causing or with the knowledge that he is likely to cause such injury or hurt, or causes a person to be in a permanent vegetative state, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for not less than ten years but which may extend to imprisonment for life, and with fine, which shall be just and reasonable to meet the medical expenses of the victim and shall be paid to the victim.

  • s.124(2): attempt to throw acid: five to seven years and fine.
  • These provisions came into the IPC by the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013, after the Justice J.S. Verma Committee report.
CaseLaxmi v Union of India (2014)

On a petition by an acid attack survivor, the Supreme Court directed regulation of over-the-counter sale of acid, a minimum compensation of ₹3 lakh to victims by State governments, and free medical treatment by private hospitals.

Exam tip

In a homicide problem, always ask in order: (1) Was death caused? (2) Was it culpable homicide under s.100? (3) Does it fall within one of the four clauses of s.101? (4) Does any Exception apply? (5) If not culpable homicide, is it s.106 (rash or negligent)?

7.10 Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseHarish Rana v Union of India (2026)

The Court permitted withdrawal of life support for a patient in a persistent vegetative state for about 13 years, the first case in which passive euthanasia was actually allowed under the Common Cause guidelines. Active euthanasia remains an offence.

Points to remember

  • Passive euthanasia actually allowed (Harish Rana, 2026); active euthanasia still an offence.
  • Culpable homicide (s.100) is the genus; murder (s.101) is the species. The difference is the degree of probability of death (Govinda; Punnayya).
  • Clause (c) of s.101: intended injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature (Virsa Singh four steps).
  • Five exceptions: grave and sudden provocation (Nanavati: no time to cool), private defence exceeded, public servant, sudden fight, consent over 18.
  • Transferred malice: s.102.
  • s.106: rash or negligent death, up to 5 years (doctors 2 years); s.106(2) hit and run.
  • Grievous hurt period reduced to 15 days (s.116(h)); acid attack s.124.
  • Kidnapping (s.137) protects guardianship of children under 18; abduction (s.138) needs force or deceit.

8.1 Word, gesture or act intended to insult modesty: s.79 BNS (s.509 IPC)

Uttering any word, making any sound or gesture, exhibiting any object in any form, intending that it be heard or seen by a woman, or intruding upon her privacy, with intent to insult her modesty. Punishment: simple imprisonment up to three years and fine.

8.2 Assault or criminal force to outrage modesty: s.74 BNS (s.354 IPC)

Punishment: one to five years and fine.

CaseRupan Deol Bajaj v Kanwar Pal Singh Gill (1995)

A senior police officer slapped a woman IAS officer on her posterior at a party. The Court held that the essence of a woman's modesty is her sex; the test is whether the act is capable of shocking the sense of decency of a woman. Outraging modesty was made out.

8.3 and 8.4 Miscarriage without consent and death thereby

Causing miscarriage without the woman's consent (s.89, s.313 IPC) is punishable with imprisonment for life or up to ten years and fine; death caused by an act done with intent to cause miscarriage (s.90, s.314 IPC) up to ten years, or life if done without consent (see Unit 7).

8.5 Kidnapping or abducting a woman to compel marriage: s.87 BNS (s.366 IPC)

Kidnapping or abducting any woman with intent that she may be compelled to marry any person against her will, or forced or seduced to illicit intercourse, or knowing it likely; also inducing a woman by criminal intimidation or abuse of authority to go from any place with such intent. Punishment: up to ten years and fine.

8.6 Selling and buying a child for prostitution: ss.98 and 99 BNS (ss.372, 373 IPC)

Selling, letting to hire or otherwise disposing of a child (under 18) with intent that the child shall be employed for prostitution or illicit intercourse (s.98), and buying, hiring or obtaining possession of a child for such purposes (s.99), are punishable with imprisonment up to ten years and fine (s.98) and with imprisonment of not less than seven years extending to fourteen years and fine (s.99). The BNS makes these gender-neutral by using "child".

8.7 Dowry death: s.80 BNS (s.304B IPC)

Statutes.80(1), BNS

Where the death of a woman is caused by any burns or bodily injury or occurs otherwise than under normal circumstances within seven years of her marriage, and it is shown that soon before her death she was subjected to cruelty or harassment by her husband or any relative of her husband for, or in connection with, any demand for dowry, such death shall be called "dowry death", and such husband or relative shall be deemed to have caused her death.

  • Punishment: not less than seven years, which may extend to life imprisonment (s.80(2)).
  • "Dowry" has the meaning in s.2 of the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961.
  • Presumption as to dowry death: s.118 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (s.113B Evidence Act).
CaseKans Raj v State of Punjab (2000)

"Soon before" is a relative term; there must be a proximate and live link between the cruelty for dowry and the death. It does not mean immediately before.

CaseSatbir Singh v State of Haryana (2021)

The Supreme Court held "soon before" should not be read as "immediately before"; there must be a proximate and live link. Courts should first record the ingredients of s.304B before invoking the presumption under s.113B.

8.8 Rape: ss.63 and 64 BNS (ss.375, 376 IPC)

Statutes.63, BNS (definition, in brief)

A man is said to commit rape if he (a) penetrates his penis, to any extent, into the vagina, mouth, urethra or anus of a woman or makes her do so with him or any other person; or (b) inserts, to any extent, any object or a part of the body, not being the penis, into the vagina, urethra or anus of a woman; or (c) manipulates any part of her body so as to cause penetration; or (d) applies his mouth to the vagina, anus or urethra of a woman; under any of seven circumstances: (i) against her will; (ii) without her consent; (iii) with her consent obtained by putting her or any person in whom she is interested in fear of death or hurt; (iv) with her consent, when he knows he is not her husband and she consents believing he is; (v) with her consent when, at the time, she is of unsound mind or intoxicated and unable to understand the nature and consequences; (vi) with or without her consent, when she is under eighteen years of age; (vii) when she is unable to communicate consent.

  • Explanation 2: consent means an unequivocal voluntary agreement by words, gestures or any form of communication; a woman who does not physically resist is not, by that fact alone, regarded as consenting.
  • Punishment (s.64): rigorous imprisonment not less than ten years, extendable to life, and fine. Aggravated forms (by police, public servants, armed forces, jail or hospital staff, relatives, guardians, teachers, persons in a position of trust, during communal violence, on a pregnant woman, repeatedly) carry not less than ten years up to life imprisonment for the remainder of natural life (s.64(2)).
  • Rape of a woman under sixteen: not less than twenty years up to life for the remainder of natural life (s.65(1)); under twelve: not less than twenty years up to life for remainder of natural life, or death (s.65(2)).
  • Gang rape (s.70); death or persistent vegetative state (s.66); repeat offenders (s.71).
  • Sexual intercourse by deceitful means (s.69, new): sexual intercourse, not amounting to rape, by deceitful means or by making a promise to marry without any intention of fulfilling it; punishment up to ten years. "Deceitful means" includes false promise of employment or promotion, inducement, or marrying after suppressing identity.
CaseTukaram v State of Maharashtra (1979)

(the Mathura case) A young tribal girl was raped by two policemen in a police station. The Supreme Court acquitted them, holding that the absence of resistance and of injuries suggested consent. The decision caused national protest and led to the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 1983, which introduced custodial rape and a presumption of absence of consent in such cases.

CaseState of Punjab v Gurmit Singh (1996)

The testimony of a rape victim does not require corroboration if it inspires confidence; courts must deal with such cases with sensitivity, and trials should be held in camera.

8.9 Custodial rape

Rape by a police officer within the limits of his police station or in his custody, by a public servant on a woman in his custody, by a member of the armed forces in an area of deployment, by management or staff of a jail, remand home, women's or children's institution, or hospital, is punished as aggravated rape under s.64(2). Under s.120 of the BSA (s.114A Evidence Act), where sexual intercourse is proved and the woman states she did not consent, the court presumes absence of consent in such prosecutions.

8.10 Marital rape

Exception 2 to s.63: sexual intercourse or sexual acts by a man with his own wife, the wife not being under eighteen years of age, is not rape.

  • s.67 punishes sexual intercourse by a husband with his wife during judicial separation or while living separately under a custom or usage, without her consent (two to seven years).
CaseIndependent Thought v Union of India (2017)

The Court read down Exception 2 to s.375 IPC, holding that intercourse with a wife between fifteen and eighteen is rape. The BNS now reflects this by fixing the age at eighteen.

  • Petitions challenging the marital rape exception for adult wives (from the split decision of the Delhi High Court in RIT Foundation v Union of India, 2022, and other cases) are pending before the Supreme Court.
  • Arguments against the exception: violates Arts. 14, 15 and 21 (bodily autonomy, sexual privacy), consent is not irrevocable on marriage, and most democracies have removed it. Arguments offered for it: protection of the institution of marriage, availability of other remedies (s.85 cruelty, Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005), and difficulty of proof.

8.11 Sexual harassment: s.75 BNS (s.354A IPC)

A man committing physical contact and advances involving unwelcome and explicit sexual overtures; a demand or request for sexual favours; showing pornography against the will of a woman; or making sexually coloured remarks. Punishment up to three years (or one year for sexually coloured remarks) and fine.

CaseVishaka v State of Rajasthan (1997)

After the gang rape of Bhanwari Devi, a social worker, the Court framed guidelines on prevention of sexual harassment at the workplace, treating it as a violation of Arts. 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21. These were replaced by the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013.

8.12 Assault with intent to disrobe: s.76 BNS (s.354B IPC)

Assault or use of criminal force on a woman, or abetting it, with intent to disrobe or compel her to be naked: three to seven years and fine.

8.13 Voyeurism: s.77 BNS (s.354C IPC)

A man who watches or captures the image of a woman engaging in a private act, in circumstances where she would usually expect not to be observed, or disseminates such image. First conviction: one to three years and fine; second: three to seven years and fine. Explanation: dissemination without consent of images captured with consent is also covered.

8.14 Stalking: s.78 BNS (s.354D IPC)

A man who follows a woman and contacts or attempts to contact her to foster personal interaction repeatedly despite a clear indication of disinterest; or monitors her use of the internet, email or any other form of electronic communication. Exceptions: in pursuit of preventing or detecting crime under State authority, under law, or reasonable and justified in the particular circumstances. First conviction: up to three years and fine; subsequent: up to five years and fine.

8.15 Cruelty by husband or relatives: ss.85 and 86 BNS (s.498A IPC)

Statutes.86, BNS

"Cruelty" means (a) any wilful conduct which is of such a nature as is likely to drive the woman to commit suicide or to cause grave injury or danger to life, limb or health (whether mental or physical) of the woman; or (b) harassment of the woman where such harassment is with a view to coercing her or any person related to her to meet any unlawful demand for any property or valuable security, or is on account of failure by her or any person related to her to meet such demand.

  • Punishment (s.85): up to three years and fine.
  • Cognizable and non-bailable, but compoundable in some States by amendment.
CaseArnesh Kumar v State of Bihar (2014)

To prevent misuse of s.498A, the Court directed that police should not automatically arrest; they must satisfy themselves of the need for arrest under the conditions now in s.35 BNSS (s.41 CrPC), record reasons, and magistrates must not authorise detention mechanically.

CaseSushil Kumar Sharma v Union of India (2005)

The Court upheld s.498A, noting that the mere possibility of misuse is not a ground to strike down a provision; it is for courts to guard against frivolous complaints.

8.16 Remedies against obscene or indecent depiction of women

  • Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, 1986: prohibits indecent representation of women in advertisements, publications, writings, paintings and figures.
  • Obscenity: s.294 BNS (s.292 IPC) punishes sale and distribution of obscene books and objects, including in electronic form; s.296 (s.294 IPC) obscene acts and songs in public places.
  • IT Act: ss.67 and 67A (obscene and sexually explicit material), s.66E (privacy).
  • Common law and civil remedies: actions in tort for defamation and invasion of privacy; injunctions; and the right to privacy under Art. 21 (Puttaswamy).
CaseAveek Sarkar v State of West Bengal (2014)

A magazine published a photograph of Boris Becker with his fiancée, nude, to protest racism. The Court rejected the Hicklin test (tendency to deprave and corrupt those susceptible) and applied the "community standards" test, judging the work as a whole and in its context. Not obscene.

Exam tip

For rape answers, structure: definition (four kinds of acts, seven circumstances), consent (Explanation 2), punishment and aggravated forms, marital rape exception (Independent Thought), and evidentiary rules (Gurmit Singh on corroboration; presumption in custodial cases).

9.1 Theft: s.303 BNS (ss.378, 379 IPC)

Statutes.303(1), BNS

Whoever, intending to take dishonestly any movable property out of the possession of any person without that person's consent, moves that property in order to such taking, is said to commit theft.

Essentials:

  1. Dishonest intention (wrongful gain or wrongful loss) at the time of taking.
  2. Movable property (including things severed from the earth).
  3. Out of the possession of another.
  4. Without that person's consent (express or implied).
  5. Moving the property in order to such taking.
  • Punishment (s.303(2)): up to three years, fine, or both; for a second or subsequent conviction, one to five years and fine. Proviso: where the value of the stolen property is less than ₹5,000 and the person is convicted for the first time, he may be punished with community service on return of the value of the property or restoration of the property.
  • A person can commit theft of his own property if he takes it from someone who has lawful possession, such as a pledgee (Illustration (j)).
  • Aggravated forms: theft in a dwelling house or means of transport or place of worship (s.305), by a clerk or servant (s.306), after preparation for causing death or hurt (s.307).
CasePyare Lal Bhargava v State of Rajasthan (1963)

A government official removed a file from his office, took it home, and returned it the next day after substituting some papers. Held: theft. Wrongful loss or gain need not be permanent; temporary dispossession with dishonest intention is enough.

CaseK.N. Mehra v State of Rajasthan (1957)

A trainee air force cadet flew off in an aircraft without authority and landed in Pakistan. Held: theft. The intention to take dishonestly existed, even though he did not intend to deprive the owner permanently.

9.2 Snatching: s.304 BNS (new)

Statutes.304(1), BNS

Theft is snatching if, in order to commit theft, the offender suddenly or quickly or forcibly seizes or secures or grabs or takes away from any person or from his possession any movable property.

Punishment: up to three years and fine (s.304(2)). It fills the gap between theft and robbery for chain and phone snatching.

9.3 Cheating: s.318 BNS (ss.415, 417, 420 IPC)

Statutes.318(1), BNS

Whoever, by deceiving any person, fraudulently or dishonestly induces the person so deceived to deliver any property to any person, or to consent that any person shall retain any property, or intentionally induces the person so deceived to do or omit to do anything which he would not do or omit if he were not so deceived, and which act or omission causes or is likely to cause damage or harm to that person in body, mind, reputation or property, is said to cheat.

  • Punishment: up to three years (s.318(2)); cheating with knowledge of causing wrongful loss to a person whose interest the offender is bound to protect, up to five years (s.318(3)); cheating and dishonestly inducing delivery of property (the old s.420 IPC), up to seven years and fine (s.318(4)).
  • Cheating by personation: s.319 (s.416 IPC).
CaseHridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v State of Bihar (2000)

To constitute cheating, the intention to deceive must exist at the time of making the inducement. A mere failure to keep a promise later is a breach of contract, not cheating.

9.4 Extortion: s.308 BNS (ss.383 to 389 IPC)

Intentionally putting any person in fear of any injury to that person or any other, and thereby dishonestly inducing the person so put in fear to deliver to any person any property, valuable security or anything signed or sealed that may be converted into a valuable security. Punishment: up to seven years and fine; higher for fear of death or grievous hurt, or accusation of offences.

9.5 Robbery and dacoity

Robbery: s.309 BNS (ss.390, 392 IPC)

  • Theft is robbery if, in order to commit theft, or in committing it, or in carrying away or attempting to carry away property obtained by theft, the offender voluntarily causes or attempts to cause death, hurt or wrongful restraint, or fear of instant death, hurt or wrongful restraint.
  • Extortion is robbery if the offender, at the time of committing extortion, is in the presence of the person put in fear, and commits the extortion by putting that person in fear of instant death, hurt or wrongful restraint, and so induces him to deliver the property then and there.
  • Punishment: rigorous imprisonment up to ten years and fine; up to fourteen years if on a highway between sunset and sunrise.

Dacoity: s.310 BNS (ss.391, 395 IPC)

When five or more persons conjointly commit or attempt to commit a robbery, or where the whole number of persons conjointly committing or attempting it, and persons present and aiding, amount to five or more, every such person commits dacoity. Punishment: imprisonment for life or rigorous imprisonment up to ten years and fine. Dacoity with murder (s.310(3)): death, life or rigorous imprisonment not less than ten years and fine.

9.6 Mischief: s.324 BNS (ss.425 to 440 IPC)

Whoever, with intent to cause, or knowing that he is likely to cause, wrongful loss or damage to the public or to any person, causes the destruction of any property, or any such change in any property or in its situation as destroys or diminishes its value or utility, or affects it injuriously, commits mischief. The BNS includes aggravated forms based on value of damage, damage to public property, and mischief by fire or explosives.

9.7 Criminal misappropriation and criminal breach of trust

Dishonest misappropriation of property: s.314 BNS (s.403 IPC)

Dishonestly misappropriating or converting to one's own use any movable property. The initial possession is innocent (for example, finding property); the dishonest intention arises later. Punishment: six months to two years and fine.

Criminal breach of trust: s.316 BNS (ss.405 to 409 IPC)

Statutes.316(1), BNS

Whoever, being in any manner entrusted with property, or with any dominion over property, dishonestly misappropriates or converts to his own use that property, or dishonestly uses or disposes of that property in violation of any direction of law prescribing the mode of discharge of the trust, or of any legal contract, or wilfully suffers any other person so to do, commits criminal breach of trust.

  • Entrustment is the essential ingredient.
  • Aggravated forms: by a carrier, wharfinger or warehouse-keeper; by a clerk or servant; by a public servant, banker, merchant or agent (s.316(5): life or up to ten years).
CaseR.K. Dalmia v Delhi Administration (1962)

Directors who invested the funds of an insurance company in an unauthorised manner for their own benefit were held guilty of criminal breach of trust. "Property" includes funds; dominion over funds as directors amounts to entrustment.

9.8 Criminal trespass: s.329 BNS (ss.441 to 447 IPC)

Entering into or upon property in the possession of another with intent to commit an offence, or to intimidate, insult or annoy any person in possession, or having lawfully entered, unlawfully remaining there with such intent. House-trespass is criminal trespass into a building, tent or vessel used as a human dwelling, place of worship or for custody of property. Lurking house-trespass and house-breaking (s.330) are aggravated forms, with higher punishment by night (s.331).

9.9 Forgery: ss.335 to 340 BNS (ss.463 to 471 IPC)

  • Making a false document (s.335): dishonestly or fraudulently making, signing, sealing or executing a document or electronic record, or altering it, intending to cause it to be believed that it was made by or with the authority of a person by whom it was not made; or getting a person of unsound mind or intoxicated or deceived to sign it.
  • Forgery (s.336(1)): making a false document or electronic record, with intent to cause damage or injury to the public or any person, or to support any claim or title, or to cause any person to part with property, or to enter into any contract, or with intent to commit fraud. Punishment up to two years (s.336(2)); forgery for cheating, up to seven years; forgery to harm reputation, up to three years.
  • Forgery of valuable security, will, and similar documents (s.338): life or up to ten years.
  • Using a forged document as genuine (s.340).
CaseMohammed Ibrahim v State of Bihar (2009)

The accused executed sale deeds of land that did not belong to him. The Court held this was not "making a false document" because he did not claim to be someone else or that the deed was executed by someone else; executing a deed claiming property one does not own is not forgery (it may be cheating).

Offences against the State

Offences against the State protect the existence, sovereignty and security of the nation and its constitutional authorities. The BNS replaced sedition with an offence of endangering the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India.

11.1 Waging war: ss.147 to 150 BNS (ss.121 to 123 IPC)

Statutes.147, BNS

Whoever wages war against the Government of India, or attempts to wage such war, or abets the waging of such war, shall be punished with death, or imprisonment for life and shall also be liable to fine.

  • Conspiracy to commit offences under s.147 (s.148; s.121A IPC): life or up to ten years.
  • Collecting arms, men or ammunition with intention of waging war (s.149; s.122 IPC): life or up to ten years.
  • Concealing with intent to facilitate a design to wage war (s.150; s.123 IPC).
  • "War" is not limited to war in the international sense; it includes an organised attempt by force to overthrow the government or to compel it to change its policy. Numbers and arms are not decisive; the purpose and intention are.
CaseState (NCT of Delhi) v Navjot Sandhu (2005)

The attack on Parliament in December 2001 was held to amount to waging war against the Government of India, since the object was to strike at the sovereign authority of the State and overawe the government.

CaseMohd. Ajmal Mohd. Amir Kasab v State of Maharashtra (2012)

The November 2008 Mumbai terror attacks were held to be waging war against the Government of India; the death sentence was upheld.

11.2 Assaulting the President or Governor: s.151 BNS (s.124 IPC)

Assaulting or wrongfully restraining, or attempting to do so, or overawing by criminal force, the President of India or the Governor of any State, with intent to induce or compel them to exercise or refrain from exercising any lawful power. Punishment: up to seven years and fine.

11.3 Sedition and its replacement

The IPC offence

Section 124A IPC punished bringing or attempting to bring into hatred or contempt, or exciting or attempting to excite disaffection towards, the Government established by law in India, by words, signs or visible representation.

CaseKedar Nath Singh v State of Bihar (1962)

A Constitution Bench upheld s.124A but read it down: only words or acts which have a tendency or intention to create public disorder or incite violence are punishable. Strong criticism of the government, without incitement to violence, is protected by Art. 19(1)(a).

CaseS.G. Vombatkere v Union of India (2022)

The Supreme Court directed that s.124A be kept in abeyance while the government reconsidered it; no new FIRs should be registered and pending proceedings were to remain stayed.

The BNS offence: s.152

Statutes.152, BNS

Whoever, purposely or knowingly, by words, either spoken or written, or by signs, or by visible representation, or by electronic communication or by use of financial means, or otherwise, excites or attempts to excite, secession or armed rebellion or subversive activities, or encourages feelings of separatist activities or endangers sovereignty or unity and integrity of India; or indulges in or commits any such act shall be punished with imprisonment for life or with imprisonment which may extend to seven years and shall also be liable to fine. Explanation: comments expressing disapprobation of the measures, or administrative or other action of the Government with a view to obtain their alteration by lawful means without exciting or attempting to excite the activities referred to in this section do not constitute an offence.

Unit 12

Defamation

Criminal defamation protects reputation, which the Supreme Court treats as part of the right to life. Ten exceptions protect truth for the public good, fair comment and good faith communications.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Defamation
  • Punishment for defamation
  • Printing or engraving matter known to be defamatory
  • Sale of printed or engraved substance containing defamatory matter

12.1 Definition: s.356(1) BNS (s.499 IPC)

Statutes.356(1), BNS

Whoever, by words either spoken or intended to be read, or by signs or by visible representations, makes or publishes in any manner, any imputation concerning any person intending to harm, or knowing or having reason to believe that such imputation will harm, the reputation of such person, is said, except in the cases hereinafter excepted, to defame that person.

Explanations

  1. Imputation concerning a deceased person may be defamation if it would harm his reputation if living and is intended to hurt the feelings of his family.
  2. Imputation concerning a company, association or collection of persons may be defamation.
  3. An imputation in the form of an alternative or expressed ironically may be defamation.
  4. An imputation harms reputation only if it directly or indirectly, in the estimation of others, lowers the moral or intellectual character of that person, or his character in respect of his caste or calling, or his credit, or causes it to be believed that his body is in a loathsome state or a state generally considered disgraceful.

Essentials

  1. Making or publishing an imputation concerning a person.
  2. By words, signs or visible representations.
  3. With intention or knowledge, or reason to believe, that it will harm reputation.
  4. Publication to a third person.

10Caution intended for the good of the person to whom conveyed or for the public good

Unlike civil law, truth alone is not a defence in criminal defamation: it must also be for the public good (Exception 1).

12.2 Punishment: s.356(2) BNS (s.500 IPC)

Simple imprisonment for a term which may extend to two years, or fine, or both, or community service. Defamation is non-cognizable and compoundable; a court takes cognizance only on a complaint by the person aggrieved (s.222 BNSS; s.199 CrPC), except where a public servant is defamed in respect of public functions, when the Public Prosecutor may file a complaint with sanction.

CaseSubramanian Swamy v Union of India (2016)

The Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of criminal defamation (ss.499, 500 IPC and s.199 CrPC). Reputation is an integral part of the right to life and dignity under Art. 21; "defamation" in Art. 19(2) includes criminal defamation; the restriction is reasonable and proportionate. Magistrates should be careful in issuing summons.

12.3 Printing or engraving matter known to be defamatory: s.356(3) BNS (s.501 IPC)

Whoever prints or engraves any matter, knowing or having good reason to believe that such matter is defamatory of any person, shall be punished with simple imprisonment up to two years, or fine, or both.

12.4 Sale of printed or engraved substance containing defamatory matter: s.356(4) BNS (s.502 IPC)

Whoever sells or offers for sale any printed or engraved substance containing defamatory matter, knowing that it contains such matter, shall be punished with simple imprisonment up to two years, or fine, or both.

1.Mens rea
Model answer

"Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea": an act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is guilty. Mens rea is the mental element, expressed in the BNS by words such as:

  • "intentionally";
  • "knowingly";
  • "voluntarily";
  • "dishonestly";
  • "fraudulently"; and
  • "reason to believe".

Exceptions are strict-liability offences in public-welfare statutes, such as food adulteration and some economic offences (State of Maharashtra v M.H. George, 1965). Nathulal v State of MP (1966) holds that mens rea is presumed to be required unless the statute excludes it expressly or by necessary implication.

3.Attempt to commit an offence
Model answer

Section 62 BNS [511 IPC] punishes an attempt, where no express provision exists, with up to half the longest term provided for the offence.

  • Stages of a crime: intention, preparation, attempt and commission. Preparation is generally not punishable, except for waging war (s.149) and dacoity (s.310(4)).
  • Tests for attempt: proximity, equivocality, and the act being "towards" the commission of the offence (Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar, 1961).
  • Impossibility is no defence (illustration, pickpocket and empty pocket).
4.When the right of private defence of the body extends to causing death
Model answer

Section 38 BNS [100 IPC] lists the assaults that justify causing death, if the assault reasonably causes apprehension of:

  1. death;
  2. grievous hurt;
  3. rape;
  4. gratifying unnatural lust;
  5. kidnapping or abduction;
  6. wrongful confinement with apprehension of being unable to seek public authorities; or
  7. an acid attack causing grievous hurt.

The right is limited by s.37 (no more harm than necessary; no right where there is time to seek public authorities). It begins with reasonable apprehension and lasts while the apprehension continues (s.40).

5.Insanity as a defence
Model answer

Section 22 BNS [84 IPC] follows the M'Naghten Rules (1843). Nothing is an offence if, at the time of the act, by reason of unsoundness of mind, the person was incapable of knowing:

  • the nature of the act; or
  • that it was wrong or contrary to law.

Only legal insanity, not medical insanity, counts. The burden is on the accused, on a preponderance of probability (s.108 BSA [105 IEA]). Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker v State of Gujarat (1964) held that a reasonable doubt raised by the evidence entitles the accused to acquittal.

6.Kidnapping and abduction
Model answer
  • Kidnapping from lawful guardianship (s.137 BNS [361 IPC]): taking or enticing a minor (under 18 for any child under the BNS) or a person of unsound mind out of the keeping of the lawful guardian, without consent. It is a strict offence; the minor's consent is irrelevant (S. Varadarajan v State of Madras, 1965, on "taking").
  • Abduction (s.138 BNS [362 IPC]): by force, or deceitful inducement, compelling a person of any age to go from any place. It is a continuing offence and is punishable only with an ulterior purpose.

8.Dowry death
Model answer

Section 80 BNS [304B IPC]: where a woman dies by burns, bodily injury or otherwise than under normal circumstances within seven years of marriage, and it is shown that soon before her death she was subjected to cruelty or harassment by her husband or relatives for dowry, it is "dowry death". The punishment is a minimum of seven years, up to life imprisonment.

  • Section 118 BSA [113B IEA] raises a presumption against the person who caused the harassment.
  • "Soon before" means a proximate and live link (Satbir Singh v State of Haryana, 2021).
  • The presumption must be considered even at the bail stage (Mahesh Chand v State of UP, 2026).
9.Organised crime under the BNS
Model answer

Section 111 BNS creates a new general offence. It covers continuing unlawful activity by a group, singly or jointly, as a member of or on behalf of an organised crime syndicate, by violence, threats, intimidation, coercion or other unlawful means, for material benefit. Examples include kidnapping, robbery, vehicle theft, extortion, land grabbing, contract killing, economic offences and cyber crimes.

Punishment:

  • If death results: death or life imprisonment and a fine of at least ₹10 lakh.
  • Otherwise: a minimum of five years, up to life imprisonment.

Petty organised crime is dealt with separately (s.112).

10."All murders are culpable homicides, but not all culpable homicides are murders." Explain.
Model answer

Culpable homicide (s.100 BNS [299 IPC])

Causing death by doing an act:

  • (a) with the intention of causing death;
  • (b) with the intention of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death; or
  • (c) with the knowledge that the act is likely to cause death.

Murder (s.101 BNS [300 IPC])

Culpable homicide is murder, except in the exceptions, if the act is done:

  1. with the intention of causing death;
  2. with the intention of causing bodily injury the offender knows to be likely to cause the death of the particular person harmed;
  3. with the intention of causing bodily injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death; or
  4. with the knowledge that the act is so imminently dangerous that it must in all probability cause death, without excuse.

The distinction: degrees of probability

State of AP v Rayavarapu Punnayya (1976) explains the distinction:

s.100s.101
(a) intention to cause death1: the same; murder unless an exception applies
(b) injury likely to cause death2: injury the offender knows likely to cause death of that particular victim (special knowledge, e.g. an enlarged spleen)
(b) injury likely to cause death3: injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death
(c) knowledge of likelihood of death4: knowledge of imminent danger; death in all probability

Under the third clause of s.101, the prosecution must prove a bodily injury, its nature, that it was intended, and that it was sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death (Virsa Singh v State of Punjab, 1958).

Exceptions reducing murder to culpable homicide (s.101)

  1. grave and sudden provocation (K.M. Nanavati v State of Maharashtra, 1962: no cooling time);
  2. exceeding the right of private defence in good faith;
  3. a public servant exceeding powers in good faith;
  4. a sudden fight without premeditation, in the heat of passion; and
  5. consent of a person above 18 (relevant to euthanasia; passive euthanasia is permitted under the Common Cause guidelines, applied in Harish Rana, 2026).

Punishment

  • Murder: s.103, death or life imprisonment. Murder by a group of five or more on grounds such as race, caste or community (mob lynching) is covered by s.103(2).
  • Culpable homicide: s.105.

Conclusion

Every murder is culpable homicide, but culpable homicide becomes murder only when the higher degree of intention or knowledge in s.101 is present and no exception applies.

11.Explain the right of private defence and its limitations under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita.
Model answer

Basis

Section 34 BNS [96 IPC]: nothing is an offence which is done in the exercise of the right of private defence. Self-help is allowed because the State cannot protect everyone at every moment.

Extent (s.35 [97])

  • Defence of one's own or another's body against offences affecting the human body.
  • Defence of property, one's own or another's, against theft, robbery, mischief, criminal trespass and attempts.
  • The right exists even against a person of unsound mind, a child or an intoxicated person (s.36).

Limitations (s.37 [99])

  • No right against acts of public servants acting in good faith under colour of office, unless the act reasonably causes apprehension of death or grievous hurt.
  • No right where there is time to have recourse to public authorities.
  • No more harm may be inflicted than is necessary.

Extension to causing death

  • In defence of the body: s.38 (death, grievous hurt, rape, unnatural lust, kidnapping, abduction, wrongful confinement, acid attack).
  • In defence of property: s.41 (robbery, house-breaking by night, mischief by fire to a dwelling, and theft, mischief or house-trespass causing apprehension of death or grievous hurt).

Commencement and duration

  • Body (s.40): from reasonable apprehension, as long as the apprehension continues.
  • Property (s.43): until the offender escapes, the property is recovered, or the help of public authorities is obtained.

Judicial principles

  • The accused need not prove the right beyond reasonable doubt; a preponderance of probability is enough (Salim Zia v State of UP, 1979).
  • Harm need not be weighed in "golden scales" (Darshan Singh v State of Punjab, 2010, which set out guidelines).
  • No right is available to an aggressor.
  • Exceeding the right in good faith reduces murder to culpable homicide (Exception 2 to s.101).

Conclusion

Private defence is a defensive right, not a right to punish. It is limited by necessity and proportion.

12.Explain the stages of a crime. When does preparation become an attempt?
Model answer

Stages

  1. Intention. Not punishable; the law does not punish mere thought.
  2. Preparation. Arranging means. Generally not punishable, because it is hard to prove criminal intent and there is room to withdraw. Exceptions: preparation to wage war (s.149 BNS), preparation for dacoity (s.310(4)), making or possessing counterfeiting instruments, and possessing forged documents.
  3. Attempt. A direct movement towards commission after preparations are made. Punishable under specific sections (for example, attempt to murder, s.109) or generally under s.62.
  4. Commission. The completed offence.

Tests to distinguish preparation from attempt

  • Proximity test: the act must be sufficiently near to completion (R v Taylor, 1859).
  • Locus poenitentiae: whether the accused could still withdraw (Malkiat Singh v State of Punjab, 1969: a truck with paddy stopped before crossing the State border was only preparation).
  • Equivocality test: the act itself must show the intent unequivocally.
  • Indian test: Abhayanand Mishra v State of Bihar (1961). An attempt begins when the accused commences doing an act with the intention of committing the offence, after preparation, and the act need not be the penultimate one. Applying for university admission with forged certificates was held to be an attempt to cheat.
  • State of Maharashtra v Mohd Yakub (1980): smuggling silver; the steps had gone beyond preparation.

Impossibility

Factual impossibility is no defence: an attempt to steal from an empty pocket is still an attempt (Asgarali Pradhania v Emperor, 1933). An attempt to commit something that is not an offence at all is not punishable.

Conclusion

The line between preparation and attempt depends on the facts. The guiding question is whether the accused has gone beyond getting ready and begun to execute the crime.

13.Discuss the law relating to rape under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita.
Model answer

Definition (s.63 BNS [375 IPC])

A man commits rape if he does the acts specified (penetration of the vagina, mouth, urethra or anus, by penis, object or body part, or application of the mouth) in any of seven circumstances:

  1. against her will;
  2. without her consent;
  3. with consent obtained by fear of death or hurt;
  4. with consent given in the belief that he is her husband;
  5. with consent given while of unsound mind or intoxicated, unable to understand;
  6. with or without consent when she is under 18; or
  7. when she is unable to communicate consent.

Consent means an unequivocal voluntary agreement. The absence of physical resistance is not consent.

Marital exception

Sexual acts by a husband with his wife, if she is not under 18, are not rape (Exception 2). Independent Thought v Union of India (2017) had read down the earlier exception for wives aged 15 to 18. A challenge to the marital exception is pending.

Punishment

  • Section 64: rigorous imprisonment for at least ten years, extendable to life, and fine. Aggravated forms under s.64(2), such as rape by a police officer, public servant, relative or doctor, carry at least ten years up to life for the remainder of natural life.
  • Section 65: rape of a woman under 16 (minimum twenty years, up to life); under 12 (minimum twenty years, up to life for the remainder of natural life, or death).
  • Section 66: rape causing death or a persistent vegetative state.
  • Section 70: gang rape; gang rape of a girl under 18 may attract death.
  • Section 69 (new): sexual intercourse by deceitful means, including a false promise of marriage or employment, up to ten years.

Procedure and evidence

  • A woman officer records the statement, which is videographed. The trial is in camera, the victim's identity is protected (s.72), and there is a time limit for investigation.
  • Section 120 BSA [114A IEA]: a presumption of absence of consent in aggravated cases.
  • The victim's past sexual history is irrelevant.
  • The two-finger test is banned (State of Jharkhand v Shailendra Kumar Rai, 2022).

Related laws

The POCSO Act, 2012 governs children; recent cases discuss age determination and adolescent relationships (State of UP v Anurudh, 2026).

Conclusion

The BNS keeps the expanded definition from the 2013 and 2018 amendments, adds deceitful sexual intercourse as a distinct offence, and imposes severe minimum sentences, with procedural safeguards for victims.

14.A and B quarrel. A strikes B once on the head with a heavy iron rod, intending to cause grievous injury. B dies due to a skull fracture. A pleads that he did not intend to kill. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Is A guilty of murder or of culpable homicide not amounting to murder?

Law:

  • Section 101 clause 3 BNS: an intention to cause bodily injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death is murder.
  • Virsa Singh v State of Punjab (1958): once it is proved that the accused intended the particular injury, and that injury is sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death, it is murder, whether or not he intended death.
  • Exception 4 (sudden fight) applies only if the offender has not taken undue advantage or acted cruelly.

Application: A deliberately struck a vital part, the head, with a heavy iron rod. The fracture was sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death. If the quarrel was a sudden fight without premeditation and A struck only one blow in the heat of passion, Exception 4 may reduce the offence.

Conclusion: Ordinarily, A is guilty of murder (s.103). If the sudden-fight exception is proved on the facts, he is guilty of culpable homicide not amounting to murder (s.105).

15.A, B and C plan to rob a jeweller. A stands guard outside, while B and C enter. When the jeweller resists, C unexpectedly shoots him dead. A pleads that he never intended any killing. Decide the liability of A, B and C.
Model answer

Issue: Are A and B liable for murder committed by C?

Law:

  • Section 3(5) BNS [34 IPC]: when a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each is liable as if he did it alone.
  • A person standing guard participates (Barendra Kumar Ghosh, 1925).
  • The act must be in furtherance of the common intention. If the killing was outside it, only the doer is liable (Mahbub Shah, 1945). If the co-accused knew that firearms were carried and that resistance was likely, an intention to kill if resisted may be inferred.
  • Robbery is defined in s.309. Dacoity (s.310) needs five or more persons, so it does not apply to three.

Application: All three shared the intention to rob, so all are guilty of robbery. If A and B knew that C carried a loaded gun to overcome resistance, the murder was in furtherance of the common intention. Otherwise, only C is liable for murder.

Conclusion: C is guilty of murder (s.103). A and B are guilty of robbery (s.309), and of murder under s.3(5) only if the common intention extended to killing.

16.Z gives his watch to a repairer, R, for repair. After the repair, R refuses to return it until his charges are paid, as he is entitled to. Z secretly takes the watch from R's shop without paying. Has Z committed theft?
Model answer

Issue: Can a person commit theft of his own property?

Law:

  • Theft (s.303 BNS [378 IPC]) is dishonestly taking movable property out of the possession of any person without that person's consent.
  • The illustrations to s.303 (corresponding to illustration (j) of s.378 IPC) show that a person who takes his own property from someone with a lawful lien over it, such as a watchmaker, commits theft.
  • "Dishonestly" means intending to cause wrongful gain or wrongful loss. Depriving R of his lawful security causes wrongful loss.

Application: R had lawful possession and a lien for his charges. Z took the watch without R's consent, intending to cause wrongful loss.

Conclusion: Z is guilty of theft. Ownership is no defence where another has a lawful possessory right.

17.W died of burns in her matrimonial home two years after her marriage. Her parents state that the husband and mother-in-law demanded a car and harassed her, including a week before her death. The husband claims it was an accident. Discuss their liability.
Model answer

Issue: Is this a dowry death, and is there a presumption against the accused?

Law:

  • Section 80 BNS [304B IPC]: an unnatural death within seven years of marriage, with cruelty or harassment for dowry "soon before" death, is dowry death. Punishment is seven years to life.
  • Section 118 BSA [113B IEA]: the court shall presume that the person who subjected her to such cruelty caused the dowry death.
  • Cruelty is also punishable under s.85 BNS [498A IPC].
  • Satbir Singh v State of Haryana (2021): there must be a proximate and live link. The presumption must also be applied at the bail stage (Mahesh Chand v State of UP, 2026).

Application: The death by burns is unnatural, it occurred within seven years of marriage, and there is evidence of a dowry demand and harassment a week before death. The presumption arises, and the burden shifts to the accused to rebut it, for example by proving an accident.

Conclusion: The husband and mother-in-law are liable under ss.80 and 85 BNS, and under the Dowry Prohibition Act, unless they rebut the presumption. The court should also consider murder (s.103) if the evidence supports it.
