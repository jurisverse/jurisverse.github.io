PART: A | Short answer questions | Answer any FIVE of the following. | 5 × 4 = 20 marks

Q: Lifting the corporate veil
The court disregards the separate personality of a company and looks at the persons behind it. This happens in cases of:
- fraud or improper conduct;
- evasion of legal obligations (*Gilford Motor Co v Horne*, 1933);
- determining enemy character (*Daimler v Continental Tyre*, 1916);
- tax evasion (*Dinshaw Maneckjee Petit*, 1927);
- sham companies (*Jones v Lipman*, 1962); and
- groups acting as a single economic unit (*DHN Food Distributors*, 1976).
Statutory instances in the Companies Act, 2013 include misstatement in a prospectus (s.34), fraudulent conduct of business (s.339) and liability of officers in default (s.2(60)). In India, see *Tata Engineering v State of Bihar* (1965) and *Delhi Development Authority v Skipper Construction* (1996).

Q: Doctrine of indoor management
Outsiders dealing with a company are entitled to assume that its internal requirements and procedures have been complied with (*Royal British Bank v Turquand*, 1856). The doctrine limits constructive notice.
Exceptions:
- knowledge of the irregularity;
- negligence, or suspicious circumstances calling for inquiry;
- forgery, which is void (*Ruben v Great Fingall Consolidated*, 1906); and
- acts outside the apparent authority of the officer.

Q: Doctrine of ultra vires
An act beyond the objects in the memorandum of association is ultra vires the company and void. It cannot be ratified even by all shareholders (*Ashbury Railway Carriage v Riche*, 1875). Acts within the company's objects but beyond the directors' powers may be ratified.
- In India: *A. Lakshmanaswami Mudaliar v LIC* (1963) (a donation beyond powers).
- Under the 2013 Act, the objects clause is required (s.4(1)(c)), but companies may adopt broad objects. Shareholders may restrain ultra vires acts by injunction.

Q: One Person Company
An OPC is defined in s.2(62) of the Companies Act, 2013: a company with only one member, who must be a natural person and an Indian citizen. Residency was relaxed in 2021 to 120 days.
- It must nominate a person to take over on the member's death or incapacity.
- There is no requirement to hold an AGM, and it has fewer compliance requirements.
- Since 2021, there are no paid-up capital or turnover limits for conversion.
- It combines limited liability with sole ownership.

Q: Promoter
A promoter is the person who takes the steps to form a company and set it going (s.2(69)). The promoter stands in a fiduciary position towards the company and must disclose secret profits (*Erlanger v New Sombrero Phosphate*, 1878).
- **Pre-incorporation contracts:** the company is not bound in common law (*Kelner v Baxter*, 1866). Under ss.15(h) and 19(e) of the Specific Relief Act, the company may adopt the contract if it is warranted by the terms of incorporation.
- **Liability:** for misstatements in a prospectus (ss.34 and 35) and fraud (s.447).

Q: Misstatement in a prospectus
**Civil liability (s.35):** every director, promoter, expert and person authorising the issue is liable to compensate subscribers for loss caused by untrue or misleading statements, or omissions.
Defences:
- withdrawal of consent;
- the issue was made without the person's knowledge; and
- reasonable grounds for belief in the statement.
**Criminal liability (s.34):** punishment as for fraud under s.447, unless the statement was immaterial or the person had reasonable grounds.
Remedies include rescission of the allotment and damages. Classic case: *Derry v Peek* (1889).

Q: Independent directors
Under s.149(6), an independent director is a director other than a managing or whole-time director, who has integrity and expertise and no pecuniary relationship with the company.
- Listed companies must have at least one-third independent directors (s.149(4)).
- Tenure is up to two terms of five years.
- They are subject to a code of conduct (Schedule IV).
- They have limited liability, only for acts with their knowledge or consent (s.149(12)).
- They are part of the audit and nomination committees.

Q: Corporate social responsibility
Under s.135, companies with a net worth of ₹500 crore or more, turnover of ₹1,000 crore or more, or net profit of ₹5 crore or more must:
- spend at least 2% of the average net profit of the preceding three years on CSR activities listed in Schedule VII; and
- constitute a CSR Committee (not required where the obligation is up to ₹50 lakh).
Unspent amounts must be transferred to a Schedule VII fund or an unspent CSR account (2019 amendment). Non-compliance is a civil penalty after the 2020 amendment.

Q: Class action
Under s.245, members or depositors may file an application before the NCLT on behalf of a class, where the affairs of the company are conducted prejudicially. Thresholds: at least 100 members, or a prescribed percentage.
Reliefs include:
- restraining ultra vires acts and breaches of the articles;
- declaring resolutions void; and
- claiming damages against the company, directors, auditors and advisers.
It was introduced after the Satyam fraud.

PART: B | Essay questions | Answer any TWO of the following. | 2 × 15 = 30 marks

Q: "A company is a legal person distinct from its members." Discuss with reference to Salomon v Salomon and the exceptions.
### Corporate personality
On incorporation (s.9), a company becomes a body corporate, with:
- perpetual succession;
- a common seal (now optional);
- the power to hold property; and
- the capacity to sue and be sued.
### Salomon v A. Salomon & Co Ltd (1897, House of Lords)
Salomon transferred his boot business to a company in which he held almost all the shares, and took secured debentures. When the company was wound up, he was paid first as a secured creditor, ahead of the unsecured creditors. The House of Lords held that the company was a different person from its members. It was not Salomon's agent or trustee, and the debentures were valid.
### Consequences of separate personality
- limited liability;
- the company's property is not the members' property (*Macaura v Northern Assurance*, 1925: a shareholder had no insurable interest in company timber);
- a company can contract with its members (*Lee v Lee's Air Farming*, 1961);
- transferable shares; and
- the company's debts are not the members' debts.
### Exceptions: lifting the veil
**Judicial:**
- fraud;
- evasion of obligations (*Gilford Motor*);
- sham (*Jones v Lipman*);
- enemy character (*Daimler*);
- tax evasion;
- groups as single economic units, applied cautiously (*Adams v Cape Industries*, 1990);
- public interest.
- In India: *LIC v Escorts* (1986); *Skipper Construction* (1996); *Vodafone International v Union of India* (2012), on looking at the whole transaction; *Balwant Rai Saluja v Air India* (2014).
- *Prest v Petrodel Resources* (2013): the evasion versus concealment principle; the veil is lifted only as a last resort.
- Recent tax cases look at substance over form (*Tiger Global*, 2026).
**Statutory:**
- reduction of members below the statutory minimum (s.3A);
- misdescription of name;
- fraudulent conduct (s.339);
- prospectus misstatements; and
- liability of officers in default.
### Conclusion
*Salomon* remains the foundation of company law. The veil is lifted only where the corporate form is abused.

Q: Explain the duties of directors under the Companies Act, 2013.
### Position of directors
Directors are agents, trustees of the company's money and powers, and managing partners in a broader sense. They owe duties to the company, not to individual shareholders (*Percival v Wright*, 1902). Section 166 codifies these duties.
### Statutory duties (s.166)
1. Act in accordance with the articles.
2. Act in good faith to promote the objects of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole, and in the best interests of the company, its employees, shareholders, the community and the environment. This is the stakeholder model.
3. Exercise due and reasonable care, skill and diligence, and independent judgement.
4. Avoid situations of direct or indirect conflict of interest.
5. Not achieve or attempt any undue gain or advantage. Any gain must be paid to the company.
6. Not assign their office; any assignment is void.
The penalty for breach is ₹1 lakh to ₹5 lakh (s.166(7)).
### Other duties
- disclosure of interest (s.184);
- restrictions on loans to directors (s.185);
- related-party transactions, with approval (s.188);
- the directors' responsibility statement (s.134(5));
- the duty to attend meetings (s.167(1)(b): vacation of office for absence for 12 months);
- not to engage in insider trading (SEBI regulations); and
- duties of independent directors (Schedule IV).
### Liability
- breach of trust;
- misfeasance (s.340);
- fraud (s.447);
- disqualification (s.164); and
- personal liability under the IBC for fraudulent or wrongful trading (s.66 IBC).
### Judicial approach
- The business judgment rule: courts do not interfere with honest commercial decisions.
- Standards of care have increased: *Re City Equitable Fire Insurance* (1925) has given way to modern objective standards.
### Conclusion
Section 166 codifies the fiduciary duties and adds stakeholder interests, with stronger enforcement mechanisms.

Q: Discuss the remedies against oppression and mismanagement under the Companies Act, 2013.
### Rule in Foss v Harbottle (1843)
The proper plaintiff for a wrong to the company is the company itself, and the majority rules. The exceptions are:
- ultra vires or illegal acts;
- fraud on the minority;
- special majority requirements; and
- personal rights.
### Statutory protection (ss.241 to 246)
**Who may apply (s.244):**
- for a company with share capital: at least 100 members, or one-tenth of members, or holders of one-tenth of the issued share capital;
- for other companies: one-fifth of members.
The NCLT may waive these requirements. A person absent from the register may in some cases be treated as a member (*Dr Bais Surgical*, 2026).
**Grounds (s.241):**
- the affairs of the company are conducted in a manner prejudicial to public interest, or oppressive to members, or prejudicial to the company; or
- a material change in management or control is likely to cause prejudice.
**Meaning of oppression:** burdensome, harsh and wrongful conduct, a lack of probity and fair dealing (*Shanti Prasad Jain v Kalinga Tubes*, 1965; *Needle Industries v Needle Industries Newey*, 1981). Mere loss of confidence or a single act is not enough; there must be a continuous course of conduct.
**Powers of the NCLT (s.242):**
- regulate the conduct of affairs;
- purchase of shares;
- restrictions on transfer;
- termination or modification of agreements;
- removal of directors; and
- recovery of undue gains.
It may also pass interim orders.
### Leading case
*Tata Consultancy Services v Cyrus Investments* (2021): the removal of an executive chairman is not oppression. The NCLAT cannot reinstate a person. Just and equitable winding up must be justified.
### Other remedies
- class action (s.245);
- investigation (ss.210 to 213); and
- the government's power to apply (s.241(2)).
### Conclusion
Sections 241 to 246 give minority shareholders effective protection, balancing majority rule with fairness.

Q: Explain winding up under the Companies Act, 2013 and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016.
### Changes after the IBC
Since 2016, winding up for inability to pay debts is governed by the IBC. The Companies Act (ss.270 to 365) covers winding up by the Tribunal on other grounds.
### Winding up by the Tribunal (s.271, Companies Act)
- a special resolution;
- acts against sovereignty or security;
- fraudulent conduct of affairs;
- default in filing financial statements for five years; and
- just and equitable grounds (*Hind Overseas v Raghunath Prasad Jhunjhunwala*, 1976: deadlock).
A company liquidator is appointed, assets are realised, and distribution follows s.326 and s.53 IBC.
### Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (IBC)
- **Initiation:** by a financial creditor (s.7), an operational creditor (s.9, after a demand notice), or the corporate debtor (s.10). The minimum default is ₹1 crore.
- **Admission:** on proof of default (*Innoventive Industries v ICICI Bank*, 2017). The record of an information utility is sufficient (*Deepak Raheja v Omkara ARC*, 2026).
- **Moratorium (s.14):** protects only the corporate debtor, not guarantors (*Tejas Shah*, 2026).
- **Process:** an interim resolution professional, then the Committee of Creditors, then a resolution plan approved by 66%.
- **Commercial wisdom:** the CoC's commercial wisdom is paramount, with review limited to ss.30(2) and 61(3) (*Essar Steel v Satish Kumar Gupta*, 2019; *Torrent Power v Ashish Rathi*, 2026).
- **Time limit:** 180 days, extendable, up to 330 days.
- **Purpose:** the IBC aims at revival, and liquidation is a last resort (*Kalyani Transco v Bhushan Power and Steel*, 2025, which upheld JSW's plan). It is not a recovery tool.
### Liquidation (ss.33 to 54, IBC)
This follows if no plan is approved. Distribution follows the waterfall in s.53:
1. insolvency costs;
2. workmen's dues for 24 months and secured creditors;
3. employees' dues;
4. unsecured financial creditors;
5. government dues and remaining secured debt;
6. other debts; and
7. equity.
### Voluntary liquidation (s.59, IBC)
For solvent companies.
### Conclusion
The IBC has replaced the old, slow winding-up regime with a time-bound, creditor-driven process that favours revival.

PART: C | Problems | Answer any TWO of the following. | 2 × 10 = 20 marks

Q: M, a former employee of AB Ltd, agreed not to solicit its customers after leaving. He forms a company, M Ltd, owned by his wife, through which he solicits AB's customers. AB Ltd seeks an injunction against M and M Ltd. Decide.
**Issue:** Can the corporate veil of M Ltd be lifted?
**Law:** The court lifts the veil where a company is a "mere cloak or sham" to evade a legal obligation (*Gilford Motor Co v Horne*, 1933; *Jones v Lipman*, 1962). In India, the non-solicitation covenant is enforceable to the extent it protects trade secrets and customer connections without restraining trade (s.27 of the Contract Act; *Niranjan Shankar Golikari*, 1967).
**Application:** M Ltd was formed to evade M's covenant and is controlled by M.
**Conclusion:** The court will lift the veil and grant an injunction against both M and M Ltd, provided the covenant is a valid non-solicitation clause and not a general restraint.

Q: The secretary of X Ltd issues a share certificate to P by forging the signatures of two directors. P claims to be a shareholder relying on the doctrine of indoor management. Decide.
**Issue:** Does the doctrine of indoor management protect a holder of a forged document?
**Law:** *Turquand*'s rule protects outsiders against internal irregularities. Forgery is an exception: a forged document is a nullity and cannot bind the company (*Ruben v Great Fingall Consolidated*, 1906).
**Application:** The share certificate was forged. It is not a mere internal irregularity.
**Conclusion:** P is not a shareholder. He may sue the secretary for fraud, and possibly the company if the secretary acted within the scope of his employment.

Q: The majority shareholders of a private company, who are also directors, divert its business to another firm they own, and refuse to declare dividends for years. Minority shareholders holding 15% of the shares seek your advice.
**Issue:** Do the facts amount to oppression or mismanagement?
**Law:**
- Sections 241 and 242: conduct of affairs in a manner oppressive to members, or prejudicial to the company's interests.
- Diversion of business by those in control is a breach of fiduciary duty (s.166(4) and (5)), and a continuous course of conduct can be oppressive (*Needle Industries*, 1981).
- Members with one-tenth of the issued share capital may apply (s.244).
**Application:** Diverting business to the directors' own firm is a breach of duty and harms the minority. Withholding dividends while siphoning profits is unfair.
**Conclusion:** The minority (15%) may file a petition before the NCLT. They may seek:
- regulation of the company's affairs;
- recovery of diverted profits;
- removal of the directors; or
- a buy-out of their shares at fair value.
They may also bring a derivative claim for breach of duty.

Q: A bank files a section 7 IBC petition against a company. The CIRP is initiated, and the Committee of Creditors approves a resolution plan by 70%. A dissenting creditor asks the NCLT to reject the plan as commercially unfair. Decide.
**Issue:** Can the Adjudicating Authority interfere with the CoC's commercial decision?
**Law:**
- The CoC's commercial wisdom is non-justiciable. Review is limited to compliance with s.30(2) (payment of CIRP costs, liquidation value to dissenting creditors, and compliance with law) and s.61(3) (*K. Sashidhar v Indian Overseas Bank*, 2019; *Essar Steel*, 2019).
- This was reaffirmed in *Torrent Power v Ashish Rathi* (2026).
- Dissenting financial creditors must receive at least their liquidation value (s.30(2)(b)).
**Application:** The plan was approved by more than 66%. Unless it violates s.30(2) or the law, the NCLT cannot reject it as commercially unfair.
**Conclusion:** The objection fails if the dissenting creditor receives at least its liquidation value, and the NCLT should approve the plan.
