# 1 | Marriage, family and the applicability of personal laws
@topics Evolution of marriage and family; Role of religion, rituals and practices; Types of family by lineage, authority, residence and size; Applicability of law: who is a Hindu, a Muslim, a Christian; Sources of Hindu, Muslim and Christian law
>> India has no single family law. Which marriage, divorce and succession law governs a person depends on religion, so the first question in any family law problem is: who is a Hindu, a Muslim or a Christian?

## 1.1 Evolution of marriage and family
- Nineteenth-century anthropologists (Lewis Morgan, Bachofen) proposed that human society moved from promiscuity, to group marriage, to polygamy, to monogamy. Westermarck argued that pair-bonding and the family were present from early times. Modern scholars treat these as broad theories rather than a fixed sequence.
- Marriage regulates sexual relations, legitimacy of children, property transmission and mutual support.
- The family is the basic unit of socialisation, economic co-operation and care.

## 1.2 Role of religion, rituals and practices
| Tradition | Nature of marriage | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| Hindu | A sacrament (samskara), indissoluble, for performance of religious duties | Ceremonies such as saptapadi; earlier no divorce (except by custom), polygamy permitted; reformed by the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, which made it partly contractual |
| Muslim | A civil contract (nikah) with religious significance | Offer and acceptance, mahr (dower), witnesses (Sunni); dissolution permitted |
| Christian | A sacrament or covenant, monogamous, lifelong | Solemnised in church by a minister; divorce through courts under the Indian Divorce Act, 1869 |
| Parsi | Contract, monogamous | Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act, 1936 |
| Secular | Civil contract | Special Marriage Act, 1954, open to all religions |

CASE: Abdul Kadir v Salima (1886) :: Mahmood J of the Allahabad High Court described Muslim marriage as purely a civil contract, not a sacrament, and explained mahr as consideration for the contract. He also upheld the husband's right to restitution of conjugal rights subject to the wife's right to resist until prompt dower is paid.

## 1.3 Types of family
| Basis | Types |
|---|---|
| Lineage (descent) | Patrilineal (descent through the father; most Indian communities) and matrilineal (through the mother; the Nairs of Kerala under the Marumakkattayam law, the Khasis and Garos of Meghalaya) |
| Authority | Patriarchal (authority with the eldest male) and matriarchal (authority with women) |
| Residence after marriage | Patrilocal (wife lives with the husband's family) and matrilocal (husband lives with the wife's family) |
| Number of conjugal units | Nuclear (couple and unmarried children), extended, joint (several generations sharing property, worship and kitchen, as in the Hindu joint family) and composite |

## 1.4 Applicability of law
### 1.4.1 Who is a Hindu?
SEC: s.2(1), Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 (same in HSA, HAMA, HMGA) :: This Act applies (a) to any person who is a Hindu by religion in any of its forms or developments, including a Virashaiva, a Lingayat or a follower of the Brahmo, Prarthana or Arya Samaj; (b) to any person who is a Buddhist, Jaina or Sikh by religion; and (c) to any other person domiciled in the territories to which this Act extends who is not a Muslim, Christian, Parsi or Jew by religion, unless it is proved that such person would not have been governed by Hindu law or by any custom or usage as part of that law.
Explanation: a legitimate or illegitimate child, both or one of whose parents are Hindus (and who is brought up as such), and a convert or reconvert to Hinduism, are Hindus.
- s.2(2): nothing in the Act applies to members of Scheduled Tribes unless the Central Government notifies otherwise.
- So "Hindu" is defined broadly and partly negatively: it covers anyone not Muslim, Christian, Parsi or Jew, unless shown otherwise.

CASE: Sastri Yagnapurushadji v Muldas Bhudardas Vaishya (1966) :: The Satsangis (Swaminarayan sect) claimed they were not Hindus and so not covered by a temple-entry law. The Supreme Court held they were Hindus. Hinduism is not a single creed but a way of life; acceptance of the Vedas as the highest authority, recognition that the means to salvation are diverse, and the realisation that the number of gods is large are its broad features.

CASE: Perumal Nadar v Ponnuswami (1971) :: A person may be converted to Hinduism without formal ceremony of purification if he or she bona fide intends to profess Hinduism and is accepted by the community. A Christian woman who married a Hindu and lived as a Hindu was held to be a Hindu.

### 1.4.1.1 Who is a Muslim?
- A person who professes Islam, that is, believes in one God (Allah) and in Muhammad as His Prophet. No formal ceremony is required; a sincere declaration (kalima) suffices, and the courts do not enquire into the sincerity of belief unless conversion is a sham to evade law.
- A person born of Muslim parents is presumed Muslim until he renounces Islam.
- Muslim personal law applies by virtue of the **Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937**, which directs that in matters of marriage, dissolution, maintenance, dower, guardianship, gifts, trusts and succession (except agricultural land), the rule of decision shall be Muslim personal law (Shariat).

### 1.4.1.2 Who is a Christian?
- s.3 of the Indian Christian Marriage Act, 1872 and s.2(d) of the Indian Succession Act, 1925: an Indian Christian is a native of India who is, or in good faith claims to be, of unmixed Asiatic descent and who professes any form of the Christian religion. Baptism is generally treated as proof of conversion.

## 1.4.2 Sources of Hindu, Muslim and Christian law
### Hindu law
| Ancient sources | Modern sources |
|---|---|
| **Shruti** (that which is heard): the Vedas; little direct legal content | **Equity, justice and good conscience** where no rule exists |
| **Smriti** (that which is remembered): Dharmasutras and Dharmashastras; the Manusmriti, Yajnavalkya Smriti, Narada Smriti | **Judicial precedent** |
| **Commentaries and digests**: the Mitakshara (Vijnaneshwara's commentary on Yajnavalkya, 11th century), which governs most of India, and the Dayabhaga (Jimutavahana, 12th century), which governs Bengal and Assam | **Legislation**: the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, Hindu Succession Act, 1956, Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 |
| **Custom**: valid if ancient, certain, reasonable, continuous, and not opposed to public policy or law | |

CASE: Collector of Madura v Moottoo Ramalinga Sathupathy (1868) :: The Privy Council said that under the Hindu system, clear proof of usage will outweigh the written text of the law.

### Muslim law
- **Primary sources**: the **Quran** (word of God); the **Sunna** or **Hadith** (traditions of the Prophet: his sayings, acts and approvals); **Ijma** (consensus of jurists); **Qiyas** (analogical deduction).
- **Secondary sources**: custom (urf), judicial decisions, legislation (the Shariat Act, 1937; the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939; the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019), justice, equity and good conscience.
- **Schools**: Sunni (Hanafi, the majority in India; Maliki; Shafi'i; Hanbali) and Shia (Ithna Ashari, Ismaili, Zaidi).

### Christian law
Canon law and English ecclesiastical law historically; now statutes: the Indian Christian Marriage Act, 1872, the Indian Divorce Act, 1869 (amended substantially in 2001) and the Indian Succession Act, 1925.

# 2 | Customary practices and the State
@topics Polygamy; Concubinage; Child marriage; Sati; Dowry; State intervention through legal measures
>> Many traditional practices harmed women and children. The State has responded with criminal laws, civil invalidity, and courts reading constitutional values into personal law.

## 2.1 Polygamy
- **Hindus**: polygamy was permitted in classical law. The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 made monogamy compulsory: a marriage is void if either party has a spouse living at the time of the marriage (ss.5(i) and 11), and bigamy is punishable (s.17 HMA read with ss.82 BNS, earlier ss.494 and 495 IPC).
- **Muslims**: a man may have up to four wives at a time, provided he treats them equally (Quran, 4:3). A fifth marriage is irregular (fasid). A Muslim woman cannot have more than one husband (polyandry is void). Under the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939, s.2(viii)(f), a wife may seek divorce if the husband, having more than one wife, does not treat her equitably.
- **Christians and Parsis**: monogamy is compulsory; bigamous marriages are void.
- **Special Marriage Act, 1954**: monogamy is a condition (s.4(a)).
- **Uttarakhand Uniform Civil Code Act, 2024**: prohibits polygamy for all residents covered.

CASE: Sarla Mudgal v Union of India (1995) :: Hindu husbands converted to Islam to marry a second time without dissolving the first marriage. The Supreme Court held that the first marriage, under the HMA, is not dissolved by conversion; the second marriage is void and the husband is guilty of bigamy under s.494 IPC.

CASE: Lily Thomas v Union of India (2000) :: The Court reaffirmed Sarla Mudgal and held that conversion for the purpose of a second marriage is a fraud on the law; it does not violate the freedom of religion to prosecute such a husband for bigamy.

## 2.2 Concubinage
- Classical Hindu law recognised the concubine (avarudh stree) and gave her a right to maintenance from the estate of the man who kept her, if the relationship was continuous.
- Modern law does not recognise concubinage, but protects women in **live-in relationships** in the nature of marriage.

CASE: D. Velusamy v D. Patchaiammal (2010) :: A "relationship in the nature of marriage" under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 requires that the couple hold themselves out to society as akin to spouses, are of legal age to marry, are otherwise qualified to marry, and have voluntarily cohabited for a significant period. A "keep" maintained for sexual purposes or as a servant would not qualify.

CASE: Indra Sarma v V.K.V. Sarma (2013) :: A woman who knowingly lived with a married man could not claim that the relationship was "in the nature of marriage" under the DV Act; the Court listed guidelines (duration, shared household, pooling of resources, sexual relationship, children, socialisation in public, intention of the parties) and urged Parliament to protect such women.

## 2.3 Child marriage
- The **Child Marriage Restraint Act, 1929** (the Sarda Act) penalised child marriages but did not invalidate them.
- The **Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006** (PCMA): a "child" is a male below 21 or a female below 18 (s.2(a)).
  - A child marriage is **voidable** at the option of the party who was a child, by petition within two years of attaining majority (s.3).
  - It is **void** if the child is taken from a lawful guardian by enticement, force or deceit, or sold or trafficked for marriage (s.12), or solemnised in contravention of an injunction (s.14).
  - Punishments for an adult male marrying a child (s.9), for performing or abetting (s.10), and for promoting or permitting (s.11): up to two years and fine up to ₹1 lakh. Offences are cognizable and non-bailable (s.15).
  - Child Marriage Prohibition Officers (s.16) and courts may issue injunctions (s.13).
- Under the HMA, a marriage below the minimum age (s.5(iii)) is neither void nor voidable but is punishable (s.18).
- **Muslim law**: classical law allows marriage on attaining puberty (presumed at 15). Courts have differed on whether the PCMA overrides Muslim personal law; the question is pending before the Supreme Court.

CASE: Independent Thought v Union of India (2017) :: Sexual intercourse with a wife below eighteen was held to be rape, reading down the marital rape exception. The Court noted the harm of child marriages and urged their prevention.

CASE: Society for Enlightenment and Voluntary Action v Union of India (2024) :: The Supreme Court issued detailed guidelines for implementing the PCMA, including appointment of Child Marriage Prohibition Officers, special police units, community awareness and accountability of officials, and suggested that child betrothals be outlawed. It treated child marriage as a violation of the rights to free choice, autonomy and childhood.

## 2.4 Sati
- Sati is the burning or burying alive of a widow with the body of her deceased husband or relative.
- The **Bengal Sati Regulation, 1829** was enacted by Lord William Bentinck, following the campaign of Raja Ram Mohan Roy.
- After the death of Roop Kanwar at Deorala, Rajasthan, in 1987, Parliament enacted the **Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act, 1987**: attempt to commit sati (s.3), abetment of sati (s.4: death or life imprisonment) and glorification of sati (s.5) are offences; it also provides for removal of temples and structures built to glorify sati.

## 2.5 Dowry
- **Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961**: "dowry" means any property or valuable security given or agreed to be given, directly or indirectly, by one party to a marriage to the other, or by the parents or any other person, at or before or any time after the marriage, in connection with the marriage (s.2). Mahr under Muslim personal law is excluded.
  - Giving or taking dowry: not less than five years and fine not less than ₹15,000 or the value of the dowry, whichever is more (s.3).
  - Demanding dowry: six months to two years and fine up to ₹10,000 (s.4).
  - Dowry received by any person other than the woman must be transferred to her (s.6).
  - Presents given at the time of marriage without demand, entered in a list, are not dowry (s.3(2)).
- **Criminal law**: dowry death (s.80 BNS; s.304B IPC) and cruelty (ss.85 and 86 BNS; s.498A IPC), with a presumption as to dowry death (s.118 BSA).
- **Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005**: "domestic violence" includes harassment for dowry, with civil remedies such as protection, residence and monetary orders.

| Sati | Bengal Sati Regulation, 1829; Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act, 1987 |

# 3 | Conversion and its effect on family
@topics Effect of conversion on marriage, adoption, guardianship and succession
>> Conversion changes the personal law that governs a person, but it does not automatically dissolve existing marriages or wipe out rights already acquired by others.

## 3.1 Conversion and marriage
### Hindu law
- Conversion of a spouse does not by itself dissolve the marriage.
- If one spouse ceases to be a Hindu by conversion to another religion, the other spouse may seek divorce (s.13(1)(ii), HMA) or judicial separation. The converting spouse cannot seek divorce on this ground, since no one may take advantage of his own wrong.
- A converting spouse who remarries during the subsistence of the Hindu marriage commits bigamy (Sarla Mudgal, 1995; Lily Thomas, 2000).

### Muslim law
- Under classical Hanafi law, apostasy of either spouse dissolved the marriage at once.
- **Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939, s.4**: the renunciation of Islam by a married Muslim woman, or her conversion to a faith other than Islam, does not by itself dissolve her marriage. But a woman converted to Islam from another faith who re-embraces her former faith is not covered by this protection. She may still seek divorce on the grounds in s.2.
- Apostasy by a Muslim husband is still generally treated as dissolving the marriage.

### Christian law
- Under the Indian Divorce Act, 1869 (s.10(1)(ii), as amended in 2001), either spouse may seek divorce if the other has ceased to be a Christian by conversion to another religion.
- The **Native Converts' Marriage Dissolution Act, 1866** allowed a native convert to Christianity to seek dissolution if the non-converting spouse deserted or repudiated him or her because of the conversion.

### Special Marriage Act and Parsi law
- Under the SMA, the religion of the parties is irrelevant; conversion is not itself a ground, but the general grounds apply.
- Under the Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act, 1936 (s.32(j)), the other spouse ceasing to be a Parsi by conversion to another religion is a ground for divorce, subject to a time limit for filing.

## 3.2 Conversion and adoption
- Under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 (HAMA), only a Hindu can adopt and only a Hindu can be adopted (ss.7, 8, 10).
- A Hindu who converts cannot adopt under HAMA.
- If a Hindu parent converts, a child already adopted is not affected; the adoption is final (s.15: a valid adoption cannot be cancelled).
- Consent of a spouse who has ceased to be a Hindu is not needed for adoption by the other (s.7 proviso, s.9).
- Non-Hindus may adopt under the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, irrespective of religion (Shabnam Hashmi, Unit 6).

## 3.3 Conversion and guardianship
SEC: s.6 proviso, Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 :: No person shall be entitled to act as the natural guardian of a minor under this section if he has ceased to be a Hindu, or if he has completely and finally renounced the world by becoming a hermit (vanaprastha) or an ascetic (yati or sanyasi).
- But the welfare of the minor is the paramount consideration (s.13), so courts may still give custody to a converted parent if that serves the child's welfare.
- Under Muslim law, a mother who becomes an apostate may lose the right of hizanat (custody) in classical law, but courts apply the welfare principle under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890.

## 3.4 Conversion and succession
- **Caste Disabilities Removal Act, 1850** (Freedom of Religion Act): no law or usage shall forfeit or impair any right of property by reason of a person renouncing his religion or being excluded from caste. So a convert does not lose his inheritance rights.
SEC: s.26, Hindu Succession Act, 1956 :: Where, before or after the commencement of this Act, a Hindu has ceased or ceases to be a Hindu by conversion to another religion, children born to him or her after such conversion and their descendants shall be disqualified from inheriting the property of any of their Hindu relatives, unless such children or descendants are Hindus at the time when the succession opens.
- So the convert himself is not disqualified; only his post-conversion children and descendants are, unless they are Hindus when succession opens.
- On the death of a convert, succession to his property is governed by the law of his new religion (for example, a Hindu who converts to Christianity is governed by the Indian Succession Act, 1925).
- Under Muslim law, a non-Muslim cannot inherit from a Muslim; but the Caste Disabilities Removal Act protects a Muslim who converts out from losing rights he already has.

| Area | Hindu converting out | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Marriage | Other spouse may seek divorce (s.13(1)(ii)) | Marriage continues until decree |
| Adoption | Cannot adopt under HAMA | Prior adoption stands |
| Guardianship | Loses natural guardianship (s.6 proviso) | Welfare of child paramount |
| Succession | Convert keeps rights (1850 Act) | Post-conversion children disqualified unless Hindus (s.26 HSA) |

## 3.5 Recent judgments (2025–26)

CASE: Chinthada Anand v State of Andhra Pradesh (2026) :: A person who converts to Christianity loses Scheduled Caste status and cannot invoke the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act.

* Conversion to Christianity ends SC status (Chinthada Anand, 2026).
* Conversion does not dissolve a Hindu marriage; the non-converting spouse may sue for divorce (s.13(1)(ii)).
* A Muslim wife's apostasy does not dissolve her marriage (s.4 DMMA, 1939).
* Christians: conversion is a ground under s.10(1)(ii) Indian Divorce Act.
* A converted Hindu cannot adopt under HAMA or act as natural guardian (s.6 proviso HMGA).
* The convert keeps inheritance rights (Caste Disabilities Removal Act, 1850); his post-conversion descendants are disqualified from Hindu succession unless Hindus (s.26 HSA).

# 4 | Matrimonial remedies
@topics Non-judicial resolution: customary dissolution, unilateral divorce, mutual consent; Divorce under Muslim law: talaq and talaq-e-tafweez; Judicial resolution: fault theory and irretrievable breakdown; Nullity; Option of puberty; Restitution of conjugal rights; Judicial separation; Desertion; Cruelty; Adultery; Other grounds; Divorce by mutual consent under the SMA, HMA and Muslim law (khula and mubarat); Bars to matrimonial relief
>> Matrimonial law offers a ladder of remedies: restitution of conjugal rights to save the marriage, judicial separation to suspend it, nullity to declare it never valid, and divorce to end it.

## 4.1 Non-judicial resolution of marital conflict
### A. Customary dissolution among Hindus
Classical Hindu law did not allow divorce, but many communities (particularly among the so-called lower castes and in some regions) recognised divorce by custom: unilateral repudiation, mutual consent, or caste panchayat decisions. s.29(2) of the HMA saves any right recognised by custom or special enactment to obtain dissolution of a Hindu marriage. The custom must be proved as ancient, certain, reasonable and not opposed to public policy.

### B. Divorce under Muslim law
| Mode | Initiated by | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Talaq-e-ahsan** (most approved) | Husband | A single pronouncement during a tuhr (period between menstruations), followed by abstinence during the iddat; revocable during iddat |
| **Talaq-e-hasan** (approved) | Husband | Three pronouncements in three successive tuhrs; irrevocable on the third |
| **Talaq-ul-biddat** (triple or instant talaq) | Husband | Three pronouncements at one time, or a single irrevocable pronouncement; declared void and illegal |
| **Talaq-e-tafweez** (delegated divorce) | Wife, under power delegated by husband | Husband delegates his power of talaq to the wife (often in the nikahnama), conditionally or absolutely; she may then divorce herself; recognised as a protection for women |
| **Khula** | Wife | Divorce at the instance of the wife, who gives or agrees to give consideration (usually return of mahr) to the husband |
| **Mubarat** | Mutual | Both desire separation; offer may come from either side |
| **Ila** | Husband | Vow of abstinence for four months or more; now obsolete |
| **Zihar** | Husband | Husband compares wife to a woman within prohibited degrees; wife may seek judicial divorce unless he does penance |
| **Lian** | Wife | Husband falsely accuses wife of adultery; wife may sue for divorce |

CASE: Shamim Ara v State of Uttar Pradesh (2002) :: A mere plea in a written statement that talaq had been given earlier does not dissolve the marriage. Talaq must be pronounced, for a reasonable cause, and preceded by attempts at reconciliation by arbiters from both families.

CASE: Shayara Bano v Union of India (2017) :: A Constitution Bench (3:2) set aside talaq-ul-biddat (instant triple talaq). Nariman J (with Lalit J) held it manifestly arbitrary and violative of Art. 14, as it allows a marriage to be broken whimsically without any attempt at reconciliation; Kurian Joseph J held it was against the Quran and so not part of Shariat protected by the 1937 Act.

SEC: Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019 :: Any pronouncement of talaq by a Muslim husband upon his wife, by words spoken or written or in electronic form or in any other manner, which is talaq-e-biddat or any similar form having the effect of instantaneous and irrevocable divorce, is void and illegal (s.3). It is punishable with imprisonment up to three years and fine (s.4). The married Muslim woman is entitled to a subsistence allowance for herself and dependent children (s.5) and to custody of minor children (s.6). The offence is cognizable if information is given by the woman or her blood relatives, compoundable at her instance, and bail is granted only after hearing her (s.7).

## Judicial resolution under the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939
SEC: s.2, DMMA, 1939 :: A Muslim woman may obtain a decree for dissolution on any of these grounds: (i) whereabouts of the husband not known for four years; (ii) husband has neglected or failed to provide for her maintenance for two years; (iii) husband sentenced to imprisonment for seven years or more; (iv) husband failed without reasonable cause to perform his marital obligations for three years; (v) husband was impotent at the time of marriage and continues to be so; (vi) husband insane for two years, or suffering from leprosy or a virulent venereal disease; (vii) she, having been given in marriage by her father or guardian before fifteen, repudiated the marriage before eighteen, provided it was not consummated; (viii) cruelty, including habitual assault, associating with women of evil repute, forcing her to lead an immoral life, disposing of her property, obstructing her religious practice, and unequal treatment where he has more than one wife; (ix) any other ground recognised as valid under Muslim law.

## 4.2 Judicial resolution: fault theory and breakdown theory
| Theory | Basis | Examples in Indian law |
|---|---|---|
| **Fault (guilt) theory** | Divorce only if one spouse commits a matrimonial offence; the innocent spouse gets relief | Adultery, cruelty, desertion (s.13(1) HMA) |
| **Consent theory** | Both spouses agree that the marriage should end | s.13B HMA, s.28 SMA, s.10A Indian Divorce Act, khula and mubarat |
| **Breakdown theory** | Marriage has broken down irretrievably, whoever is at fault | s.13(1A) HMA (non-resumption of cohabitation for one year after a decree of judicial separation or restitution); proposed as a general ground |

- The **Law Commission's 71st Report (1978)** and **217th Report (2009)** recommended irretrievable breakdown as a ground; the Marriage Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2010 lapsed.

CASE: Naveen Kohli v Neelu Kohli (2006) :: The spouses had lived apart for many years with bitter litigation. The Court said that where a marriage has broken down irretrievably, keeping it alive serves no purpose, and recommended that Parliament add irretrievable breakdown as a ground.

CASE: Shilpa Sailesh v Varun Sreenivasan (2023) :: A Constitution Bench held that the Supreme Court can dissolve a marriage on the ground of irretrievable breakdown in exercise of its power under Art. 142 to do complete justice, even if one spouse opposes, after considering factors such as the period of separation, attempts at reconciliation, and the economic and social position of the parties. It can also waive the six-month period under s.13B.

## 4.3 Nullity of marriage
### Hindu Marriage Act
SEC: s.5, HMA :: A marriage may be solemnised between any two Hindus if: (i) neither party has a spouse living at the time of the marriage; (ii) neither party is incapable of giving valid consent due to unsoundness of mind, or though capable, suffers from mental disorder of such a kind or extent as to be unfit for marriage and procreation, or is subject to recurrent attacks of insanity; (iii) the bridegroom has completed 21 and the bride 18 years; (iv) the parties are not within the degrees of prohibited relationship, unless custom permits; (v) the parties are not sapindas of each other, unless custom permits.

| Void marriage (s.11) | Voidable marriage (s.12) |
|---|---|
| Contravenes s.5(i), (iv) or (v): bigamy, prohibited degrees, sapinda relationship | Not consummated owing to impotence of the respondent |
| Void ab initio; a decree of nullity is declaratory; either party may petition | Contravenes s.5(ii) (mental incapacity) |
| No legal status as husband and wife | Consent obtained by force or by fraud as to the nature of the ceremony or a material fact or circumstance concerning the respondent |
| Children are legitimate under s.16 | Respondent was pregnant by some person other than the petitioner at the time of marriage |
| | Valid until annulled; only the aggrieved party may petition, within time limits |

- Breach of the age condition (s.5(iii)) does not make the marriage void or voidable under the HMA; it is punishable under s.18 and governed by the PCMA.

CASE: Yuvraj Digvijay Singh v Yuvrani Pratap Kumari (1970) :: Impotence means physical or mental incapacity to consummate the marriage. The burden is on the petitioner. On the facts, the husband failed to prove the wife's impotence, and the petition failed.

### Muslim law
- **Batil (void)**: marriage with a woman within prohibited degrees by consanguinity, affinity or fosterage; marriage with another person's wife. No rights or obligations arise; children illegitimate.
- **Fasid (irregular)**: marriage without witnesses (Sunni law), with a fifth wife, with a woman undergoing iddat, with a non-kitabiya (for a man), or with two sisters together. It may be terminated by either party at any time; if consummated, the wife is entitled to dower and children are legitimate, but there is no inheritance between spouses.
- **Sahih (valid)**: all conditions met.

### Special Marriage Act and Christian law
- SMA: void marriages (s.24, non-fulfilment of conditions in s.4 or impotence of the respondent); voidable (s.25: wilful refusal to consummate, pregnancy by another, consent obtained by coercion or fraud).
- Indian Divorce Act: nullity on grounds of impotence, prohibited degrees, lunacy, or a former spouse living (s.19).

## 4.4 Option of puberty
- **HMA s.13(2)(iv)**: a wife may seek divorce if her marriage (whether consummated or not) was solemnised before she attained fifteen, and she repudiated it after attaining fifteen but before eighteen.
- **DMMA s.2(vii)**: a Muslim woman given in marriage by her father or guardian before fifteen may repudiate it before eighteen, provided it has not been consummated.
- **PCMA s.3**: a child marriage is voidable at the option of the contracting party who was a child (male or female), by petition within two years of attaining majority.

## 4.5 Restitution of conjugal rights
SEC: s.9, HMA :: When either the husband or the wife has, without reasonable excuse, withdrawn from the society of the other, the aggrieved party may apply by petition to the district court for restitution of conjugal rights, and the court, on being satisfied of the truth of the statements and that there is no legal ground why the application should not be granted, may decree restitution. Explanation: where a question arises whether there has been reasonable excuse for withdrawal, the burden of proving reasonable excuse is on the person who has withdrawn.
- Also s.22 SMA; s.32 Indian Divorce Act; available under Muslim law as a suit (Abdul Kadir v Salima).
- Non-compliance with an RCR decree for one year is a ground for divorce for either party (s.13(1A)(ii)); the decree can be enforced by attachment of property (Order XXI Rule 32 CPC), not by physical compulsion.

CASE: T. Sareetha v T. Venkata Subbaiah (1983) :: The Andhra Pradesh High Court (Choudary J) struck down s.9 as violating Arts. 14 and 21, holding that compelling a woman to have sexual relations against her will violates her privacy and dignity.

CASE: Harvinder Kaur v Harmander Singh Choudhry (1984) :: The Delhi High Court disagreed with Sareetha, holding that RCR aims at cohabitation and consortium, not merely sexual intercourse, and that introducing constitutional law into the home is like introducing a bull into a china shop.

CASE: Saroj Rani v Sudarshan Kumar Chadha (1984) :: The Supreme Court upheld s.9, holding that it serves a social purpose as an aid to preventing break-up of marriage. A fresh challenge to s.9 based on the right to privacy (Puttaswamy) is pending before the Supreme Court.

## 4.6 Judicial separation
SEC: s.10, HMA :: Either party may petition for judicial separation on any ground specified in s.13(1), and the wife also on any ground in s.13(2). Where a decree is passed, it is no longer obligatory for the petitioner to cohabit with the respondent; the court may rescind the decree if it considers it just and reasonable.
- The marriage subsists; neither spouse can remarry.
- Non-resumption of cohabitation for one year after the decree is a ground for divorce for either party (s.13(1A)(i)).

## 4.7 Desertion
SEC: s.13(1)(ib) and Explanation, HMA :: The respondent has deserted the petitioner for a continuous period of not less than two years immediately preceding the presentation of the petition. "Desertion" means desertion of the petitioner by the other party without reasonable cause and without the consent or against the wish of such party, and includes the wilful neglect of the petitioner by the other party.

Essentials for the deserting spouse:
1. **Factum of separation**: actual cessation of cohabitation.
2. **Animus deserendi**: intention to bring cohabitation permanently to an end.
For the deserted spouse:
3. Absence of consent.
4. Absence of conduct giving reasonable cause to leave.

- **Constructive desertion**: the spouse who stays at home drives the other out by conduct; the one who causes the separation is the deserter.

CASE: Bipinchandra Jaisinghbhai Shah v Prabhavati (1957) :: The wife left for her parents' home and did not return despite the husband's attempts. The Supreme Court explained the essentials (factum and animus for the deserter; absence of consent and reasonable cause for the deserted). On the facts, the husband failed to prove animus deserendi throughout the statutory period, since he had not made sincere efforts to bring her back.

CASE: Savitri Pandey v Prem Chandra Pandey (2002) :: Desertion means the intentional permanent forsaking and abandonment of one spouse by the other without consent and without reasonable cause; it is a total repudiation of marital obligations.

## 4.8 Cruelty
SEC: s.13(1)(ia), HMA :: The respondent has, after the solemnisation of the marriage, treated the petitioner with cruelty.
- The Act does not define cruelty. It may be physical or mental. Before 1976, cruelty was only a ground for judicial separation, and the conduct had to cause reasonable apprehension of harm. Since 1976, it is a ground for divorce, without that qualification.

CASE: N.G. Dastane v S. Dastane (1975) :: The standard of proof in matrimonial cases is preponderance of probabilities, not proof beyond reasonable doubt. Cruelty means conduct causing a reasonable apprehension in the petitioner's mind that it would be harmful or injurious to live with the respondent. On the facts, the wife's behaviour amounted to cruelty, but the husband had condoned it.

CASE: V. Bhagat v D. Bhagat (1994) :: Mental cruelty is conduct that inflicts such mental pain and suffering on the other spouse as would make it impossible to live together. Making false and baseless allegations of insanity and adultery against the spouse in pleadings is mental cruelty.

CASE: Samar Ghosh v Jaya Ghosh (2007) :: The Court gave an illustrative list of instances of mental cruelty, including acute mental pain making living together impossible, sustained reprehensible conduct, unilateral refusal of sexual intercourse for a considerable period without justification, unilateral decision not to have children, and a long period of separation showing that the marriage has broken beyond repair.

CASE: Narendra v K. Meena (2016) :: The wife's persistent effort to compel the husband to separate from his aged parents, and her repeated threats and an attempt to commit suicide, were held to amount to mental cruelty.

## 4.9 Adultery
- s.13(1)(i), HMA: the respondent has, after solemnisation of the marriage, had voluntary sexual intercourse with any person other than his or her spouse. Since 1976, a single act suffices; earlier, "living in adultery" was required.
- Adultery is ordinarily proved by circumstantial evidence, on a preponderance of probabilities.
- In **Joseph Shine v Union of India (2018)**, s.497 IPC (criminal adultery) was struck down, but the Court said adultery remains a civil wrong and a ground for divorce.

## 4.11 Divorce by mutual consent
| Law | Provision | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Hindu Marriage Act | s.13B | Living separately for one year or more; unable to live together; mutually agreed; second motion not earlier than six months and not later than eighteen months after the first |
| Special Marriage Act | s.28 | Same structure (one year apart, six to eighteen months) |
| Indian Divorce Act | s.10A (added 2001) | Living separately for two years or more |
| Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act | s.32B | Living separately for one year or more |
| Muslim law | Khula and mubarat | By agreement, without court, though courts adjudicate disputes |

CASE: Sureshta Devi v Om Prakash (1991) :: Mutual consent must continue until the decree is passed; either party may withdraw consent before the second motion, and the court cannot pass a decree on the basis of the initial joint petition alone.

CASE: Amardeep Singh v Harveen Kaur (2017) :: The six-month waiting period under s.13B(2) is directory, not mandatory. The court may waive it if the parties have been separated for more than eighteen months before the first motion, mediation and reconciliation efforts have failed, the parties have settled all issues, and the waiting period would only prolong their agony.

## 4.12 Bars to matrimonial relief: s.23 HMA
SEC: s.23(1), HMA :: In any proceeding under this Act, whether defended or not, if the court is satisfied that (a) any of the grounds for relief exists and the petitioner is not in any way taking advantage of his or her own wrong or disability; (b) where the ground is adultery, the petitioner has not been accessory to, connived at or condoned the act, and where the ground is cruelty, the petitioner has not condoned it; (bb) where divorce is sought by mutual consent, such consent has not been obtained by force, fraud or undue influence; (c) the petition is not presented or prosecuted in collusion with the respondent; (d) there has not been any unnecessary or improper delay; and (e) there is no other legal ground why relief should not be granted, then the court shall decree such relief accordingly.
- s.23(2): before granting relief, the court must make every endeavour to bring about reconciliation (except for certain grounds such as conversion, insanity, venereal disease, renunciation and presumption of death).

| Bar | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 4.12.1 Strict proof | The court must be "satisfied"; Dastane held the standard is preponderance of probabilities |
| 4.12.2 Taking advantage of own wrong or disability | A spouse cannot rely on his own misconduct (for example, the deserter cannot seek divorce for desertion) |
| 4.12.3 Accessory | Petitioner actively participated in or facilitated the adultery |
| 4.12.4 Connivance | Petitioner knowingly permitted or encouraged the adultery |
| 4.12.5 Collusion | An agreement between the parties to fabricate or suppress evidence to obtain a decree |
| 4.12.6 Condonation | Forgiveness with restoration of the offending spouse to the original position, usually shown by resumed cohabitation; a later matrimonial offence revives the condoned one (Dastane) |
| 4.12.7 Improper or unnecessary delay | Unexplained delay suggesting the petitioner was not seriously aggrieved |
| 4.12.8 Residuary clause | Any other legal ground for refusing relief |

TIP: Learn the HMA by structure: s.5 (conditions), s.9 (RCR), s.10 (JS), s.11 (void), s.12 (voidable), s.13 (divorce grounds), s.13B (mutual consent), s.23 (bars), ss.24 and 25 (maintenance). Then add the SMA and Muslim law equivalents.

# 5 | Alimony and maintenance
@topics Maintenance of neglected wives, divorced wives, children and parents under the Code of Criminal Procedure (now BNSS); Alimony and maintenance as an independent remedy under personal laws; Maintenance pendente lite and permanent alimony as ancillary relief; Maintenance of divorced Muslim women under the 1986 Act
>> Maintenance prevents destitution. It is available as a quick secular remedy under the criminal procedure code, as an independent right under personal laws, and as ancillary relief in matrimonial cases.

## 5.1 Maintenance under s.144 BNSS (s.125 CrPC)
SEC: s.144(1), Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 :: If any person having sufficient means neglects or refuses to maintain (a) his wife, unable to maintain herself; or (b) his legitimate or illegitimate minor child, whether married or not, unable to maintain itself; or (c) his legitimate or illegitimate child (not being a married daughter) who has attained majority, where such child is, by reason of any physical or mental abnormality or injury, unable to maintain itself; or (d) his father or mother, unable to maintain himself or herself, a Magistrate of the first class may, on proof of such neglect or refusal, order such person to make a monthly allowance for their maintenance, at such monthly rate as the Magistrate thinks fit.
- "Wife" includes a woman who has been divorced by, or has obtained a divorce from, her husband and has not remarried.
- A minor married daughter may claim from her father until marriage, if her husband lacks sufficient means.
- Interim maintenance and expenses of proceedings may be ordered; there is no statutory ceiling (the ₹500 limit was removed in 2001).
- The wife is not entitled if she is living in adultery, refuses without sufficient reason to live with her husband, or they are living separately by mutual consent (s.144(4)).
- It is secular: it applies to all persons irrespective of religion.

CASE: Chaturbhuj v Sita Bai (2008) :: The object of maintenance proceedings is not to punish a person for past neglect, but to prevent vagrancy and destitution of a deserted wife by providing her food, clothing and shelter by a speedy remedy.

CASE: Mohd. Ahmed Khan v Shah Bano Begum (1985) :: A Constitution Bench held that s.125 CrPC applies to all, including Muslims, and a divorced Muslim wife unable to maintain herself is entitled to maintenance beyond the iddat period; the payment of mahr is not a sum payable on divorce that would exempt the husband. The Court also called for a Uniform Civil Code.

## 5.4 The Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986
Enacted after Shah Bano.
- s.3(1)(a): a divorced woman is entitled to "a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance to be made and paid to her within the iddat period by her former husband".
- s.3(1)(b): maintenance for children for two years from birth if she maintains them.
- s.3(1)(c) and (d): mahr and properties given to her at or after marriage.
- s.4: if she cannot maintain herself after iddat, the magistrate may order her relatives who would inherit her property, or the State Wakf Board, to pay maintenance.
- s.5: the parties may opt, by declaration, to be governed by s.125 CrPC.

CASE: Danial Latifi v Union of India (2001) :: A Constitution Bench upheld the 1986 Act but interpreted it to protect women: the husband must make a reasonable and fair provision for the future of the divorced wife, including maintenance, within the iddat period. This provision is not limited to the iddat period and must cover her whole life until she remarries. So read, the Act does not violate Arts. 14, 15 and 21.

CASE: Mohd. Abdul Samad v State of Telangana (2024) :: The Supreme Court held that a divorced Muslim woman can claim maintenance under s.125 CrPC (now s.144 BNSS); the 1986 Act does not exclude the secular remedy. The two remedies are parallel, and the 1986 Act is an additional, not substitute, remedy.

### Critical review of the 1986 Act
- It was criticised as a legislative reversal of Shah Bano that left divorced Muslim women worse off than others.
- Danial Latifi's interpretation (lifetime provision within iddat) and Abdul Samad's holding (s.125 still available) have largely restored parity.
- Practical difficulties remain in enforcing orders against relatives and Wakf Boards.

## 5.2 Maintenance as an independent remedy under personal laws
### Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956
- **Wife** (s.18): a Hindu wife is entitled to be maintained by her husband during her lifetime. She may live separately without forfeiting maintenance if the husband is guilty of desertion, cruelty, has another wife living, keeps a concubine, has converted, or for any other justifiable cause. She is not entitled if she is unchaste or ceases to be a Hindu.
- **Widowed daughter-in-law** (s.19): from her father-in-law, to the extent of coparcenary property, if she cannot maintain herself from her own or her husband's or children's estate.
- **Children and aged or infirm parents** (s.20): a Hindu is bound to maintain his or her legitimate or illegitimate children (until majority for sons; for unmarried daughters, until marriage) and aged or infirm parents (including a childless stepmother).
- **Dependants of a deceased Hindu** (ss.21 and 22): heirs must maintain the dependants out of the estate inherited.
- **Amount** (s.23): position and status of the parties, reasonable wants, whether living separately with justification, the claimant's own means, the number of claimants.

### Muslim law
- During marriage, the husband must maintain his wife (nafaqa) if she is not disobedient (nashiza) and the marriage is consummated or she is willing to cohabit.
- After divorce, maintenance during iddat under personal law, plus the 1986 Act.
- Children: the father is bound to maintain sons until puberty and daughters until marriage.

### Other laws
- The **Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007** allows parents and senior citizens to claim maintenance from children and relatives before a Tribunal (up to ₹10,000 per month under the original Act).
- The **Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005** provides monetary relief (s.20).

# 6 | The child and the family
@topics Legitimacy; Adoption; Custody, maintenance and education; Guardianship
>> Family law treats the welfare of the child as paramount: in deciding legitimacy, adoption, custody and guardianship, the child's interest prevails over the claims of parents.

## 6.1 Legitimacy
### Presumption of legitimacy
SEC: s.116, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (s.112 Evidence Act) :: The fact that any person was born during the continuance of a valid marriage between his mother and any man, or within two hundred and eighty days after its dissolution, the mother remaining unmarried, shall be conclusive proof that he is the legitimate son of that man, unless it can be shown that the parties to the marriage had no access to each other at any time when he could have been begotten.

CASE: Goutam Kundu v State of West Bengal (1993) :: Courts cannot order blood tests (or DNA tests) as a matter of course to settle paternity; a strong prima facie case of non-access is needed, and the presumption of legitimacy should not be lightly disturbed.

CASE: Nandlal Wasudeo Badwaik v Lata Nandlal Badwaik (2014) :: Where a DNA test, which is scientifically accurate, shows that the husband is not the father, the DNA result will prevail over the presumption under s.112, since the presumption was enacted when such tests were not available.

### Legitimacy of children of void and voidable marriages
SEC: s.16, HMA :: Notwithstanding that a marriage is null and void under s.11, any child of such marriage who would have been legitimate if the marriage had been valid shall be legitimate; a child of a voidable marriage annulled under s.12 shall be deemed legitimate; but such a child shall not have any rights in the property of any person other than the parents.

CASE: Revanasiddappa v Mallikarjun (2023) :: A three-judge bench held that children of void or voidable marriages, legitimate under s.16, are entitled to a share in their parent's property, including the share that would fall to the parent in coparcenary property on a notional partition immediately before the parent's death. They are not coparceners by birth in the Hindu joint family.

### Muslim law: acknowledgement of paternity
Where paternity is uncertain, a Muslim father may acknowledge a child as his legitimate child (iqrar), if: the ages of the parties allow the relationship; the child is not known to be the offspring of another; the child is not the offspring of zina (adultery or fornication); the acknowledger intends to confer legitimacy, not merely sonship; and the child, if adult, does not repudiate it. Acknowledgement only raises a presumption of a lawful marriage; it cannot legitimise a child where the marriage is shown to be impossible.

CASE: Habibur Rahman Chowdhury v Altaf Ali Chowdhury (1922) :: The Privy Council held that acknowledgement can only establish legitimacy where the paternity is uncertain; it cannot legitimise a child who is proved to be illegitimate.

## 6.2 Adoption
### Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956
| Requirement | Section |
|---|---|
| Valid adoption: by a person capable of adopting and having the right; the person giving has capacity; the person adopted is capable of being adopted; in compliance with the Act | s.6 |
| A male Hindu of sound mind and full age may adopt; if he has a living wife, her consent is needed unless she has renounced the world, ceased to be a Hindu, or been declared of unsound mind | s.7 |
| A female Hindu of sound mind and full age may adopt; if married, she needs her husband's consent, unless he has renounced the world, ceased to be a Hindu or been declared of unsound mind (as amended in 2010) | s.8 |
| Persons capable of giving: father, mother, or guardian (with court permission) | s.9 |
| Person who may be adopted: a Hindu, not already adopted, not married (unless custom permits), not above fifteen (unless custom permits) | s.10 |
| Conditions: no living Hindu son (or grandson or great-grandson) when adopting a son, no living daughter or son's daughter when adopting a daughter; age difference of at least 21 years when adopting a child of the opposite sex; the same child cannot be adopted by two persons; actual giving and taking with intent to transfer | s.11 |
| Effects: the child becomes the child of the adoptive parents for all purposes from the date of adoption; ties with the birth family are severed, except for prohibited degrees of marriage and property already vested | s.12 |
| A valid adoption cannot be cancelled | s.15 |

### Adoption under the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015
- Provides a secular route: any prospective adoptive parent, irrespective of religion, may adopt an orphan, abandoned or surrendered child (ss.56 to 73), under the Adoption Regulations framed by the Central Adoption Resource Authority (CARA), currently the Adoption Regulations, 2022.
- Since a 2021 amendment, the District Magistrate (not a court) issues adoption orders.
- Single persons may adopt; a single male cannot adopt a girl child.

CASE: Shabnam Hashmi v Union of India (2014) :: The Court held that the JJ Act is an enabling, secular law; any person, including a Muslim, may adopt under it irrespective of personal law. But the right to adopt is not a fundamental right under Art. 21, given diverse personal law beliefs.

- **Muslims, Christians, Parsis and Jews** have no law of adoption in their personal laws; they may take a child in guardianship under the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, or adopt under the JJ Act.

## 6.3 Custody, maintenance and education
- Custody is decided on the welfare principle: the welfare of the child is the paramount consideration (s.13, HMGA; s.17, Guardians and Wards Act, 1890; s.26, HMA allows orders on custody, maintenance and education in matrimonial proceedings).
- **HMGA s.6(a)**: the custody of a minor who has not completed five years shall ordinarily be with the mother.
- **Muslim law (hizanat)**: the mother is entitled to custody of a son until seven and a daughter until puberty (Hanafi law); after that, the father. The right is lost on remarriage to a stranger or immoral conduct. Courts now decide by the child's welfare.

CASE: Gaurav Nagpal v Sumedha Nagpal (2009) :: In custody matters, the welfare of the child is the paramount and controlling consideration, overriding the legal rights of parents; the child's comfort, health, education, moral upbringing and intellectual development, and the child's own preferences if old enough, must be considered.

CASE: Roxann Sharma v Arun Sharma (2015) :: The custody of a child below five should ordinarily be with the mother under s.6(a) HMGA; the father must show that the mother's custody would harm the child.

- Maintenance of children: s.20 HAMA; s.144 BNSS; ancillary orders under s.26 HMA.
- Education: Art. 21A (free and compulsory education for 6 to 14), Art. 51A(k), and the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009.

## 6.4 Guardianship
### Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956
- **Kinds of guardians**: natural guardian (s.6), testamentary guardian appointed by will (s.9), guardian appointed or declared by court (under the Guardians and Wards Act), and de facto guardian (who cannot deal with the minor's property, s.11).
SEC: s.6, HMGA :: The natural guardians of a Hindu minor, in respect of the minor's person and property (excluding his undivided interest in joint family property), are: (a) in the case of a boy or an unmarried girl, the father, and after him, the mother; provided that the custody of a minor who has not completed five years shall ordinarily be with the mother; (b) in the case of an illegitimate boy or unmarried girl, the mother, and after her, the father; (c) in the case of a married girl, the husband.

CASE: Githa Hariharan v Reserve Bank of India (1999) :: The RBI refused to accept an application signed by the mother as guardian of her minor son. The Court held that the word "after" in s.6(a) does not mean "after the lifetime of the father" but "in the absence of the father", which includes his indifference or inability to look after the minor. So read, the provision does not discriminate against mothers, and the mother can act as natural guardian even during the father's lifetime.

- **Powers of a natural guardian** (s.8): may do all acts necessary or reasonable for the benefit of the minor or the realisation, protection or benefit of the minor's estate; but cannot, without the court's permission, mortgage, charge, sell, gift, exchange or otherwise transfer immovable property, or lease it for more than five years or beyond one year after the minor attains majority. A transfer in breach is voidable at the instance of the minor.
- **Welfare paramount** (s.13).

### Muslim law
- **Guardianship of the person** (for custody, see hizanat).
- **Guardianship of property**: the father; the executor appointed by the father's will; the paternal grandfather; the executor of the paternal grandfather. The mother is not a natural guardian of property, though she may be appointed by the court.

TIP: Guardianship and custody are separate: guardianship is about legal authority over person and property, custody is about physical care. In every custody question, start and end with "welfare of the child is paramount".

## 6.5 Recent judgments (2025–26)

CASE: R. Rajendran v Kamar Nisha (2025) :: The presumption of legitimacy for a child born during a valid marriage (s.112 IEA, now s.116 BSA) is strong. A DNA test should be ordered only in cases of eminent necessity.

* DNA tests against presumption of legitimacy only on eminent necessity (R. Rajendran, 2025).
* Birth during a valid marriage is conclusive proof of legitimacy (s.116 BSA), subject to non-access; DNA may prevail (Nandlal Badwaik).
* Children of void and voidable marriages are legitimate (s.16 HMA) and share in parents' property including notional coparcenary share (Revanasiddappa).
* HAMA ss.6 to 12 govern Hindu adoption; JJ Act provides a secular route (Shabnam Hashmi).
* Custody: welfare paramount (Gaurav Nagpal); child under five ordinarily with mother.
* Natural guardian: father, and "after" him (in his absence) mother (Githa Hariharan); court permission needed to transfer immovable property (s.8).

# 7 | Family and its changing patterns
@topics Attenuation of family ties; Working women and their impact on spousal relationship, composition of family, status and role of women, decision-making; Factors affecting the family: demographic, environmental, religious, legislative; Processes of social change: Sanskritisation, Westernisation, secularisation, universalisation, parochialisation, modernisation, industrialisation and urbanisation
>> The Indian family is moving from large joint households with male authority towards smaller, more equal units, under pressure from economic change, migration, education and law.

## 7.1 New emerging trends
### 7.1.1 Attenuation of family ties
- Decline of the joint family and rise of nuclear households, especially in cities.
- Migration for work weakens daily contact with kin; elderly parents increasingly live alone, which led to the Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007.
- New forms: single-parent families, live-in relationships, childless couples, and families formed by adoption or assisted reproduction (Assisted Reproductive Technology (Regulation) Act, 2021; Surrogacy (Regulation) Act, 2021).

### 7.1.2 Working women and the spousal relationship
| Area | Change |
|---|---|
| Spousal relationship | More companionate and egalitarian; also more conflict over roles and housework |
| Composition of family | Fewer children, later marriage, need for childcare from grandparents or crèches |
| Status and role of women | Economic independence, greater say in marriage and fertility decisions, but "double burden" of paid and domestic work |
| Decision-making | Shift from patriarchal authority to joint decision-making, especially on finances and children's education |
Laws supporting working women: Maternity Benefit (Amendment) Act, 2017 (26 weeks' leave, now under the Code on Social Security, 2020); the POSH Act, 2013; equal remuneration provisions now in the Code on Wages, 2019.

## 7.2 Factors affecting the family
- **Demographic**: falling fertility, rising life expectancy, ageing population, adverse sex ratio, later age at marriage.
- **Environmental**: displacement by development projects and disasters, rural to urban migration, housing constraints.
- **Religious**: weakening of ritual prescriptions, inter-religious marriages under the SMA, conversion disputes.
- **Legislative**: the Hindu Code Bills of 1955 to 1956, the Special Marriage Act, the Dowry Prohibition Act, the Domestic Violence Act, the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 making daughters coparceners (Vineeta Sharma v Rakesh Sharma, 2020), and the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019.

CASE: Supriyo v Union of India (2023) :: A Constitution Bench held unanimously that there is no fundamental right to marry and that the Court cannot read the Special Marriage Act to include same-sex marriages; it is for Parliament to decide. It recognised that queer persons have the right to choose a partner and to cohabit, and the Union agreed to set up a committee to examine the rights and entitlements of queer couples.

## 7.3 Processes of social change in India
| Process | Meaning | Effect on family |
|---|---|---|
| **Sanskritisation** (M.N. Srinivas) | Lower castes adopting customs, rituals and lifestyle of upper castes to raise status | Adoption of restrictions such as prohibition of widow remarriage, dowry practices |
| **Westernisation** (M.N. Srinivas) | Changes brought by British rule and Western contact: technology, institutions, ideology, values | Nuclear families, individualism, romantic marriage, women's education |
| **Secularisation** | Declining influence of religion on social life; rational explanation replacing ritual | Civil marriage, divorce acceptance, inter-caste marriage |
| **Universalisation and parochialisation** (McKim Marriott) | Universalisation: local traditions rising to all-India (great tradition); parochialisation: great tradition elements absorbed into local practice | Spread of common marriage rituals; local adaptation of codified law |
| **Modernisation** | Rationality, scientific temper, universal values, achievement over ascription | Equality in law, egalitarian family ideals |
| **Industrialisation** | Shift from agricultural to factory and service work | Separation of home and workplace, decline of the family as a production unit |
| **Urbanisation** | Growth of cities and urban ways of life | Smaller households, anonymity, dual-income families |

TIP: Link each social process to a concrete legal change. For example: industrialisation and urbanisation led to nuclear families, which in turn created the need for the Senior Citizens Act, 2007 and maintenance remedies for parents.

* Joint families are weakening; nuclear and new family forms are rising.
* Working women change spousal roles and decision-making; laws on maternity and harassment support them.
* Demographic, environmental, religious and legislative factors drive change.
* Sanskritisation and Westernisation (Srinivas), universalisation and parochialisation (Marriott), modernisation, industrialisation and urbanisation explain the transformation.
* No fundamental right to marriage; same-sex marriage left to Parliament (Supriyo, 2023).

# 8 | Establishment of Family Courts
@topics Family Courts Act, 1984: objects, establishment, jurisdiction, procedure, appeals
>> Family Courts were created to settle family disputes quickly, by conciliation where possible, in a less adversarial setting than ordinary civil courts.

## Background and objects
- The Law Commission's 59th Report (1974) recommended a different approach to family disputes, and women's organisations pressed for special courts.
- The **Family Courts Act, 1984** aims to promote conciliation and secure speedy settlement of disputes relating to marriage and family affairs.

## Establishment: s.3
- The State Government, after consulting the High Court, shall establish a Family Court for every area in the State comprising a city or town whose population exceeds one million, and may establish Family Courts for other areas.
- Judges (s.4): persons with at least seven years' experience as judicial officers, or members of tribunals, or advocates with seven years' practice, or persons with special qualifications; preference to women and to persons committed to protecting the institution of marriage and promoting the welfare of children.

## Jurisdiction: s.7
A Family Court exercises the jurisdiction of a district court or subordinate civil court in suits and proceedings of the following nature (Explanation to s.7(1)):
1. a suit or proceeding between the parties to a marriage for a decree of nullity, restitution of conjugal rights, judicial separation or dissolution of marriage;
2. a suit or proceeding for a declaration as to the validity of a marriage or the matrimonial status of any person;
3. a suit or proceeding between the parties to a marriage with respect to their property;
4. a suit or proceeding for an order or injunction in circumstances arising out of a marital relationship;
5. a suit or proceeding for a declaration as to the legitimacy of any person;
6. a suit or proceeding for maintenance;
7. a suit or proceeding in relation to the guardianship of the person or custody of, or access to, any minor.
It also exercises the jurisdiction of a magistrate under Chapter IX of the CrPC (now the corresponding chapter of the BNSS, maintenance under s.144) (s.7(2)). Civil courts are excluded from these matters where a Family Court exists (s.8).

## Procedure
- **Duty to promote settlement** (s.9): the Family Court must in the first instance endeavour to assist and persuade the parties to arrive at a settlement, and may adjourn proceedings for this purpose.
- **Procedure** (s.10): the CPC applies generally, but the court may lay down its own procedure to arrive at a settlement or at the truth.
- **Proceedings in camera** (s.11): if the court so desires, and must be if either party so desires.
- **Assistance of medical and welfare experts** (s.12), including counsellors, preferably women.
- **Right to legal representation** (s.13): no party is entitled as of right to be represented by a legal practitioner; but the court may seek the assistance of a legal expert as amicus curiae if it considers it necessary in the interest of justice. In practice, courts generally permit lawyers.
- **Relaxed rules of evidence** (s.14): the court may receive any report, statement, document or information which in its opinion assists it to deal effectively with the dispute, whether or not otherwise admissible.
- **Record of oral evidence** (s.15): only a memorandum of the substance of evidence need be recorded.
- **Judgment** (s.16): concise statement of the case, the point for determination, the decision and reasons.

## Appeals: s.19
- An appeal lies to the High Court from every judgment or order (not interlocutory) of a Family Court, on facts and law, within thirty days.
- No appeal lies from a decree or order passed with the consent of the parties, or from an order under the maintenance chapter of the BNSS (but revision lies to the High Court).
- A Bench of two or more judges of the High Court hears appeals.

## Assessment
- Strengths: specialised forum, conciliation, counsellors, in-camera hearings, less formality.
- Weaknesses: not established in all districts, shortage of counsellors, lawyers' dominance in practice, long pendency, inconsistent use of conciliation.

TIP: Memorise s.7 jurisdiction (seven heads plus maintenance) and the distinctive procedure (ss.9, 11, 12, 13, 14). These are favourite short-note topics.

* Family Courts Act, 1984: for speedy settlement and conciliation.
* Mandatory for cities with population over one million (s.3).
* Jurisdiction (s.7): matrimonial reliefs, validity of marriage, matrimonial property, injunctions, legitimacy, maintenance, guardianship and custody; plus maintenance under the BNSS.
* Conciliation first (s.9); in camera (s.11); no lawyer as of right (s.13); relaxed evidence (s.14).
* Appeal to the High Court within 30 days (s.19); no appeal from consent orders.

# 9 | Securing a Uniform Civil Code
@topics Religious pluralism and its implications; Connotations of the directive in Article 44; Impediments to the formulation of a Uniform Civil Code
>> Article 44 directs the State to endeavour to secure a uniform civil code. After seventy-five years, Goa and Uttarakhand have one; the rest of India still has religion-based personal laws.

SEC: Art. 44 :: The State shall endeavour to secure for the citizens a uniform civil code throughout the territory of India.

## 9.1 Religious pluralism and its implications
- India has many religions and, within each, many schools, sects and customs. Personal laws on marriage, divorce, maintenance, adoption, guardianship and succession differ by religion.
- Arts. 25 and 26 protect freedom of religion, subject to public order, morality, health and other Fundamental Rights; Art. 25(2)(b) allows the State to make laws for social welfare and reform. Personal laws are not wholly immune from Part III (Shayara Bano).
- Arts. 29 and 30 protect minority cultural rights; the Sixth Schedule and Arts. 371A (Nagaland) and 371G (Mizoram) protect tribal customary law.
- The result is legal pluralism: equality between women of different religions is uneven, and conflict-of-laws problems arise in inter-religious marriages and conversions.

## 9.2 Connotations of the directive in Article 44
### Constituent Assembly debates
- Supporters (Dr B.R. Ambedkar, K.M. Munshi, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar) argued that a uniform code would promote national unity, gender justice and secularism; Ambedkar said that personal laws need not be religion's preserve and the State may legislate.
- Opponents (Mohammad Ismail, Naziruddin Ahmad, B. Pocker Sahib) wanted personal laws of communities protected, or at least changes only with community consent.
- The compromise: a non-justiciable Directive Principle, using the word "endeavour".

### Judicial observations
CASE: Mohd. Ahmed Khan v Shah Bano Begum (1985) :: The Court regretted that Art. 44 had remained a dead letter and said that a common civil code would help national integration by removing disparate loyalties to laws with conflicting ideologies.

CASE: Sarla Mudgal v Union of India (1995) :: The Court asked the government to take a fresh look at Art. 44, noting that the Hindu code reforms showed that personal law could be codified; but it did not and could not direct legislation.

CASE: John Vallamattom v Union of India (2003) :: Striking down s.118 of the Indian Succession Act (which restricted Christians from making charitable bequests), the Court regretted that Parliament had not yet enacted a uniform civil code.

CASE: Jose Paulo Coutinho v Maria Luiza Valentina Pereira (2019) :: The Court cited Goa as a "shining example" of a uniform civil code applicable to all, irrespective of religion (subject to limited exceptions), and noted that no attempt had been made to frame a code for the whole country.

### Meaning of "uniform"
- A single code replacing all personal laws, or
- common principles of equality applied across personal laws (uniformity of rights rather than identical rules), or
- an optional secular code (as under the Special Marriage Act and Indian Succession Act) available to all.
The **21st Law Commission (2018)** consultation paper said a uniform civil code was "neither necessary nor desirable at this stage" and recommended instead reforming each personal law to remove discrimination (for example, a uniform age of marriage, making mahr and maintenance fairer, codifying divorce). The **22nd Law Commission** invited public views afresh in 2023.

### Existing examples
- **Goa**: the Portuguese Civil Code of 1867, continued after liberation, applies to all residents in matters of marriage, divorce and succession, with community of property between spouses and compulsory registration of marriage.
- **Uttarakhand**: the **Uniform Civil Code, Uttarakhand Act, 2024** came into force on 27 January 2025. It applies to all residents except members of Scheduled Tribes; it prohibits polygamy and halala, prescribes uniform grounds of divorce and a uniform marriage age (21 for men and 18 for women), gives equal inheritance rights to sons and daughters, and requires registration of marriages and of live-in relationships.
- **Secular statutes** already apply across religions: the Special Marriage Act, 1954; the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961; s.144 BNSS; the JJ Act, 2015 (adoption); the PCMA, 2006; the DV Act, 2005.

## 9.3 Impediments to a Uniform Civil Code
- **Religious sensitivity and minority apprehension**: fear that "uniform" will mean imposition of majority norms.
- **Diversity of customs** even within the majority community (matrilineal systems, regional customs, tribal laws).
- **Constitutional protections** for tribal customary law (Sixth Schedule, Arts. 371A, 371G) and freedom of religion.
- **Lack of political consensus** and the risk of communal polarisation.
- **Absence of a draft**: no comprehensive model code has been widely discussed at the national level.
- **Federal questions**: personal law is in the Concurrent List (Entry 5, List III), so Parliament and States can both legislate, raising the possibility of different "uniform" codes in different States.

### Ways forward suggested
- Reform within each personal law to remove gender discrimination.
- Gradual, consensual codification, starting with common subjects (registration of marriage, age of marriage, maintenance).
- Strengthening optional secular laws.
- Wide consultation with communities, women's groups and legal experts.

Present both views; examiners reward balance.

* Art. 44 is a non-justiciable directive to "endeavour" to secure a uniform civil code.
* Religious pluralism is protected by Arts. 25 to 30 and tribal provisions, but personal laws are subject to Part III.
* Courts have urged a UCC (Shah Bano, Sarla Mudgal, John Vallamattom) and praised Goa (Jose Paulo Coutinho).

* Impediments: minority fears, diversity, constitutional protections, political consensus, federal structure.
