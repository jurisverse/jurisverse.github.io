Unit 1

Nature and development of banking

Banking in India moved from indigenous bankers to presidency banks, then to the Reserve Bank and a regulated commercial banking system. Banking and currency are Union subjects, but money-lending is a State subject.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • History and evolution of banking in India
  • Constitutional perspectives: Union List entries 36, 37, 38, 43, 44, 45 and 46
  • State List entry 30

1.1 History and evolution

  • Indigenous bankers: shroffs, seths, chettiars and mahajans financed trade with hundis (indigenous bills of exchange).
  • Agency houses and early banks: the Bank of Hindostan (1770); the presidency banks of Bengal (1806), Bombay (1840) and Madras (1843), merged into the Imperial Bank of India (1921), which became the State Bank of India (1955).
  • Swadeshi banks: Punjab National Bank (1894), Bank of India (1906), Canara Bank, Indian Bank and others.
  • Reserve Bank of India (1935, under the RBI Act, 1934), nationalised in 1949.
  • Banking Companies Act, 1949 (renamed the Banking Regulation Act in 1966).
  • Nationalisation of 14 banks (1969) and 6 more (1980); entry of new private banks (1993 onwards); consolidation of public sector banks (2017 to 2020), leaving 12 public sector banks.
  • Digital banking: UPI (2016), payments banks, small finance banks, and the digital rupee (e₹, pilot from 2022).

Points to remember

  • Banking is a Union subject (Entries 43 to 46 and 36 to 38 of List I); money-lending is in List II, entry 30.
  • Parliament can legislate on cooperative banks' banking activities (Pandurang Chaugule).

Unit 2

Relationship of banker and customer

The relationship between banker and customer is primarily that of debtor and creditor, overlaid with special duties: to honour cheques, keep secrecy and follow the customer's mandate.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Banker, banking business, customer, types of accounts
  • Contract between banker and customer
  • Banker's lien
  • Banking instruments

2.1 Banker, banking and customer

  • Banking (s.5(b), BR Act): accepting deposits of money from the public for lending or investment, repayable on demand or otherwise, and withdrawable by cheque, draft, order or otherwise.
  • Banking company (s.5(c)): any company that transacts the business of banking in India.
  • Customer: a person who has an account with the bank; duration is not essential, but there must be some recognisable course of dealing in the nature of banking business (Commissioners of Taxation v English, Scottish and Australian Bank, 1920).
  • Types of accounts: savings, current, fixed and recurring deposit, joint accounts (either or survivor, former or survivor), accounts of minors, partnerships, companies, trusts, and NRI accounts (NRE, NRO, FCNR(B)).

2.2 Contract between banker and customer

  • General relationship: debtor and creditor; the banker can use the money as he likes and need not account for its use (Foley v Hill, 1848). The customer must make a demand to recover the money (Joachimson v Swiss Bank Corporation, 1921).
  • Other relationships: trustee (money deposited for a specific purpose), agent (collecting cheques, paying bills), bailee (safe deposit), and lessor and lessee (lockers).
  • Banker's duties: to honour cheques when funds are available (wrongful dishonour gives rise to damages, especially for a trader); to maintain secrecy of the customer's affairs, subject to exceptions; to follow the mandate; to give notice before closing an account.
CaseTournier v National Provincial and Union Bank of England (1924)

The banker's duty of secrecy is a legal duty arising from contract, but it is not absolute. There are four exceptions: disclosure under compulsion of law; a duty to the public; where the bank's interests require disclosure; and disclosure with the customer's express or implied consent.

  • In India, the duty is also affected by statutes (Income-tax Act, PMLA, Bankers' Books Evidence Act, Credit Information Companies Act, and RBI KYC directions), and by the right to privacy (Puttaswamy, 2017).
  • Banker's rights: general lien, set-off, appropriation of payments, and charging interest and commission.
  • Appropriation of payments: the debtor may appropriate; failing him, the creditor; failing both, the rule in Clayton's case (1816): first in, first out, in a running account (ss.59 to 61 Contract Act).

2.3 Banker's lien

  • A banker has a general lien on all goods and securities bailed to him as banker, in the absence of a contract to the contrary (s.171 Contract Act). It is an implied pledge: the banker can sell after reasonable notice.
  • No lien on articles in safe custody, documents deposited for a specific purpose, or money held in trust.
CaseSyndicate Bank v Vijay Kumar (1992)

The Supreme Court held that a banker's general lien under s.171 is more than a mere right to retain; it is an implied pledge. The bank could retain fixed deposit receipts deposited as security for a guarantee and apply them towards other debts of the customer, since there was no contract to the contrary.

2.4 Banking instruments

  • Bank notes: promissory notes issued by the RBI (s.26 RBI Act); legal tender.
  • Banker's draft (demand draft): an order by one branch on another to pay; treated as a bill of exchange (s.85A NI Act).
  • Deposit receipts: acknowledgment of a fixed deposit; not negotiable.
  • Letters of credit: an undertaking by the issuing bank to pay the beneficiary against conforming documents; governed by UCP 600 (2007); autonomous from the underlying contract, except for fraud (U.P. State Sugar Corporation v Sumac International, 1997).
  • Bank guarantees and indemnities: an independent undertaking enforceable according to its terms.
  • Travellers' cheques, postal orders, dividend warrants (treated as cheques) and bonds.

2.5 Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseCanara Bank v Kavita Chowdhary (2026)

A collecting bank that delays presenting a customer's cheque is liable for deficiency in service.

Points to remember

  • Collecting bank's delay is deficiency in service (2026).
  • Banking (s.5(b)) and banking company (s.5(c)); customer; types of accounts.
  • Debtor-creditor (Foley v Hill), demand needed (Joachimson); trustee, agent and bailee roles.
  • Duty of secrecy with exceptions (Tournier); statutory limits.
  • Appropriation; Clayton's case.
  • General lien is an implied pledge (s.171; Syndicate Bank v Vijay Kumar).
  • Instruments: notes, drafts, deposit receipts, letters of credit (UCP 600; Sumac), guarantees.

Unit 3

Law relating to banking companies in India

The Banking Regulation Act (originally the Banking Companies Act, 1949) is the main law regulating banks. It controls entry, business, management, capital and exit, with the RBI as regulator.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Banking Companies Act, 1949: extent and application, business of banking companies, control and management by the RBI, suspension of business and winding up, speedy disposal of winding up

3.1 Extent and application

  • The Act applies to banking companies, and with modifications to corresponding new banks (nationalised banks), SBI and its subsidiaries (to the extent specified), regional rural banks and cooperative banks (Part V, s.56).
  • Renamed the Banking Regulation Act in 1966 when extended to cooperative banks.

3.2 Business of banking companies

  • Permitted business (s.6): borrowing, lending, discounting, issuing letters of credit, collecting, acting as agents, guarantees, safe deposit, and other forms of business listed.
  • Prohibited business (s.8): trading in goods, except in connection with realisation of security.
  • Restrictions: on holding immovable property (s.9), on loans to directors and their concerns (s.20), on removal of records, and on payment of dividends (s.15).
  • Capital and reserves (ss.11 to 17): minimum paid-up capital; transfer of 20% of profits to a reserve fund (s.17).
  • Cash reserve (s.18) and statutory liquidity ratio (s.24).

3.3 Control and management by the RBI

  • Licensing (s.22): no company may carry on banking business without an RBI licence.
  • Management (s.10A and s.10B): a majority of the board must have special knowledge in specified fields; chairman and whole-time directors need RBI approval (s.35B).
  • Power to issue directions (s.35A), inspection (s.35), control over advances (s.21), removal of managerial persons (s.36AA), supersession of the board (s.36ACA), and approval of amalgamations (s.44A).
  • Section 35AA (2017): the Central Government may authorise the RBI to direct banks to initiate insolvency proceedings against defaulters under the IBC.
  • Banking Laws (Amendment) Act, 2025: raised the "substantial interest" threshold to ₹2 crore, extended the tenure of directors in cooperative banks (other than chairman and whole-time directors) from 8 to 10 years, and allowed up to four nominees for deposits and lockers (simultaneous or successive).

3.4 Suspension of business and winding up

  • Moratorium and reconstruction (s.45): on the RBI's application, the Central Government may impose a moratorium on a banking company and the RBI may prepare a scheme of reconstruction or amalgamation, as in the case of YES Bank (2020) and Lakshmi Vilas Bank's amalgamation with DBS Bank India (2020).
  • Winding up (Part III, ss.38 to 45): only by the High Court, on the RBI's application in specified cases (inability to pay debts, conduct detrimental to depositors, or failure to comply with the Act).

3.5 Speedy disposal of winding up proceedings (Part IIIA, ss.45A to 45X)

  • The High Court has exclusive jurisdiction to decide all claims; the RBI may be appointed official liquidator; preferential payments to depositors; summary procedures; and time limits.
  • Deposit insurance: the DICGC Act, 1961 (amended 2021) insures deposits up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank, payable within 90 days of a moratorium.

Points to remember

  • The BR Act (1949, renamed 1966) applies to banking companies and, with modifications, to cooperative banks.
  • Business (s.6), prohibitions (s.8), capital and reserves, CRR (s.18) and SLR (s.24).
  • RBI control: licensing (s.22), directions (s.35A), management, IBC referral (s.35AA); 2025 amendments.
  • Moratorium and reconstruction (s.45: YES Bank); winding up by the High Court; speedy disposal (Part IIIA); deposit insurance of ₹5 lakh.

Unit 5

Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934; cooperative banking

The RBI is India's central bank: it issues currency, conducts monetary policy, acts as banker to the government and the banks, regulates and supervises the financial system, and manages foreign exchange.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Characteristics, functions, objectives, legal status and organisation of the RBI
  • Banking, currency, banker to government, exchange control, non-banking companies and supervision (ss.17, 18 and 42)
  • Cooperative banking law

5.1 Legal status and organisation

  • Established on 1 April 1935 under the RBI Act, 1934; nationalised from 1 January 1949. It is a body corporate with perpetual succession.
  • Central Board of Directors (s.8): Governor, up to four Deputy Governors, directors nominated by the Central Government, and government officials. Local Boards for four regions.
  • The Central Government may give directions to the RBI in the public interest, after consulting the Governor (s.7).

5.2 Objectives and functions

  • Preamble: to regulate the issue of bank notes, keep reserves to secure monetary stability, operate the currency and credit system to its advantage, and (since 2016) to operate a modern monetary policy framework to meet the challenge of an increasingly complex economy.
  • Monetary policy: the Monetary Policy Committee (ss.45ZA to 45ZL, inserted 2016) of six members (three from the RBI and three appointed by the Government) sets the policy repo rate to achieve the inflation target set by the Central Government (CPI inflation of 4%, within a band of 2% to 6%).
  • Currency: sole right to issue bank notes (s.22); notes are legal tender (s.26). The Central Government may declare series of notes to cease to be legal tender (s.26(2)).
CaseVivek Narayan Sharma v Union of India (2023)

A Constitution Bench (4:1) upheld the November 2016 demonetisation of ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes. It held that the power under s.26(2) is not limited to "any series" in a narrow sense, that the decision-making process was not flawed because the RBI had been consulted, and that the measure was proportionate to its aims. The dissent held that the initiative came from the Government, so it should have been done by legislation.

  • Banker to government (ss.20, 21 and 21A): manages the public debt and accounts of the Centre and the States; ways and means advances.
  • Banker's bank (ss.17, 18 and 42): s.17 lists the business the RBI may transact (accepting deposits, purchase and rediscount of bills, loans and advances to banks and others); s.18 permits direct discount and loans in special circumstances for regulating credit; s.42 requires scheduled banks to maintain a cash reserve ratio with the RBI. The RBI is the lender of last resort.
  • Foreign exchange: custodian of reserves; administers FEMA (s.10 FEMA, authorised persons).
  • Non-banking financial companies (Chapter IIIB, ss.45H to 45QB): registration (s.45-IA), net owned funds, reserve fund (s.45-IC), prudential directions and powers to remove directors or supersede boards (s.45-IE).
  • Payment systems: under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007 (UPI, NEFT, RTGS).
  • Supervision: inspection of banks (s.35 BR Act), the Board for Financial Supervision, risk-based supervision, and prompt corrective action.

5.3 Cooperative banking law

  • The Cooperative Credit Societies Act, 1904 introduced cooperatives for rural credit; the Cooperative Societies Act, 1912 extended them; each State now has its own Cooperative Societies Act (Andhra Pradesh Cooperative Societies Act, 1964), and the Multi-State Cooperative Societies Act, 2002 (amended 2023) applies to multi-State societies.
  • Cooperative banks: State cooperative banks, district central cooperative banks and urban cooperative banks. Primary agricultural credit societies are not banks.
  • The Banking Regulation (Cooperative Societies) Rules, 1966 and Part V of the BR Act regulate cooperative banks; the 2020 amendment expanded RBI control.
  • The 97th Constitutional Amendment (2011) inserted Part IXB on cooperative societies. In Union of India v Rajendra N. Shah (2021), the Supreme Court struck down Part IXB in so far as it relates to State cooperative societies (for want of ratification by half the States), but upheld it for multi-State societies.

Points to remember

  • The RBI (1935, nationalised 1949): Central Board; government directions (s.7).
  • Monetary policy through the MPC (4% CPI target, 2% to 6% band).
  • Currency issue (s.22), legal tender (s.26); demonetisation upheld (Vivek Narayan Sharma).
  • Banker to government; banker's bank (ss.17, 18 and 42); lender of last resort.
  • Foreign exchange, NBFC regulation (Chapter IIIB), payment systems, supervision.
  • Cooperative banking: 1904 and 1912 Acts, State and multi-State Acts, the 1966 Rules, the 2020 amendment, the 97th Amendment (Rajendra N. Shah).

Unit 6

Nationalisation of banks

In 1969, 14 major banks were nationalised to direct credit to priority sectors. The first law was struck down; the second was enacted with changes, and a further 6 banks were nationalised in 1980.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Acts, 1969 and 1970
  • Legislative competence for nationalisation
  • Banks before and after nationalisation

6.1 The Acts of 1969 and 1970

  • On 19 July 1969, the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance nationalised the 14 largest banks (deposits above ₹50 crore), replaced by the Act of 1969.
CaseRustom Cavasjee Cooper v Union of India (Bank Nationalisation case) (1970)

An eleven-judge bench struck down the 1969 Act (10:1). The Act was discriminatory under Art. 14, because it prohibited the nationalised banks from carrying on banking business while other banks could. The compensation principles under Art. 31(2) were not relevant to the value of the property acquired, so the "compensation" was illusory. The Court also rejected the earlier view (A.K. Gopalan) that each fundamental right must be read in isolation, holding that Arts. 19 and 31 are not mutually exclusive.

  • Parliament then enacted the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1970, with new compensation provisions and without the bar on the former banks' business. The 25th Amendment (1971) replaced "compensation" with "amount" in Art. 31(2).
  • Six more banks were nationalised by the Act of 1980.

6.2 Legislative competence

  • Entries 43, 44 and 45 of List I (banking corporations and banking); Entry 42 of List III (acquisition and requisitioning of property); Entries 7 and 52 of List I (industries declared by Parliament); Entries 24, 26 and 27 of List II (industries, trade and commerce, production and distribution within the State); Entry 33 of List III (trade and commerce in products of controlled industries); and Entries 54 and 56 of List I.
  • Constitutional provisions then in force: Art. 19(1)(f) and 19(5) (property and reasonable restrictions), and Art. 31 (compensation), both now deleted by the 44th Amendment.

6.3 Banks before and after nationalisation

  • Before: banks were controlled by industrial houses, lending concentrated in large industry and trade, few rural branches, and frequent bank failures.
  • After: rapid branch expansion in rural areas (the lead bank scheme), priority sector lending targets (40% of adjusted net bank credit), deposit mobilisation, and financial inclusion (later, Jan Dhan accounts from 2014).
  • Problems: lower efficiency and profitability, political interference in lending, rising non-performing assets (NPAs), frauds (for example, the Punjab National Bank–Nirav Modi fraud of 2018), and bank robberies and cyber frauds.
  • Responses: the Narasimham Committee reforms, recapitalisation, the IBC (2016), the asset quality review (2015), consolidation of PSBs (2017 to 2020), the National Asset Reconstruction Company (2021), and the Financial Services Institutions Bureau (2022) for appointments.

Points to remember

  • 14 banks nationalised in 1969; R.C. Cooper struck down the 1969 Act (Art. 14 discrimination; illusory compensation; Arts. 19 and 31 not exclusive).
  • The 1970 Act; 6 more banks in 1980; 25th Amendment.
  • Legislative competence under Lists I, II and III.
  • Impact: branch expansion and priority lending; problems of NPAs and frauds; reforms and consolidation.

Unit 7

Foreign exchange control and banking for non-residents

FERA treated foreign exchange violations as crimes in a period of scarcity. FEMA (1999) replaced it with a civil framework focused on facilitating trade and payments and managing the foreign exchange market.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • FERA, 1973: authorised dealers
  • restrictions on dealing and payments (ss.3 to 10, 13 to 17, 19, 22 to 27)
  • Banking for non-resident Indians

7.1 FERA, 1973

  • Enacted to conserve foreign exchange and regulate dealings in it; in force from 1 January 1974.
  • Authorised dealers (s.6): only authorised dealers (banks licensed by the RBI) could deal in foreign exchange; money changers (s.7).
  • Restrictions (s.8): no person other than an authorised dealer could buy, borrow, sell, lend or exchange foreign exchange without RBI permission. Payments (s.9): restrictions on payments to non-residents. Restrictions on import and export of currency and bullion (s.13), on securities (s.19), on holding assets and acquiring immovable property abroad and taking up employment abroad, and on companies with non-resident interest (s.29).
  • Contraventions were criminal offences with presumption of culpable mental state and reverse burden; arrest without warrant; heavy penalties.

7.2 Replacement by FEMA, 1999

  • FEMA (in force from 1 June 2000) aims to facilitate external trade and payments and promote the orderly development of the foreign exchange market.
  • Capital account (s.6) and current account (s.5) transactions: current account transactions are free except as restricted by the Government; capital account transactions are regulated by the RBI (debt) and the Government (non-debt instruments, since 2019).
  • Authorised persons (s.10) replace authorised dealers.
  • Contraventions are civil: penalty up to three times the sum involved, or ₹2 lakh where not quantifiable (s.13); adjudication by the Adjudicating Authority, appeal to the Appellate Tribunal and the High Court; compounding (s.15). Detention only for failure to pay penalty.
  • Serious cases of illegal foreign exchange dealings now fall under the PMLA, 2002 and the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018.

7.3 Banking for non-residents

  • NRE accounts: rupee accounts for foreign earnings; repatriable; interest tax-free in India.
  • NRO accounts: rupee accounts for Indian income; repatriation within limits (up to USD 1 million a year after taxes).
  • FCNR(B) accounts: term deposits in foreign currency; protected from exchange rate risk.
  • The Liberalised Remittance Scheme allows resident individuals to remit up to USD 2,50,000 a year for permitted purposes.

Points to remember

  • FERA (1973, in force 1974): authorised dealers; strict restrictions; criminal offences.
  • FEMA (1999, in force 1 June 2000): facilitation; current and capital account; civil penalties; compounding.
  • PMLA and the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act deal with serious cases.
  • NRI banking: NRE, NRO, FCNR(B); LRS.

Unit 8

Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881

A negotiable instrument passes from hand to hand like money, and a holder in due course takes it free from defects in the title of prior parties. The NI Act defines the instruments, the rights and liabilities of parties, and the criminal offence of cheque dishonour.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Meaning and kinds of negotiable instruments: promissory notes, bills of exchange, cheques
  • Holder and holder in due course
  • Parties
  • Negotiation
  • Presentment
  • Discharge
  • Dishonour, noting and protest
  • Payment for honour
  • Crossing of cheques
  • Pledge of stocks, shares, life policies and documents of title
  • Guarantee and hypothecation

8.1 Meaning and kinds

  • Negotiable instrument (s.13): a promissory note, bill of exchange or cheque payable to order or bearer.
  • Promissory note (s.4): an unconditional written undertaking, signed by the maker, to pay a certain sum of money to, or to the order of, a certain person, or to the bearer (bank notes excepted).
  • Bill of exchange (s.5): an unconditional written order, signed by the maker, directing a certain person to pay a certain sum of money to, or to the order of, a certain person or the bearer.
  • Cheque (s.6): a bill of exchange drawn on a specified banker, payable on demand. It includes the electronic image of a truncated cheque and a cheque in electronic form.
  • Instruments by custom: hundis.

8.2 Holder, holder in due course and parties

  • Holder (s.8): a person entitled in his own name to the possession of the instrument and to receive or recover the amount due.
  • Holder in due course (s.9): a holder who, for consideration, became the possessor before maturity, without sufficient cause to believe that any defect existed in the title of the person from whom he derived title. A holder in due course takes free from defects of title and from equities (s.53), and every prior party is liable to him until the instrument is satisfied (s.36). Some defences remain good against him: forgery, material alteration, and minority.
  • Parties: drawer, drawee, payee, maker, acceptor, indorser and indorsee. Liability: maker of a note and acceptor of a bill (s.32); drawer (s.30); indorser (s.35).
  • Presumptions (s.118): consideration, date, time of acceptance, transfer before maturity, and holder in due course.

8.3 Negotiation, presentment and discharge

  • Negotiation (s.14): transfer so as to constitute the transferee a holder. By delivery for bearer instruments (s.47); by indorsement and delivery for order instruments (s.48). Kinds of indorsement: blank, special, restrictive, conditional, and sans recourse.
  • Presentment: for acceptance (s.61, bills payable after sight) and for payment (s.64), at the proper place and time; excused in certain cases (s.76).
  • Discharge: by payment in due course (s.10, s.82), cancellation, release, material alteration (s.87), and other modes.

8.4 Dishonour, noting, protest and payment for honour

  • Dishonour by non-acceptance (s.91) or non-payment (s.92). Notice of dishonour must be given to prior parties to charge them (s.93).
  • Noting (s.99): a notary public records the fact of dishonour on the instrument. Protest (s.100): a formal certificate of dishonour by a notary. Protest is compulsory for foreign bills (s.104).
  • Acceptance and payment for honour (ss.108 to 116): a stranger may accept or pay for the honour of a party, acquiring the rights of the holder against that party.

8.5 Cheques: crossing and protection of bankers

  • Crossing (ss.123 to 131): general crossing (two parallel transverse lines, with or without "and company"), special crossing (the name of a banker), and "not negotiable" crossing (s.130: the holder cannot give a better title than he has). "Account payee" is a direction to the collecting banker by banking practice.
  • Paying banker's protection (s.85 and s.128): payment in due course of a cheque purporting to be indorsed by the payee.
  • Collecting banker's protection (s.131): a banker who in good faith and without negligence receives payment for a customer of a crossed cheque is not liable to the true owner if the customer's title is defective.

8.6 Dishonour of cheques: s.138

  • Offence: dishonour of a cheque for insufficiency of funds or because it exceeds the arranged amount, for the discharge of a legally enforceable debt or liability. Conditions: presentation within the cheque's period of validity (three months under RBI directions); a written demand notice within 30 days of receiving information of dishonour; failure to pay within 15 days of receiving the notice. Punishment: imprisonment up to two years, or fine up to twice the amount, or both.
  • Presumption (s.139): the holder is presumed to have received the cheque for the discharge of a debt or liability, unless the contrary is proved.
  • Companies (s.141): every person in charge of and responsible for the business of the company is liable; non-executive directors are not liable without specific averments.
  • Jurisdiction (s.142(2), 2015 amendment): the court where the payee's bank branch is situated (account payee cheques).
  • Interim compensation up to 20% of the amount (s.143A, 2018) and deposit of 20% in appeal (s.148). Offences are compoundable (s.147).
CaseRangappa v Sri Mohan (2010)

A three-judge bench held that the presumption under s.139 includes the existence of a legally enforceable debt or liability. It is rebuttable; the accused may rebut it on a preponderance of probabilities, by his own evidence or by reference to the complainant's evidence, and need not enter the witness box.

CaseIn re Expeditious Trial of Cases under Section 138 of NI Act (2021)

A Constitution Bench gave directions to speed up s.138 cases: summary trials, treating the inquiry under s.202 CrPC as possible by affidavits, a single trial for multiple cheques arising from one transaction within twelve months, and asked Parliament to consider special courts and pre-litigation mediation.

8.7 Pledge of stocks, shares, life policies and documents of title; guarantee and hypothecation

  • Pledge (s.172 Contract Act): bailment of goods as security for a debt; possession passes to the pledgee. Shares are pledged through depositories (Depositories Act, 1996); life policies by assignment under s.38 of the Insurance Act, 1938; documents of title to goods (bills of lading, warehouse receipts) by indorsement and delivery.
  • Hypothecation: a charge on movable property without transfer of possession (used for vehicles and stock); defined in s.2(1)(n) of the SARFAESI Act.
  • Guarantee (s.126 Contract Act): the surety's liability is co-extensive with the principal debtor's (s.128); a bank may proceed against the surety without first suing the debtor.
  • Enforcement: the SARFAESI Act, 2002 allows secured creditors to enforce security without court intervention after a 60-day notice (s.13(2)), subject to appeal to the Debts Recovery Tribunal (s.17).
CaseMardia Chemicals Ltd v Union of India (2004)

The Supreme Court upheld the SARFAESI Act, which allows banks to enforce security interests without the intervention of courts, as a reasonable response to the problem of NPAs. It struck down the requirement of a 75% pre-deposit for an appeal to the DRT under s.17(2) as onerous and oppressive.

Points to remember

  • Promissory notes, bills and cheques (ss.4 to 6, 13).
  • Holder and holder in due course (ss.8 and 9); takes free of defects (s.53); presumptions (s.118).
  • Negotiation, presentment and discharge; dishonour, noting and protest; payment for honour.
  • Crossing (ss.123 to 131); protection of paying and collecting bankers (ss.85 and 131).
  • Section 138: notice within 30 days, payment within 15 days; presumption including the debt (Rangappa); company liability; interim compensation; expeditious trial directions (2021).
  • Pledge, hypothecation, guarantee; SARFAESI (Mardia Chemicals).

Part A Short answer questions5 × 4 = 20 marks

Answer any FIVE of the following.

1.Who is a customer?
Model answer

There is no statutory definition. A customer is a person who has an account with a bank, or for whom the bank agrees to open one (Commissioners of Taxation v English, Scottish and Australian Bank, 1920). The duration of the dealings is not essential.

  • The relationship begins when the account is opened.
  • Status as a customer matters for the protection of a collecting banker (s.131, NI Act), the duty of secrecy, and the banker's lien.
  • Persons using bank services occasionally, without an account, are generally not customers.
2.Garnishee order
Model answer

A garnishee order is a court order attaching money due from a third person (the garnishee, such as a bank) to the judgment-debtor (the customer), for the benefit of the decree-holder (Order XXI Rule 46A CPC).

  • The bank must stop payment of the attached amount and pay it into court, or show cause.
  • The order attaches balances existing at the time of service, not future credits, unless the order says so.
  • Joint accounts and trust accounts require care.
3.Banker's lien
Model answer

A banker's lien is a general lien under s.171 of the Contract Act. The bank may retain securities and valuables of a customer, which came into its hands as a banker, for a general balance of account, unless there is an agreement to the contrary.

  • It is an implied pledge, so the bank can sell after reasonable notice.
  • It does not apply to articles in safe custody, money deposited for a specific purpose, or trust property known to the bank.
  • The right of set-off is separate.
4.Duty of secrecy
Model answer

The bank's duty not to disclose information about a customer's account is a qualified duty (Tournier v National Provincial and Union Bank of England, 1924). Disclosure is permitted:

  • under compulsion of law (income tax, PMLA, court orders);
  • where there is a duty to the public;
  • where the bank's interest requires it; or
  • with the customer's express or implied consent.

In India, see also s.13 of the Banking Companies (Acquisition) Act, the Credit Information Companies Act, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, and RBI directions. Right to Information cases have held that RBI inspection reports are disclosable (RBI v Jayantilal N. Mistry, 2015).

5.Protection of the paying banker
Model answer

Under s.85 of the NI Act:

  • a banker who pays an order cheque in due course is discharged, even if an endorsement is forged; and
  • a banker who pays a bearer cheque to the bearer is discharged.

Payment in due course (s.10) means payment in good faith, without negligence, according to the apparent tenor of the instrument, to a person in possession.

  • There is no protection for a forged drawer's signature. The bank must bear the loss unless the customer was negligent (Canara Bank v Canara Sales Corporation, 1987).
  • Crossed cheques are dealt with under s.128.
6.Functions of the Reserve Bank of India
Model answer

The RBI was established by the RBI Act, 1934. Its functions are:

  • monetary authority: formulating monetary policy, with the Monetary Policy Committee targeting inflation of 4% ± 2% (s.45ZA);
  • currency issue (s.22);
  • banker to the government and manager of public debt;
  • banker's bank and lender of last resort;
  • regulator and supervisor of banks and NBFCs (Banking Regulation Act; Chapter IIIB);
  • foreign exchange management (FEMA);
  • payment systems (Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007); and
  • developmental and consumer protection functions, including the Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2021.
7.Non-performing asset
Model answer

A non-performing asset is a loan or advance where interest or principal remains overdue for more than 90 days (RBI prudential norms). NPAs are classified as:

  • substandard (up to 12 months);
  • doubtful; and
  • loss.

Provisioning is required. NPA classification triggers recovery under SARFAESI (secured debts) and is a requirement for proceeding under s.13(2). Dharani Sugars v Union of India (2019) quashed the RBI's 12 February 2018 circular.

8.Demand notice under section 13(2) of SARFAESI
Model answer

When a borrower defaults and the account is classified as an NPA, the secured creditor may, by written notice, require the borrower to discharge the liabilities within 60 days, failing which it may exercise its powers under s.13(4):

  • take possession;
  • take over management;
  • appoint a manager; and
  • require the borrower's debtors to pay.

The borrower may make a representation, and the creditor must reply within 15 days with reasons (s.13(3A); Mardia Chemicals v Union of India, 2004). An appeal lies to the DRT under s.17. SARFAESI does not apply to agricultural land (s.31(i)).

9.Bank nationalisation
Model answer
  • The Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1969 nationalised 14 major banks, and six more were nationalised in 1980.
  • R.C. Cooper v Union of India (1970, the Bank Nationalisation case): the 1969 Act was struck down for discriminatory exclusion and non-payment of fair compensation. It was re-enacted.
  • Objectives: social control, priority sector lending and branch expansion.
  • Later trends: bank mergers, which reduced the number of public sector banks to 12 by 2020, and the recapitalisation of public sector banks.

Part B Essay questions2 × 15 = 30 marks

Answer any TWO of the following.

10.Explain the nature of the relationship between banker and customer.
Model answer

General relationship: debtor and creditor

  • When a customer deposits money, the bank becomes a debtor and the customer a creditor (Foley v Hill, 1848). The money belongs to the bank.
  • The bank must repay on demand at the branch where the account is kept, and a demand is needed before repayment is due.
  • Limitation runs from the date of demand (Art. 22, Limitation Act, for deposits).

Special relationships

  1. Creditor and debtor: when a customer borrows.
  2. Trustee and beneficiary: for safe-custody articles and money given for a specific purpose.
  3. Bailor and bailee: in safe deposit lockers, although banks argue that the relationship is a lease. The Supreme Court has directed the RBI to lay down guidelines (Amitabha Dasgupta v United Bank of India, 2021).
  4. Principal and agent: in collecting cheques, paying bills, and buying and selling securities.
  5. Lessor and lessee: for lockers.
  6. Pledgor and pledgee; mortgagor and mortgagee.

Obligations of the banker

  • to honour cheques within the available balance;
  • not to dishonour wrongfully;
  • to maintain secrecy (Tournier);
  • to maintain accounts accurately;
  • to give notice before closing an account; and
  • to give reasonable care and service.
  • Delay in presenting a cheque for collection is a deficiency in service (Canara Bank v Kavita Chowdhary, 2026).
  • Banks are liable under consumer law.

Rights of the banker

  • general lien;
  • set-off;
  • appropriation of payments (Contract Act ss.59 to 61, and Clayton's case); and
  • charging interest and fees.

Duties of the customer

  • to draw cheques carefully, avoiding facilitation of fraud (London Joint Stock Bank v Macmillan, 1918); and
  • to inform the bank of forgery.

Termination

By notice, death, insolvency, insanity, garnishee order, or closure.

Conclusion

The basic relationship is debtor and creditor, with layers of fiduciary and agency duties. Consumer law and RBI directions increasingly shape it.

11.Explain the regulation of banking companies under the Banking Regulation Act, 1949.
Model answer

Purpose

The Act consolidates the law relating to banking companies and gives the RBI wide regulatory powers.

Key provisions

  • Business (ss.5(b) and 6): banking is accepting deposits for lending or investment, repayable on demand. Permitted businesses are listed, and trading is prohibited (s.8).
  • Licence (s.22): compulsory from the RBI. It may be cancelled.
  • Capital and reserves (ss.11 to 17): minimum capital, reserve funds, and CRR and SLR (ss.18 and 24).
  • Management (ss.10 and 10A to 10B): restrictions on directors and appointment of directors and chairmen. The RBI may remove managerial personnel and supersede boards (ss.36AA and 36ACA).
  • Loans and advances (s.20): restrictions on loans to directors. The RBI may direct lending policies (s.21).
  • Control over branches (s.23) and inspection (s.35).
  • Directions (s.35A).
  • Resolution of stressed assets (ss.35AA and 35AB, 2017): the RBI may direct banks to initiate insolvency proceedings.
  • Moratorium and amalgamation (s.45): the RBI may apply for a moratorium and prepare a scheme. Examples are Yes Bank (2020) and Lakshmi Vilas Bank (2020).
  • Winding up (ss.38 to 45): through the High Court, with the RBI as official liquidator.
  • Cooperative banks: brought under fuller RBI regulation by the 2020 amendment.
  • Penalties (ss.46 and 47A).

Banking Laws (Amendment) Act, 2025

  • up to four nominees for accounts;
  • a redefined "substantial interest";
  • changes in reporting dates; and
  • longer tenures for cooperative bank directors.

Conclusion

The Act gives the RBI comprehensive control over the entry, operation, governance and exit of banks, which has been strengthened after recent bank failures.

12.Discuss the mechanisms for recovery of bank loans in India.
Model answer

Civil suits

These are slow, which led to special laws.

Recovery of Debts and Bankruptcy Act, 1993 (RDB Act)

  • Debts Recovery Tribunals hear claims of ₹20 lakh and above, with appeals to the DRAT, on a 25% to 50% deposit.
  • Recovery certificates are executed by recovery officers.
  • Banks can recover dues summarily. Civil court jurisdiction is barred (s.18).
  • A DRT certificate is not a "decree" for the purpose of initiating insolvency (HDFC Bank v Kishore Mehta, 2026).

SARFAESI Act, 2002

  • Secured creditors may enforce security without court intervention:
  • a 60-day notice under s.13(2);
  • a reply to objections under s.13(3A);
  • possession under s.13(4); and
  • sale.
  • The District Magistrate assists in taking possession (s.14).
  • Appeals lie to the DRT (s.17).
  • Upheld in Mardia Chemicals (2004).
  • It is inapplicable to agricultural land and to debts below ₹1 lakh.
  • Asset reconstruction companies are regulated under it.

Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016

  • Financial creditors may initiate CIRP against corporate debtors (s.7).
  • Personal guarantors can be pursued (Part III; Lalit Kumar Jain, 2021; Dilip B. Jiwrajka, 2023).
  • The IBC is not a mere recovery tool (Dhanlaxmi Bank, 2026).

Lok Adalats

These handle small loans and settlements.

Criminal remedies

  • s.138 of the NI Act for dishonoured cheques; and
  • cheating, for fraudulent borrowers.
  • Wilful defaulter rules and RBI master directions apply. Fraud classification requires a hearing (State Bank of India v Rajesh Agarwal, 2023).

Conclusion

A layered system of DRTs, SARFAESI and the IBC has improved recovery. Courts insist on due process and fair hearing for borrowers.

13.Discuss the role of the Reserve Bank of India in regulating foreign exchange and banking for non-residents.
Model answer

Evolution

FERA 1973 was a criminal, control-oriented law. FEMA 1999 is a civil law that facilitates external trade and payments, under which violations attract penalties.

FEMA framework

  • Current account transactions (s.5): freely permitted, subject to reasonable restrictions (the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, currently USD 250,000 per year).
  • Capital account transactions (s.6): regulated by the RBI and the government, including FDI, external commercial borrowings and overseas investment.
  • Authorised persons (s.10): banks and money changers.
  • Adjudication (ss.13 to 16) and appeals to the Appellate Tribunal.
  • Enforcement by the Enforcement Directorate.
  • Penalties: up to three times the sum involved.

Non-resident banking

  • NRE accounts: repatriable, tax-free interest, in rupees.
  • NRO accounts: for income earned in India; repatriation is limited.
  • FCNR(B) accounts: term deposits in foreign currency, free of exchange risk for the depositor.
  • Special Non-Resident Rupee accounts.
  • Investment by NRIs and OCIs under portfolio schemes and FDI rules.
  • Remittance facilities.

Anti-money laundering

  • PMLA 2002 and KYC norms;
  • FATF standards; and
  • reporting of suspicious transactions to the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU-IND).

Related law

The Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) and Imposition of Tax Act, 2015.

Conclusion

The RBI manages foreign exchange through a liberal but regulated FEMA regime, and offers non-residents a range of account facilities, balancing capital flows with stability.

Part C Problems2 × 10 = 20 marks

Answer any TWO of the following.

14.A customer deposits a local cheque for ₹2 lakh for collection. The bank fails to present it for 45 days, by which time the drawer's account has insufficient funds and the cheque is dishonoured. The customer sues the bank. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Is the collecting bank liable for delay?

Law:

  • A collecting banker acts as the customer's agent and must present the cheque within a reasonable time.
  • Under s.131 of the NI Act, protection applies only to good-faith collection without negligence.
  • Canara Bank v Kavita Chowdhary (2026): delay by a collecting bank in presenting a cheque is a deficiency in service under the Consumer Protection Act.

Application: A 45-day delay is unreasonable and caused the loss.

Conclusion: The bank is liable to compensate the customer. A consumer complaint is maintainable.

15.A bank pays a cheque for ₹5 lakh on which the customer's signature has been skilfully forged. The customer discovers it a month later and demands a refund. The bank claims the customer's negligence in keeping the cheque book. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Who bears the loss on a forged drawer's signature?

Law:

  • A forged signature is a nullity. A bank paying on it has no mandate and cannot debit the customer (Canara Bank v Canara Sales Corporation, 1987).
  • Section 85 of the NI Act protects only against forged endorsements, not forged drawer signatures.
  • The customer's negligence must be proximate and directly cause the forgery to defeat the claim (London Joint Stock Bank v Macmillan, 1918). Mere carelessness in keeping a cheque book is not enough.

Application: No proximate negligence by the customer is shown.

Conclusion: The bank must re-credit ₹5 lakh, with interest.

16.A bank issues a notice under section 13(2) of SARFAESI to a farmer for default on a loan secured by mortgage of his agricultural land, and later takes possession of the land. Advise the farmer.
Model answer

Issue: Does SARFAESI apply to agricultural land?

Law:

  • Section 31(i) of SARFAESI: the Act does not apply to security interests created in agricultural land.
  • Whether land is agricultural is determined by its nature and use at the time of the security (ITC Ltd v Blue Coast Hotels, 2018; Indian Bank v K. Pappireddiyar, 2018).
  • The remedy is an application under s.17 to the DRT, or a writ.

Application: If the land was agricultural and used for agriculture, SARFAESI proceedings are void.

Conclusion: The farmer can apply to the DRT under s.17 to set aside the possession. The bank must use a civil suit or the RDB Act instead.

17.A bank wrongfully dishonours a trader's cheque for ₹50,000, though there were sufficient funds. The payee's business is harmed, and the trader's reputation suffers. Advise the trader.
Model answer

Issue: The liability of a bank for wrongful dishonour.

Law:

  • A banker must honour cheques drawn within the funds available (s.31, NI Act). The bank is liable to the drawer for loss or damage caused by wrongful dishonour.
  • For traders, substantial damages may be awarded without proof of actual loss, because of injury to credit (Rolin v Steward, 1854; Gibbons v Westminster Bank, 1939, where nominal damages were awarded to non-traders).
  • Consumer protection remedies are also available.

Application: Sufficient funds existed, so the dishonour was wrongful.

Conclusion: The trader can claim substantial damages for loss of reputation and credit, before a civil court or the consumer commission.
