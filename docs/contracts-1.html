History and nature of contractual obligations

Contract law grew out of procedure: English courts first enforced promises through narrow writs, and the general idea of an enforceable promise arrived only when assumpsit replaced them.

Why history matters for an Indian student

The Indian Contract Act, 1872 is a codification of English common law as it stood in the mid-nineteenth century. Words such as consideration, privity, frustration and quasi-contract are English inventions, and Indian courts still read them in the light of English decisions unless the Act says otherwise. Knowing how those ideas were born explains why the Act is shaped the way it is.

The old writs

Medieval English law had no general law of contract. A plaintiff had to fit the claim into one of the royal writs, each with its own procedure.

  • Debt: recovery of a fixed sum of money owed, for example the price of goods delivered or a loan. The defendant could defeat it by wager of law, that is, by producing compurgators who swore he owed nothing.
  • Covenant: enforcement of a promise made under seal (a deed). By the fourteenth century courts insisted on a sealed document, so informal promises fell outside it.
  • Account: used against a bailiff, receiver or partner who had handled another's money and had to render an account.
  • Detinue: recovery of a specific chattel wrongfully withheld.

These writs left a large gap: an informal promise to do something (not to pay a fixed sum) could not be sued on at all.

Action on the case and assumpsit

The gap was filled by trespass on the case, a flexible action for wrongs not covered by trespass proper. Courts first allowed it where a person undertook (assumpsit) a task and performed it badly, causing harm (misfeasance). In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries it was extended to cases where the person did nothing at all (nonfeasance). Finally, in Slade's Case (1602) the King's Bench held that assumpsit could be brought instead of debt, because every executory contract implied a promise to pay. Assumpsit, a tort-based action, thus became the general remedy for broken promises.

CaseSlade's Case (1602)

The plaintiff sold a crop of wheat and rye; the buyer did not pay. The court allowed assumpsit in place of debt, holding that every contract executory imports in itself an assumpsit. This avoided wager of law and made assumpsit the general action on informal contracts.

How consideration emerged

Once assumpsit was available for any informal promise, courts needed a test to separate promises worth enforcing from mere gratuitous ones. The answer was consideration: something given, done or promised in exchange for the promise. By the seventeenth century it was settled that a promise not under seal was enforceable only if supported by consideration. The rule survives in s.25 of the Indian Act.

The moral basis of contractual obligation

Why should the law enforce promises at all? Four answers are usually given.

  • Promise principle: a person who freely promises creates a moral obligation; the law reinforces it (Fried, Contract as Promise).
  • Reliance: the promisee changes position in reliance on the promise; law protects that reliance (this explains promissory estoppel).
  • Bargain and exchange: markets depend on reliable exchange; enforcement lowers the cost of trade.
  • Natural law: pacta sunt servanda, agreements must be kept, a principle drawn from canon and Roman law and now a rule of international treaty law too.

Subjective and objective theories

Will theory (subjective)

Nineteenth-century jurists such as Savigny and Pothier treated a contract as a meeting of two wills (consensus ad idem). If the parties did not in fact intend the same thing, there was no contract.

Objective theory

English and American courts moved to an objective test: what matters is what a reasonable person would understand from the words and conduct, not what each party secretly meant. Holmes famously said the law has nothing to do with the actual state of the parties' minds.

CaseSmith v Hughes (1871)

A buyer bought oats thinking they were old oats; they were new. Blackburn J held that if a reasonable man would believe the other party was assenting to the terms proposed, he is bound, whatever his real intention. The objective test governs formation.

Indian law combines both. Section 13 defines consent as agreement "upon the same thing in the same sense", which is subjective language, but courts decide whether that agreement exists by looking at outward words and conduct.

Sanctity of contract and freedom of contract

The classical view held that adults should be free to make whatever bargains they choose, and courts should enforce them strictly.

CasePrinting and Numerical Registering Co v Sampson (1875)

Sir George Jessel MR said that if there is one thing more than another which public policy requires, it is that men of full age and competent understanding shall have the utmost liberty of contracting, and their contracts freely entered into shall be held sacred and enforced.

The twentieth century qualified this freedom:

  • standard form contracts drafted by the stronger party (railway tickets, insurance policies, bank forms);
  • statutory controls on rent, wages, interest and consumer terms;
  • courts striking down unconscionable terms as opposed to public policy.
CaseCentral Inland Water Transport Corporation v Brojo Nath Ganguly (1986)

A service rule allowing a government company to terminate a permanent employee on three months' notice without reasons was struck down. The Supreme Court held that an unfair and unreasonable clause in a contract between parties of grossly unequal bargaining power is opposed to public policy and void under s.23.

The Indian Contract Act, 1872

  • Drafted by the Third Indian Law Commission in England, largely on English principles, with some departures (for example, past consideration and consideration from a third party are recognised).
  • In force from 1 September 1872. It now extends to the whole of India.
  • Originally covered sale of goods and partnership as well; those were moved to the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 and the Indian Partnership Act, 1932.
  • The Preamble says the Act defines and amends "certain parts" of the law relating to contracts. It is therefore not exhaustive: courts may apply principles of justice, equity and good conscience where the Act is silent.
CaseIrrawaddy Flotilla Co v Bugwandas (1891)

The Privy Council held that the Contract Act is not a complete code dealing with the law relating to contracts; it deals only with the topics it covers.

Points to remember

  • Medieval writs of debt, covenant and account left informal promises unenforceable.
  • Assumpsit, an action on the case, became the general contract action after Slade's Case (1602).
  • Consideration developed as the test for which informal promises the law would enforce.
  • Formation is judged objectively (Smith v Hughes), though s.13 speaks of consensus ad idem.
  • Freedom of contract is now limited by public policy and statutes protecting weaker parties (Brojo Nath Ganguly).
  • The Contract Act is not exhaustive (Irrawaddy Flotilla).

Unit 2

Agreement and contract: definitions, elements and kinds

Every contract is an agreement, but only an agreement that meets the conditions of s.10 is a contract.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Definitions in s.2
  • Essential elements of a valid contract (s.10)
  • Kinds of contracts

The chain of definitions in s.2

The Act builds the idea of a contract in steps.

Statutes.2(a), (b)

A proposal is made when one person signifies to another his willingness to do or abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining the assent of that other. A proposal, when accepted, becomes a promise.

Statutes.2(e)

Every promise and every set of promises, forming the consideration for each other, is an agreement.

Statutes.2(h)

An agreement enforceable by law is a contract.

So: proposal + acceptance = promise; promise + consideration = agreement; agreement + enforceability = contract. Salmond summed it up: a contract is an agreement creating and defining obligations between the parties.

Other key definitions:

  • s.2(c): the person making the proposal is the promisor; the person accepting is the promisee.
  • s.2(f): promises which form the consideration for each other are reciprocal promises.
  • s.2(g): an agreement not enforceable by law is void.
  • s.2(i): an agreement enforceable at the option of one or more parties but not the others is a voidable contract.
  • s.2(j): a contract which ceases to be enforceable by law becomes void when it ceases to be enforceable.

Essential elements: s.10

Statutes.10

All agreements are contracts if they are made by the free consent of parties competent to contract, for a lawful consideration and with a lawful object, and are not hereby expressly declared to be void. Nothing here affects any law requiring a contract to be in writing, attested or registered.

From s.10 and the general law, a valid contract needs:

  1. Offer and acceptance: a lawful proposal and an absolute acceptance (ss.3 to 9).
  2. Intention to create legal relations: not stated in the Act but applied by Indian courts from English law.
  3. Lawful consideration: ss.2(d), 23 and 25.
  4. Capacity of parties: ss.11 and 12.
  5. Free consent: ss.13 to 22.
  6. Lawful object: s.23.
  7. Not expressly declared void: ss.24 to 30 and 56.
  8. Certainty: s.29.
  9. Possibility of performance: s.56.
  10. Formalities, where a statute requires writing or registration (for example, s.25(1), the Registration Act, 1908, and s.54 of the Transfer of Property Act for sale of immovable property worth ₹100 or more).

Intention to create legal relations

Social and domestic arrangements are presumed not to be intended as legally binding; commercial agreements are presumed to be.

CaseBalfour v Balfour (1919)

A husband working in Ceylon promised his wife £30 a month while she stayed in England. Held: no contract. Arrangements between husband and wife are ordinarily not intended to create legal relations.

CaseRose and Frank Co v J.R. Crompton and Bros (1925)

A business agreement stated it was "not a formal or legal agreement" and would not be subject to the courts. Held: the express clause rebutted the usual presumption, so the agreement itself was not binding, though orders actually accepted under it were.

Void, voidable, illegal and unenforceable

  • Void agreement (s.2(g)): never enforceable, for example an agreement with a minor, or a wagering agreement.
  • Voidable contract (s.2(i)): valid until the aggrieved party rescinds it, for example a contract induced by coercion, undue influence, fraud or misrepresentation (ss.19, 19A).
  • Contract that becomes void (s.2(j)): valid when made but later unenforceable, for example through supervening impossibility (s.56).
  • Illegal agreement: forbidden by law. Every illegal agreement is void, but not every void agreement is illegal. Collateral transactions to an illegal agreement are also tainted; collateral transactions to a merely void agreement are not (Gherulal Parakh v Mahadeodas Maiya, discussed in Unit 7).
  • Unenforceable contract: valid in substance but cannot be enforced because of a technical defect, such as want of stamp or registration, or a time-barred debt.

Express, implied and quasi

An express contract is made in words. An implied contract is inferred from conduct (s.9): boarding a bus implies a promise to pay the fare. Quasi-contracts (ss.68 to 72) are not contracts at all; the law imposes an obligation to prevent unjust enrichment (Unit 9).

Electronic contracts

Statutes.10A, Information Technology Act, 2000

Where formation of a contract involves communication of proposals, acceptances or revocations in electronic form, the contract shall not be deemed unenforceable solely because electronic form was used.

Click-wrap and browse-wrap terms, email exchanges and electronic signatures under the IT Act are valid ways to contract.

CaseTrimex International FZE v Vedanta Aluminium Ltd (2010)

A concluded contract was held to have been formed by an exchange of emails, even though a formal written contract was to follow and was never signed.

Standard form contracts

Printed terms offered on a take-it-or-leave-it basis. Courts protect the weaker party by requiring reasonable notice of the terms, construing ambiguity against the drafter (contra proferentem), and striking down unconscionable terms under s.23.

Exam tip

In an essay on "All contracts are agreements but all agreements are not contracts", quote ss.2(e), 2(h) and 10, list the elements, and give one example of an agreement that fails each element.

Points to remember

  • Proposal + acceptance = promise; promise + consideration = agreement; agreement enforceable by law = contract.
  • s.10 lists free consent, competent parties, lawful consideration, lawful object and not expressly void.
  • Intention to create legal relations is presumed in commercial but not in social or domestic dealings (Balfour v Balfour).
  • Void, voidable, illegal and unenforceable contracts must be distinguished; illegality also taints collateral transactions.
  • E-contracts are valid under s.10A of the IT Act, 2000.

Proposal and acceptance

A contract begins with a proposal capable of acceptance and an acceptance that is absolute; the timing rules of ss.4 to 6 decide when each becomes binding.

Proposal (offer)

Statutes.2(a)

When one person signifies to another his willingness to do or to abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining the assent of that other to such act or abstinence, he is said to make a proposal.

Essentials of a valid proposal

  • It must express willingness to be bound, not merely to negotiate.
  • It must be made with a view to obtaining assent.
  • Its terms must be certain (s.29).
  • It must be communicated to the offeree (s.4).
  • It must not contain a term that silence will amount to acceptance.
  • It may be express or implied (s.9), specific (to a named person) or general (to the world at large).

General offers

CaseCarlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co (1893)

The company advertised £100 to anyone who caught influenza after using its smoke ball as directed, and deposited £1,000 to show sincerity. Mrs Carlill used it and caught influenza. Held: the advertisement was a general offer; performing the conditions was acceptance, and notice of acceptance was dispensed with by the nature of the offer.

CaseLalman Shukla v Gauri Dutt (1913)

A servant traced the defendant's missing nephew, not knowing that a reward had been announced. Held: he could not claim the reward. There can be no acceptance without knowledge of the offer.

Cross offers

Two identical offers crossing in the post do not make a contract, because neither is an acceptance of the other: Tinn v Hoffman (1873).

Proposal and invitation to proposal

An invitation to treat (invitation to offer) is a preliminary step that invites others to make offers. Examples: display of goods in a shop, catalogues, price lists, advertisements, auction announcements, calls for tenders, prospectuses.

CasePharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots Cash Chemists (1953)

Goods on the shelves of a self-service shop are an invitation to treat. The customer makes the offer at the cash desk, and the shop accepts it there, where a pharmacist supervised the sale.

CaseHarvey v Facey (1893)

Asked "Will you sell us Bumper Hall Pen? Telegraph lowest cash price", the defendant replied "Lowest price for Bumper Hall Pen £900". Held: the reply was a statement of price, not an offer, so the plaintiff's "We agree to buy" made no contract.

  • Auctions: the bid is the offer; the fall of the hammer is acceptance; a bid may be withdrawn before then (s.64, Sale of Goods Act, 1930). An advertisement of an auction is not an offer to hold it: Harris v Nickerson (1873).
  • Tenders: a call for tenders is an invitation; each tender is an offer: Spencer v Harding (1870). In government tenders the State's choice is subject to judicial review for arbitrariness: Tata Cellular v Union of India (1994).

Floating (standing) offers and tenders

A tender to supply goods "as and when required" over a period is a standing offer. Each order placed is a separate acceptance, creating a separate contract. The offeror may revoke the standing offer for the future but must honour orders already placed.

CaseGreat Northern Railway v Witham (1873)

Witham tendered to supply stores as required over a year. The railway's orders were acceptances, so he was bound to supply what had been ordered, though he could withdraw as to future orders.

Dumping of goods (unsolicited goods)

Sending goods without request and saying that silence or retention will be treated as acceptance does not bind the recipient. Silence is not acceptance (Felthouse v Bindley, below). The recipient owes at most the duty of an involuntary bailee, and in consumer settings such a practice can amount to an unfair trade practice under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019.

Acceptance

Statutes.2(b)

When the person to whom the proposal is made signifies his assent thereto, the proposal is said to be accepted.

Statutes.7

Acceptance must be absolute and unqualified, and expressed in some usual and reasonable manner, unless the proposal prescribes the manner. If the proposal prescribes a manner and the acceptance is not made in that manner, the proposer may insist within a reasonable time on that manner; if he does not, he accepts the acceptance.

Statutes.8

Performance of the conditions of a proposal, or acceptance of any consideration for a reciprocal promise offered with a proposal, is an acceptance.

Rules of acceptance

  • Absolute and unqualified: a conditional acceptance is a counter-offer, which destroys the original offer: Hyde v Wrench (1840).
  • Only the person to whom the offer is made can accept it (Boulton v Jones, 1857).
  • It must be communicated by the offeree or his authorised agent. Unauthorised information is not acceptance: Powell v Lee (1908), where a manager privately told an applicant he had been selected but the board then changed its mind.
  • Silence is not acceptance.
CaseFelthouse v Bindley (1862)

An uncle wrote to his nephew: "If I hear no more about him, I consider the horse mine." The nephew said nothing. Held: no contract. The offeror cannot impose silence as acceptance.

Communication: when is it complete?

Statutes.4

Communication of a proposal is complete when it comes to the knowledge of the person to whom it is made. Communication of an acceptance is complete as against the proposer when it is put in a course of transmission to him so as to be out of the power of the acceptor; as against the acceptor, when it comes to the knowledge of the proposer.

Instantaneous communication

For telephone, telex and similar instantaneous modes, the contract is complete only when the acceptance is heard by the proposer, and it is made at the place where the acceptance is received.

CaseEntores Ltd v Miles Far East Corporation (1955)

Acceptance by telex from Amsterdam to London: the contract was made in London where the acceptance was received.

CaseBhagwandas Goverdhandas Kedia v Girdharilal Parshottamdas (1966)

An offer made from Khamgaon was accepted by telephone from Ahmedabad. By majority, the Supreme Court held the contract was made at Ahmedabad (where the acceptance was spoken and heard) for purposes of jurisdiction. Hidayatullah J dissented, arguing the postal rule in s.4 should apply to all modes.

For e-mail, s.13 of the IT Act fixes time and place of dispatch and receipt: an electronic record is received when it enters the designated computer resource, and is deemed dispatched and received at the place of business of the originator and the addressee.

Revocation

Statutes.5

A proposal may be revoked at any time before the communication of its acceptance is complete as against the proposer, but not afterwards. An acceptance may be revoked at any time before the communication of acceptance is complete as against the acceptor, but not afterwards.

Statutes.6

A proposal is revoked (1) by notice of revocation; (2) by lapse of the time prescribed, or a reasonable time; (3) by failure of the acceptor to fulfil a condition precedent to acceptance; (4) by the death or insanity of the proposer, if the fact comes to the knowledge of the acceptor before acceptance.

Other modes recognised by courts: counter-offer, rejection, and subsequent illegality or destruction of the subject-matter.

The "window" for revocation in a postal contract

A posts an offer to B on Monday; it reaches B on Tuesday; B posts acceptance on Wednesday; it reaches A on Friday.

  • A can revoke his offer up to Wednesday (before B posts acceptance).
  • B can revoke his acceptance up to Friday (before it reaches A), by a faster mode such as telephone.
  • A promise to keep an offer open is not binding unless supported by consideration (an option contract): Dickinson v Dodds (1876).
Exam tip

Problem questions on communication almost always turn on s.4 and s.5. Draw a simple timeline with dates, mark when communication is complete as against each party, and then apply the revocation windows.

Points to remember

  • Advertisements, shop displays, catalogues, auctions and calls for tender are invitations to treat; the response is the offer.
  • A general offer is accepted by performing its conditions (Carlill); knowledge of the offer is essential (Lalman Shukla).
  • Acceptance must be absolute; a qualified acceptance is a counter-offer (Hyde v Wrench).
  • Silence is not acceptance (Felthouse v Bindley); unsolicited goods do not bind the recipient.
  • s.4: acceptance is complete against the proposer on posting and against the acceptor on receipt.
  • For instantaneous communication the contract is made where acceptance is received (Bhagwandas Kedia).
  • A standing offer is accepted afresh by each order (Great Northern Railway v Witham).

Consideration

Ex nudo pacto non oritur actio: a bare promise gives no right of action. Consideration is the price paid for the promise.

Definition

Statutes.2(d)

When, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a consideration for the promise.

The classic English definition is from Currie v Misa (1875): some right, interest, profit or benefit accruing to one party, or some forbearance, detriment, loss or responsibility given, suffered or undertaken by the other.

Essentials

1. At the desire of the promisor

An act done at the request of a third party, or voluntarily, is not consideration.

CaseDurga Prasad v Baldeo (1880)

The plaintiff built shops in a market at the Collector's order. The defendant, who later occupied a shop, promised him a commission. Held: no consideration, because the building was not done at the desire of the defendant.

2. May move from the promisee or any other person

Unlike English law, Indian law allows consideration to come from a third party.

CaseChinnaya v Ramayya (1882)

An old woman gifted property to her daughter on condition that the daughter pay an annuity to the woman's brother. The daughter promised the brother to pay and then refused. Held: the brother could sue. Consideration had moved from the mother, and s.2(d) allows that.

3. Past, present or future

  • Past: "has done". Past consideration is valid in India if the act was done at the promisor's desire (for example, a service rendered earlier at request and later paid for by promise). In England past consideration is no consideration (Re McArdle, 1951), subject to the request exception in Lampleigh v Brathwait (1615).
  • Present (executed): "does".
  • Future (executory): "promises to do".

4. Must be real, not illusory

It must have some value in the eye of the law. Performing an existing legal duty is not consideration (Collins v Godefroy, 1831), though doing more than the duty is (Glasbrook Bros v Glamorgan CC, 1925). Performing an existing contractual duty owed to a third party can be good consideration (Scotson v Pegg, 1861). In England, a practical benefit to the promisor can support a promise of extra pay (Williams v Roffey Bros, 1990).

5. Need not be adequate

Statutes.25, Explanation 2

An agreement to which the consent of the promisor is freely given is not void merely because the consideration is inadequate; but the inadequacy may be taken into account by the court in deciding whether the consent was freely given.

Illustration (f) to s.25: A agrees to sell a horse worth ₹1,000 for ₹10. The agreement is a contract if A's consent was free, but inadequacy is evidence on that question. English authority: Chappell v Nestlé (1960), chocolate wrappers as part consideration.

Privity of contract

Two related rules:

  1. Privity of consideration: in England consideration must move from the promisee. India rejects this (Chinnaya).
  2. Privity of contract: only a party to the contract can sue on it. A stranger cannot sue even if the contract was for his benefit. India accepts this rule.
CaseTweddle v Atkinson (1861)

The fathers of a bride and groom agreed to pay the groom money. The groom sued the bride's father's estate. Held: he could not sue, as he was not a party to the contract.

CaseDunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co v Selfridge and Co (1915)

Dunlop sold tyres to a dealer who agreed not to sell below list price and to obtain the same promise from retailers. Selfridge, a retailer, broke the promise. Held: Dunlop could not sue Selfridge, as Dunlop was not party to that contract and gave no consideration to Selfridge.

CaseM.C. Chacko v State Bank of Travancore (1969)

The Supreme Court held that a person not a party to a contract cannot enforce it, even though it was made for his benefit, unless he comes within a recognised exception.

Exceptions to privity

  • Trust or charge: a beneficiary under a trust or a charge on property can enforce it: Khwaja Muhammad Khan v Husaini Begum (1910), where a bride enforced her father-in-law's promise of an allowance charged on property.
  • Family settlements and marriage arrangements: a member of the family for whose benefit a provision is made in a family arrangement or marriage settlement can enforce it.
  • Acknowledgement or estoppel: where the promisor acknowledges the third party's right, for example by paying him or admitting liability.
  • Assignment: an assignee of contractual rights can sue (s.130 TPA for actionable claims).
  • Agency: a principal can sue on a contract made by his agent.
  • Covenants running with land: a purchaser with notice is bound by restrictive covenants (Tulk v Moxhay, 1848).
  • Statutory exceptions: for example, third-party claims against insurers under the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988.

Exceptions to "no consideration, no contract": s.25

Statutes.25

An agreement made without consideration is void, unless (1) it is expressed in writing and registered, and made on account of natural love and affection between parties standing in a near relation to each other; or (2) it is a promise to compensate, wholly or in part, a person who has already voluntarily done something for the promisor, or something the promisor was legally compellable to do; or (3) it is a promise in writing, signed by the person to be charged or his agent, to pay wholly or in part a debt which the creditor might have enforced but for the law of limitation.

CaseRajlukhy Dabee v Bhootnath Mookerjee (1900)

A husband promised in a registered document to pay his wife maintenance, after quarrels. Held: void. Though near relations, the document showed no natural love and affection.

Further exceptions:

  • Explanation 1 to s.25: completed gifts are valid without consideration.
  • s.185: no consideration is needed to create an agency.
  • s.127: anything done or promise made for the benefit of the principal debtor is sufficient consideration for a surety.
  • Charitable subscriptions: where the promisee has acted on the promise, for example by incurring liability, the promise is enforceable: Kedar Nath Bhattacharji v Gorie Mahomed (1886), a subscription for building a town hall.

Promissory estoppel

Where a promise is made intending it to be acted on, and it is acted on, the promisor may be stopped from going back on it, even without consideration.

CaseCentral London Property Trust v High Trees House (1947)

A landlord promised to halve the rent during wartime. Denning J held that he could not claim the full rent for the war years, because the tenant had relied on the promise.

CaseMotilal Padampat Sugar Mills v State of Uttar Pradesh (1979)

The State promised a sales tax exemption to new industrial units; the company set up a factory relying on it. The Supreme Court held the State bound by promissory estoppel; the government is not exempt from the doctrine, except where public interest requires otherwise.

Law Commission and evaluation of the doctrine

The Law Commission of India in its 13th Report (1958) reviewed the Contract Act and did not recommend abolishing consideration; it favoured the existing structure while suggesting limited reforms. Critics argue that:

  • consideration is technical and often fictional (a peppercorn suffices);
  • the doctrine defeats reasonable reliance, which is why equity created promissory estoppel;
  • other systems (civil law countries) enforce serious promises without any bargain requirement.

Defenders reply that consideration separates bargains from casual or gratuitous promises and gives commercial certainty. Lord Mansfield's attempt in Pillans v Van Mierop (1765) to treat consideration as only evidence of seriousness was rejected by later courts.

Points to remember

  • Consideration must be at the desire of the promisor (Durga Prasad v Baldeo).
  • In India it may move from a third party (Chinnaya v Ramayya) and may be past.
  • Inadequacy does not void the contract but is evidence on free consent (s.25 Expl. 2).
  • A stranger to the contract cannot sue (Tweddle v Atkinson, Dunlop v Selfridge, M.C. Chacko), subject to trust, family settlement, acknowledgement, assignment and agency exceptions.
  • s.25 saves agreements based on natural love and affection (written and registered), compensation for past voluntary services and written promises to pay time-barred debts.
  • Promissory estoppel binds even the State (Motilal Padampat).

Capacity to contract

The law protects those who cannot protect themselves: a minor's agreement is void, and a person of unsound mind can contract only when of sound mind.

Who is competent?

Statutes.11

Every person is competent to contract who is of the age of majority according to the law to which he is subject, who is of sound mind, and who is not disqualified from contracting by any law to which he is subject.

Statutes.12

A person is of sound mind for contracting if, at the time he makes the contract, he is capable of understanding it and of forming a rational judgment as to its effect upon his interests. A person usually of unsound mind but occasionally of sound mind may contract when of sound mind; a person usually of sound mind but occasionally of unsound mind may not contract when of unsound mind.

Who is a minor?

Under s.3 of the Indian Majority Act, 1875, a person domiciled in India attains majority on completing eighteen years. The Act applies irrespective of religion for purposes of contract, though it does not affect capacity in matters of marriage, dower, divorce and adoption.

Nature of a minor's agreement: void ab initio

The Act did not say expressly whether a minor's agreement was void or voidable. The Privy Council settled it.

CaseMohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose (1903)

Dharmodas, a minor, mortgaged his house to a moneylender for ₹20,000, of which about ₹8,000 was actually advanced. The lender's agent knew he was a minor. The minor sued to set aside the mortgage. Held: (1) a minor's agreement is void ab initio, since ss.10 and 11 require competent parties; (2) the minor need not repay the advance, because ss.64 and 65 apply only to contracts made between competent parties; (3) there is no estoppel against a minor where the other party knew the truth.

Consequences of the rule

  • No ratification: a minor cannot ratify on attaining majority an agreement made during minority, because a void agreement cannot be ratified. A fresh contract with fresh consideration is needed.
  • No estoppel: a minor who misrepresents his age is not estopped from pleading minority, because estoppel cannot override a statutory incapacity (s.121 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, earlier s.115 of the Evidence Act). Confirmed in Sadiq Ali Khan v Jai Kishore (1928).
  • No specific performance for or against a minor, because there is no mutuality: Mir Sarwarjan v Fakhruddin Mohd Chowdhury (1912), where a guardian's contract to buy land for a minor was not enforced.
  • No liability in tort where the tort is merely a way of enforcing the void contract: R. Leslie Ltd v Sheill (1914), where a minor who lied about his age to borrow money could not be made to repay it as damages for deceit.
  • Minor cannot be adjudged insolvent, since he cannot incur contractual debts.

Restitution where the minor misrepresented his age

Should a minor who obtained property by fraud keep it? Indian courts applied equity.

CaseKhan Gul v Lakha Singh (1928)

A minor who had fraudulently represented himself as an adult sold land and took the price but refused to deliver the land. The Lahore High Court (Full Bench, Shadi Lal CJ) held that he must restore the money received, applying the equitable principle of restitution (now in the Specific Relief Act).

Statutes.33(2)(b), Specific Relief Act, 1963

Where a defendant successfully resists a suit on the ground that the agreement is void because he was not competent to contract, the court may require him to restore any benefit he received from the other party, to the extent that he or his estate has benefited.

Statutes.30, Specific Relief Act, 1963

On adjudging rescission of a contract, the court may require the party to whom relief is granted to restore so far as may be any benefit he has received and to make compensation.

So the minor who is plaintiff in a cancellation suit, or who as defendant successfully pleads minority, may be ordered to give back what he obtained, though never to pay damages or repay out of his other assets.

Necessaries supplied to a minor

Statutes.68

If a person incapable of entering into a contract, or anyone whom he is legally bound to support, is supplied by another person with necessaries suited to his condition in life, the person who furnished such supplies is entitled to be reimbursed from the property of such incapable person.

  • "Necessaries" means goods and services suited to the minor's station in life and actual needs at the time: food, clothing, shelter, education, medical aid, and legal services to protect his property.
  • The supplier must prove the goods were necessaries and that the minor was not already adequately supplied: Nash v Inman (1908), where eleven fancy waistcoats supplied to a Cambridge undergraduate who already had enough clothes were held not necessaries.
  • Liability is of the minor's property, not personal. It arises in quasi-contract, not contract.

Beneficial and detrimental agreements

A minor can be a promisee or beneficiary: nothing prevents him from receiving a benefit.

  • A mortgage executed in favour of a minor who has advanced the money is enforceable by him: Raghava Chariar v Srinivasa Raghava Chariar (1916) (Madras Full Bench).
  • A minor may be admitted to the benefits of a partnership with the consent of all partners (s.30, Indian Partnership Act, 1932), but is not personally liable for losses.
  • A minor can be an agent, binding the principal, though not personally liable to the principal (s.184).
  • A minor can draw, endorse and negotiate negotiable instruments, binding all parties except himself (s.26, Negotiable Instruments Act).
  • A guardian may, within his powers, enter a contract for the minor's benefit, for example to buy necessaries or for marriage expenses: courts have enforced such contracts where they are within the guardian's competence and for the minor's benefit (Srikakulam Subrahmanyam v Kurra Subba Rao, 1948, Privy Council, where a sale by the mother as guardian to pay off a debt was upheld).

Evaluation of the law on minors

  • The void-ab-initio rule protects minors firmly but can be harsh on honest traders, and encourages dishonest minors.
  • Equitable restitution (Khan Gul; s.33 SRA) softens the harshness.
  • Critics suggest that India adopt a "voidable at the minor's option" model like English law, combined with a clear statutory restitution rule (as in the English Minors' Contracts Act, 1987).
  • Modern problems: minors contracting online (app purchases, gaming, e-commerce); these are void but platforms rely on parental consent mechanisms.

Persons of unsound mind

  • Idiots, lunatics, drunkards and persons under the influence of drugs, at the time of contracting, are incapable.
  • An agreement by a person of unsound mind is void (as with minors), though he is liable for necessaries out of his property under s.68.
  • A lunatic may contract in a lucid interval.

Other persons disqualified by law

  • Alien enemies: during war, an alien enemy cannot contract with Indian citizens except with government licence.
  • Foreign sovereigns and ambassadors: can contract and sue, but cannot be sued without consent of the Central Government (s.86, CPC).
  • Insolvents: an undischarged insolvent cannot deal with property vested in the official receiver.
  • Convicts: in England, while undergoing sentence; Indian law has no general disability.
  • Companies: capacity limited by the objects clause; acts outside it are ultra vires and void: Ashbury Railway Carriage and Iron Co v Riche (1875).

Points to remember

  • A minor is a person under eighteen (Indian Majority Act, 1875).
  • A minor's agreement is void ab initio and cannot be ratified (Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose).
  • No estoppel against a minor; but equity may order restitution of benefits (Khan Gul; s.33 SRA).
  • Necessaries: supplier is reimbursed from the minor's property only (s.68; Nash v Inman).
  • A minor may be a beneficiary, promisee, agent or admitted partner.
  • A person of unsound mind can contract only during sound mind (s.12).

Free consent

Consent obtained by pressure, domination, deceit or error is not the free consent s.10 demands. Most such contracts are voidable; a shared mistake of fact makes them void.

Consent and free consent

Statutes.13

Two or more persons are said to consent when they agree upon the same thing in the same sense.

Statutes.14

Consent is said to be free when it is not caused by (1) coercion (s.15), (2) undue influence (s.16), (3) fraud (s.17), (4) misrepresentation (s.18), or (5) mistake, subject to ss.20, 21 and 22. Consent is "caused" by any of these when it would not have been given but for its existence.

Where there is no consent at all (for example, a party signs not knowing what the document is), there is no agreement. Where consent exists but is not free, the contract is usually voidable.

6.1 Coercion

Statutes.15

Coercion is the committing, or threatening to commit, any act forbidden by the Indian Penal Code, or the unlawful detaining, or threatening to detain, any property, to the prejudice of any person whatever, with the intention of causing any person to enter into an agreement. Explanation: it is immaterial whether the Indian Penal Code is or is not in force where the coercion is employed.

Current law

The Indian Penal Code was repealed and replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 from 1 July 2024. Section 15 has not been textually amended, but under s.8 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 a reference to a repealed enactment is read as a reference to the re-enacting one, so "act forbidden by the IPC" is now read as an act forbidden by the BNS.

Elements

  • An act forbidden by the penal law, or unlawful detention of property, committed or threatened.
  • To the prejudice of any person (not necessarily the contracting party).
  • By any person (not necessarily the other contracting party).
  • With intention of causing any person to enter into an agreement.
CaseChikham Amiraju v Chikham Seshamma (1917)

A husband threatened to commit suicide unless his wife and son executed a release of property in favour of his brother. The Madras High Court (majority) held this was coercion: attempt to commit suicide was then punishable under the IPC, so a threat to commit it was a threat of an act forbidden by the Code.

CaseRanganayakamma v Alwar Setti (1889)

A thirteen-year-old widow was prevented from removing her husband's body for cremation until she agreed to adopt a boy. The adoption was set aside as obtained by coercion.

Economic duress

Illegitimate commercial pressure, such as a threat to break a contract unless the victim pays more, can vitiate consent if it leaves no practical choice: Pao On v Lau Yiu Long (1980); Universe Tankships v ITWF (1983). Indian courts reach similar results through s.23 public policy and the unconscionability principle (Brojo Nath Ganguly, Unit 1), and by allowing recovery under s.72 of money paid under pressure.

Money paid under protest to avoid unlawful seizure of property could be recovered under s.72 as paid "under coercion"; the Privy Council read coercion in s.72 in its ordinary sense, not confined to s.15.

Evaluation of s.15

  • The link to the penal code is narrow: a threat of a civil wrong or of lawful action used for an improper purpose is not coercion.
  • The reference to the repealed IPC is outdated.
  • "Threat to commit suicide" was coercion when suicide attempt was an offence; the BNS does not carry over the general offence of attempt to suicide (IPC s.309), so after 2024 such a threat may no longer fit s.15 and is better argued as undue influence. This is a good point for evaluation answers.

6.2 Undue influence

Statutes.16(1)

A contract is said to be induced by undue influence where the relations subsisting between the parties are such that one of them is in a position to dominate the will of the other and uses that position to obtain an unfair advantage over the other.

Statutes.16(2)

A person is deemed to be in a position to dominate the will of another (a) where he holds a real or apparent authority over the other, or stands in a fiduciary relation; or (b) where he makes a contract with a person whose mental capacity is temporarily or permanently affected by age, illness or mental or bodily distress.

Statutes.16(3)

Where a person in a position to dominate enters into a transaction which on its face or on the evidence appears unconscionable, the burden of proving that it was not induced by undue influence lies on the person in the position to dominate.

Three stages (Raghunath Prasad v Sarju Prasad)

CaseRaghunath Prasad v Sarju Prasad (1923)

The Privy Council laid down the order of inquiry: (1) were the relations such that one party was in a position to dominate the other's will; (2) if so, did he use that position to obtain the contract; (3) is the transaction unconscionable? If so, the burden shifts to the dominant party. On the facts, high interest alone, without proof of domination, did not show undue influence.

CaseMannu Singh v Umadat Pande (1890)

A disciple gifted all his property to his spiritual guru to secure benefits to his soul. Held: the gift was voidable for undue influence. Spiritual advisers are a classic dominant relationship.

Relations presumed to involve domination: parent and child, guardian and ward, doctor and patient, spiritual adviser and devotee, solicitor and client, trustee and beneficiary. Husband and wife is not a presumed relation; domination must be proved.

Independent advice

The dominant party rebuts the presumption most easily by showing the weaker party had competent independent advice before acting, understood the transaction and acted freely.

Pardanashin women

A pardanashin woman lives in seclusion, does not deal with the outside world. Courts give her special protection.

CaseKali Bakhsh Singh v Ram Gopal Singh (1913)

The Privy Council held that a person relying on a deed executed by a pardanashin woman must prove that it was explained to her and understood by her, and that she had independent advice. The protection rests on her presumed inexperience, not on any presumption of undue influence.

The same protection has been extended to illiterate or inexperienced women who are not strictly pardanashin.

Unconscionable bargains

Moneylending at exorbitant rates to a person in distress is the common case. The Usurious Loans Act, 1918 also allows courts to reopen unfair loan transactions.

Statutes.19A

When consent is caused by undue influence, the contract is voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so caused. The court may set it aside absolutely, or, if the party entitled to avoid it has received any benefit, on such terms as the court thinks just.

CaseSubhas Chandra Das Mushib v Ganga Prasad Das Mushib (1967)

The Supreme Court held that mere relationship is not enough. The plaintiff must first show the other party was in a position to dominate his will; only then does the inquiry move to whether the position was used and whether the bargain was unconscionable.

6.3 Misrepresentation

Statutes.18

Misrepresentation means and includes (1) the positive assertion, in a manner not warranted by the information of the person making it, of that which is not true, though he believes it to be true; (2) any breach of duty which, without an intent to deceive, gains an advantage to the person committing it by misleading another to his prejudice; (3) causing, however innocently, a party to an agreement to make a mistake as to the substance of the thing which is the subject of the agreement.

  • Misrepresentation is an innocent false statement: the maker honestly believes it.
  • The statement must be of fact, not opinion (Bisset v Wilkinson, 1927) or mere puff.
  • Misrepresentation of law: everyone is presumed to know the law, so a false statement of the general law is ordinarily not actionable. But a misstatement of foreign law, or of private rights (for example, about the effect of a particular deed), is treated as one of fact.
  • It must induce the contract and the other party must have acted on it.
Statutes.19, Exception

If consent was caused by misrepresentation or by silence fraudulent within s.17, the contract is nevertheless not voidable if the party whose consent was so caused had the means of discovering the truth with ordinary diligence.

This differs from English law, where the victim is not deprived of relief merely because he could have checked (Redgrave v Hurd, 1881).

6.4 Fraud

Statutes.17

Fraud means any of the following acts committed by a party to a contract, or with his connivance, or by his agent, with intent to deceive another party or his agent, or to induce him to enter the contract: (1) the suggestion, as a fact, of that which is not true, by one who does not believe it to be true; (2) the active concealment of a fact by one having knowledge or belief of the fact; (3) a promise made without any intention of performing it; (4) any other act fitted to deceive; (5) any act or omission the law specially declares fraudulent.

Statutes.17, Explanation

Mere silence as to facts likely to affect the willingness of a person to enter into a contract is not fraud, unless the circumstances are such that it is the duty of the person keeping silence to speak, or unless his silence is in itself equivalent to speech.

Essentials

  • A false representation of fact (suggestio falsi) or active concealment (suppressio veri).
  • Knowledge of falsity or recklessness: Derry v Peek (1889), where directors honestly but wrongly believed they had permission to run steam trams; held no fraud, as fraud needs absence of honest belief.
  • Intent to deceive or to induce the contract.
  • The other party must have been actually deceived and must have acted on it. Fraud which does not cause consent does not make the contract voidable (Explanation to s.19).

When does silence amount to fraud?

  1. Duty to speak: contracts uberrimae fidei (of utmost good faith) such as insurance, family settlements, partnership, company prospectuses; and fiduciary relations.
  2. Silence equivalent to speech: illustration (c) to s.17, where B says "If you do not deny it, I shall assume the horse is sound," and A says nothing.
  3. Half-truths: a true statement that is misleading because of what it leaves out.
  4. Change of circumstances: a statement true when made becomes false before the contract, and the maker stays silent: With v O'Flanagan (1936), where a medical practice's income collapsed before sale.
CaseMithoolal Nayak v Life Insurance Corporation of India (1962)

The insured concealed serious illness when taking a life policy. The Supreme Court held the policy voidable for fraudulent suppression of material facts, since insurance is a contract of utmost good faith.

Effect

Voidable at the option of the deceived party (s.19), who may also insist on being put in the position he would have been in had the representation been true. Fraud is also the tort of deceit, giving a claim for damages.

6.5 Mistake

Mistake of fact

Statutes.20

Where both parties to an agreement are under a mistake as to a matter of fact essential to the agreement, the agreement is void. Explanation: an erroneous opinion as to the value of the thing which forms the subject-matter of the agreement is not to be deemed a mistake as to a matter of fact.

Kinds of bilateral mistake:

  • As to existence of subject-matter (res extincta): illustration (a) to s.20, a horse already dead at the time of sale; Couturier v Hastie (1856), a cargo of corn already sold off.
  • As to identity of subject-matter: Raffles v Wichelhaus (1864), the two ships named Peerless.
  • As to title (res sua): Cooper v Phibbs (1867), a man leased a fishery that already belonged to him.
  • As to quality: only if it makes the thing essentially different from what was agreed. In Bell v Lever Bros (1932), a golden handshake paid under a shared mistake that the contracts could not be terminated without compensation was not void.
  • As to quantity or price, when essential.

Mistake of law

Statutes.21

A contract is not voidable because it was caused by a mistake as to any law in force in India; but a mistake as to a law not in force in India has the same effect as a mistake of fact.

The maxim is ignorantia juris non excusat. But money paid under a mistake of law can still be recovered under s.72 (Unit 9).

Unilateral mistake

Statutes.22

A contract is not voidable merely because it was caused by one of the parties being under a mistake as to a matter of fact.

Exceptions where unilateral mistake makes the agreement void:

  • Mistake as to the identity of the other party, where identity is vital.
CaseCundy v Lindsay (1878)

A rogue named Blenkarn ordered goods by letter, signing so as to look like Blenkiron and Co, a reputable firm. The sellers meant to deal only with Blenkiron. Held: no contract; the rogue got no title and could pass none to an innocent buyer.

  • Contrast face-to-face dealings, where the seller is presumed to intend to deal with the person present, so the contract is voidable for fraud, not void: Phillips v Brooks (1919); Lewis v Averay (1972). The House of Lords confirmed the written-contract rule in Shogun Finance v Hudson (2003).
  • Mistake as to the nature of the document (non est factum): a person who signs a document fundamentally different from what he thought, without negligence, is not bound: Foster v Mackinnon (1869). The plea is narrow: Saunders v Anglia Building Society (1971).
Exam tip

In a problem, first ask whether there was any consent (if not, there is no agreement), then which factor caused it, then the section that gives the effect, then any exception (ordinary diligence, s.19A terms, restitution under s.64 or s.65).

Points to remember

  • s.14 lists five factors; consent is "caused" if it would not have been given otherwise.
  • Coercion is wide: any person, any victim, penal-code acts or unlawful detention of property (Amiraju).
  • Undue influence: domination, use of position, unconscionable bargain; burden shifts under s.16(3) (Raghunath Prasad).
  • Pardanashin women get special protection (Kali Bakhsh Singh).
  • Fraud needs knowledge of falsity (Derry v Peek); silence is fraud only where there is a duty to speak or silence is equivalent to speech.
  • Bilateral mistake of fact makes the agreement void (s.20); mistake of Indian law or unilateral mistake does not, except mistake as to identity or non est factum.

Legality of object and void agreements

Even a freely made bargain fails if its object or consideration is unlawful, or if the Act expressly declares that kind of agreement void.

Section 23: when consideration or object is unlawful

Statutes.23

The consideration or object of an agreement is lawful, unless it is forbidden by law; or is of such a nature that, if permitted, it would defeat the provisions of any law; or is fraudulent; or involves or implies injury to the person or property of another; or the court regards it as immoral, or opposed to public policy. Every agreement of which the object or consideration is unlawful is void.

1. Forbidden by law

An act punishable by statute, or prohibited by rules having the force of law. Example: an agreement to sell liquor without the licence required by law, or to trade in goods banned by import control.

CaseMannalal Khetan v Kedar Nath Khetan (1977)

A transfer of shares made in contravention of a statutory provision that was prohibitory and mandatory (carrying a penalty) was held void. Where a statute imposes a penalty, it implies a prohibition, and an agreement in breach of it is void.

2. Would defeat the provisions of any law

The act is not itself prohibited, but allowing the agreement would circumvent a statute. Example: an agreement by a debtor not to plead limitation, or a benami arrangement designed to defeat land-ceiling or tax laws; illustration (f) to s.23, a promise to drop a prosecution for robbery in return for restoration of goods.

3. Fraudulent

An agreement to defraud third parties or the public. Illustration (h): A, B and C agree to divide gains acquired by them by fraud. Scott v Brown, Doering, McNab and Co (1892): an agreement to buy shares to create a false premium on the stock exchange was unenforceable.

4. Injury to person or property of another

An agreement to commit a tort or crime, or to cause loss to another. Agreements creating bonded labour, or for a loan repayable by personal servitude, fall here and are also prohibited by Article 23 of the Constitution and the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976.

5. Immoral

CaseGherulal Parakh v Mahadeodas Maiya (1959)

The Supreme Court held that "immoral" in s.23 is confined to sexual morality and must be judged by the prevailing standards of the time. It should not be extended to cover every agreement against general morals; wagering was not "immoral" in this sense.

Example: letting a house or carriage to a prostitute for her trade, if the lessor knows the purpose (Pearce v Brooks, 1866). Agreements for future separation of husband and wife are also treated as immoral or opposed to public policy.

6. Opposed to public policy

Public policy is "an unruly horse" (Burrough J, Richardson v Mellish, 1824). Courts apply it with care. Recognised heads:

  • trading with the enemy;
  • stifling prosecution of non-compoundable offences;
  • traffic in public offices and titles, or to influence public servants;
  • marriage brokerage (payments for procuring a marriage);
  • agreements interfering with parental duty or the course of justice (maintenance and champerty in England; in India, champertous agreements are void only if extortionate or unconscionable);
  • unconscionable terms between parties of unequal bargaining power.
CaseCentral Inland Water Transport Corporation v Brojo Nath Ganguly (1986)

Public policy is not static. An unconscionable term in a contract between unequal parties, such as termination of permanent service by notice, is void under s.23 as opposed to public policy.

Partial illegality

Statutes.24

If any part of a single consideration for one or more objects, or any one or any part of any one of several considerations for a single object, is unlawful, the agreement is void.

The legal part survives only if it can be severed (compare s.57, reciprocal promises to do legal and illegal things: the legal part is enforceable).

Effects of illegality

  • The agreement is void; neither party can enforce it.
  • In pari delicto potior est conditio defendentis: where both parties are equally at fault, the position of the defendant is stronger, so money paid cannot be recovered.
  • Ex turpi causa non oritur actio: no action arises from a base cause.
  • Exceptions: the plaintiff was not equally guilty (for example, coerced or deceived), or repented before the illegal purpose was carried out.
  • Collateral transactions to an illegal agreement are also void.

Restraint of marriage: s.26

Statutes.26

Every agreement in restraint of the marriage of any person, other than a minor, is void.

A promise not to marry at all, or not to marry anyone except a particular person, is void (Lowe v Peers, 1768). Indian law, unlike English law, voids even partial restraints. Restraints on remarriage of widows found in some customs are not saved by contract.

Restraint of trade: s.27

Statutes.27

Every agreement by which anyone is restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business of any kind is to that extent void. Exception 1: one who sells the goodwill of a business may agree with the buyer to refrain from carrying on a similar business within specified local limits, so long as the buyer or his successor carries on a like business there, provided the limits are reasonable.

CaseMadhub Chunder v Rajcoomar Doss (1874)

Two shopkeepers agreed that one would close his shop in a locality in return for compensation. The Calcutta High Court held the agreement void, even though the restraint was partial. Under s.27 any restraint, total or partial, is void unless it falls within an exception.

This differs from English law, where reasonable restraints are valid (Nordenfelt v Maxim Nordenfelt, 1894).

Exceptions and qualifications

  • Sale of goodwill: s.27 Exception 1, within reasonable local limits.
  • Partnership Act, 1932: a partner may agree not to carry on competing business during the partnership (s.11(2)); an outgoing partner (s.36(2)), partners on dissolution (s.54) and a seller of goodwill of the firm (s.55(3)) may agree to reasonable restraints.
  • Restraint during employment: a negative covenant operating while the contract subsists is valid.
CaseNiranjan Shankar Golikari v Century Spinning and Manufacturing Co (1967)

An employee agreed to serve exclusively for five years and not to work elsewhere during that period. The Supreme Court upheld the restraint because it operated during the term of employment; restraints operating after termination are generally void.

CaseSuperintendence Company of India v Krishan Murgai (1981)

A clause restraining an employee from serving a competitor for two years after leaving was held void under s.27.

CasePercept D'Mark (India) v Zaheer Khan (2006)

A right-of-first-refusal clause operating after expiry of a cricketer's management contract was held void as a restraint of trade under s.27.

  • Trade combinations: agreements regulating business among traders (for example, pooling or price fixing) were sometimes upheld as not restraining trade (Fraser and Co v Bombay Ice Manufacturing Co, 1904), but anti-competitive agreements are now governed by s.3 of the Competition Act, 2002.
  • Exclusive dealing: a trader agreeing to deal only with one supplier during the contract is valid if it promotes trade: Gujarat Bottling Co v Coca Cola Co (1995).

Restraint of legal proceedings: s.28

Statutes.28

Every agreement (a) by which a party is restricted absolutely from enforcing his rights under or in respect of any contract by the usual legal proceedings in the ordinary tribunals, or which limits the time within which he may thus enforce his rights; or (b) which extinguishes the rights of any party, or discharges any party from any liability, on the expiry of a specified period so as to restrict any party from enforcing his rights, is void to that extent.

Clause (b) was added by an amendment of 1997 to overcome decisions that had upheld clauses extinguishing rights after a short period.

Exceptions:

  • Exception 1: agreement to refer future disputes to arbitration.
  • Exception 2: agreement to refer existing disputes to arbitration.
  • Exception 3 (added 2013): a guarantee agreement of a bank or financial institution extinguishing rights or discharging liability on expiry of a specified period of not less than one year from the date of occurrence or non-occurrence of a specified event.
  • Exclusive jurisdiction clauses: parties may choose one of two or more courts that already have jurisdiction; this is not an absolute restraint: A.B.C. Laminart v A.P. Agencies (1989); Swastik Gases v Indian Oil Corporation (2013). They cannot confer jurisdiction on a court that has none.

Uncertain agreements: s.29

Statutes.29

Agreements the meaning of which is not certain, or capable of being made certain, are void.

Illustrations: A agrees to sell B "a hundred tons of oil" (void, the kind is uncertain), but if A deals only in coconut oil, the meaning is capable of being made certain. An agreement to sell a car "on hire-purchase terms" was void for uncertainty in Scammell v Ouston (1941). An agreement to agree in future is not a contract.

Wagering agreements: s.30

Statutes.30

Agreements by way of wager are void; and no suit shall be brought for recovering anything alleged to be won on any wager, or entrusted to any person to abide the result of any game or other uncertain event. Exception: subscriptions or contributions towards any plate, prize or sum of money of the value of five hundred rupees or upwards, to be awarded to the winner of any horse race, are not unlawful.

Essentials (from Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball, Hawkins J)

  • A promise to pay money or money's worth.
  • On the determination of an uncertain future event (or past event unknown to the parties).
  • Mutual chances of gain and loss: each party may win or lose.
  • Neither party has control over the event.
  • Neither has any interest in the event other than the stake.

Wagers distinguished

  • Insurance: the insured has an insurable interest; the contract is one of indemnity (except life). Not a wager.
  • Commercial transactions: genuine sales for future delivery are valid; a transaction where both intend only to pay differences in price is a wager.
  • Games of skill: competitions where success depends substantially on skill are not wagers.
CaseGherulal Parakh v Mahadeodas Maiya (1959)

Two parties agreed to enter wagering contracts with third parties and share profits and losses. Held: a wagering agreement is void but not illegal (except in states such as Gujarat and Maharashtra, where it is made illegal by statute). A collateral agreement, such as a partnership to carry on wagering, is not void and can be enforced.

CaseDr K.R. Lakshmanan v State of Tamil Nadu (1996)

Betting on horse racing was held to be a game of mere skill, not gambling, because skill in assessing horses, jockeys and form predominates.

Current law

Online money games are now regulated by the Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025, which prohibits offering online money games. Agreements to stake money on such games would be void under s.30 and also forbidden by law under s.23.

Exam tip

Remember the contrast that examiners like: in English law, reasonable restraints of trade are valid; in India, s.27 voids all restraints except the statutory exceptions, though courts accept negative covenants during employment.

Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseVijaya Bank v Prashant B. Narnaware (2025)

A clause requiring a newly recruited officer to serve a minimum period, or pay liquidated damages for leaving early, was held not to be a restraint of trade under s.27. It operates only during employment and protects a legitimate interest of the employer.

Points to remember

  • Minimum-service bonds with liquidated damages are not a restraint of trade under s.27 (Vijaya Bank, 2025).
  • s.23 lists six heads of unlawfulness; immoral means sexual immorality (Gherulal Parakh); public policy can strike down unconscionable terms (Brojo Nath Ganguly).
  • Illegality taints collateral transactions; mere voidness (as in wagers) does not.
  • s.27 voids partial restraints too (Madhub Chunder); exceptions are sale of goodwill and Partnership Act provisions; restraint during employment is valid (Niranjan Golikari).
  • s.28 voids clauses barring or time-limiting legal proceedings; arbitration and bank guarantee clauses are saved; exclusive jurisdiction clauses are valid.
  • Wagering agreements are void, not illegal; horse-race prizes of ₹500 or more are excepted.

Unit 8

Discharge of a contract

A contract ends when its obligations are performed, excused, broken, released by agreement or barred by time.

What the syllabus lists for this unit

  • reciprocal promises
  • time as the essence
  • By breach: anticipatory and present

  • By period of limitation
  • By agreement: rescission, alteration, remission, waiver, extension of time, accord and satisfaction

Modes of discharge

  1. Performance (actual or attempted)
  2. Agreement (novation, rescission, alteration, remission, waiver)
  3. Impossibility (frustration)
  4. Lapse of time (limitation)
  5. Operation of law (death in personal contracts, insolvency, merger, unauthorised material alteration)
  6. Breach (actual or anticipatory)

8.1 Performance

Statutes.37

The parties to a contract must either perform, or offer to perform, their respective promises, unless performance is dispensed with or excused under the Act or any other law. Promises bind the representatives of the promisor in case of his death, unless a contrary intention appears.

Tender (offer to perform): s.38

A valid tender, if refused, discharges the promisor from further performance (but not from a debt, which remains payable). Conditions:

  • unconditional;
  • made at a proper time and place;
  • under such circumstances that the promisee has a reasonable opportunity of ascertaining that the person offering is able and willing to do the whole of what he is bound to do;
  • of the whole obligation, not part;
  • to the promisee or his authorised agent;
  • if goods, giving a reasonable opportunity to inspect;
  • of money, in the exact amount and in legal tender.

Who must perform? ss.40 to 45

  • No time specified: within a reasonable time (s.46).
  • Specific day, no hour: during usual business hours at the usual place (s.47).
  • Application by promisee needed: at a proper time and place (s.48).
  • No place fixed and no application needed: promisor must apply to promisee to appoint a reasonable place (s.49).
  • In the manner and at the time prescribed or sanctioned by the promisee (s.50).

Reciprocal promises: ss.51 to 58

  • Promises to be performed simultaneously: the promisor need not perform unless the promisee is ready and willing (s.51).
  • Order of performance as fixed by the contract or by the nature of the transaction (s.52).
  • Where one party prevents the other from performing, the contract becomes voidable at the option of the party prevented (s.53).
  • Where one promise cannot be performed until the other is performed, the party who fails first cannot claim performance (s.54).
  • Reciprocal promises to do legal and illegal things: the legal part is enforceable (s.57).
  • Alternative promise, one branch illegal: only the legal branch can be enforced (s.58).

Time as the essence: s.55

Statutes.55

When a party fails to perform at the fixed time and the intention was that time should be of the essence, the contract becomes voidable at the option of the promisee. If time was not essential, the contract does not become voidable, but the promisee is entitled to compensation for loss. If the promisee accepts late performance, he cannot claim compensation unless he gives notice at the time of acceptance.

Whether time is of the essence depends on the express terms, the nature of the contract and surrounding circumstances.

  • In mercantile contracts time is ordinarily of the essence.
  • In contracts for sale of immovable property, the presumption is that time is not of the essence: Chand Rani v Kamal Rani (1993) (Constitution Bench).
  • In building and construction contracts, time is generally not of the essence where there are provisions for extension of time and liquidated damages: Hind Construction Contractors v State of Maharashtra (1979).

Appropriation of payments: ss.59 to 61

Where a debtor owes several debts and pays without full discharge of all: the debtor's express or implied intention governs (s.59); failing that the creditor may appropriate to any lawful debt, even a time-barred one (s.60); failing both, the payment goes to debts in order of time (s.61).

8.2 Breach

Actual (present) breach

Failure to perform when performance is due, or performance that is defective.

Anticipatory breach

Statutes.39

When a party has refused to perform, or disabled himself from performing, his promise in its entirety, the promisee may put an end to the contract, unless he has signified by words or conduct his acquiescence in its continuance.

CaseHochster v De la Tour (1853)

The defendant engaged the plaintiff as a courier from 1 June, then wrote in May repudiating. The plaintiff sued at once. Held: he could sue immediately without waiting for 1 June.

The innocent party has an election:

  • Accept the repudiation, treat the contract as ended and sue at once; damages are measured by reference to the date fixed for performance, subject to the duty to mitigate.
  • Keep the contract alive and wait; but then the contract continues for the benefit of both, and any intervening event (such as frustration) may also benefit the defaulter: Avery v Bowden (1855), where the outbreak of the Crimean War frustrated the contract before the date for performance, so the shipowner lost his claim.

8.3 Impossibility of performance (frustration)

Statutes.56

An agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void. A contract to do an act which, after the contract is made, becomes impossible, or, by reason of some event which the promisor could not prevent, unlawful, becomes void when the act becomes impossible or unlawful. Where one person has promised to do something which he knew, or with reasonable diligence might have known, and which the promisee did not know, to be impossible or unlawful, the promisor must compensate the promisee for any loss.

Initial and supervening impossibility

  • Initial: known to both, the agreement is void (s.56 para 1); unknown to both, void as mistake (s.20); known only to promisor, compensation (s.56 para 3).
  • Supervening: the contract becomes void (s.56 para 2). This is the Indian statutory form of frustration.

Meaning of "impossible"

CaseSatyabrata Ghose v Mugneeram Bangur and Co (1954)

A developer agreed to sell plots and make roads; the land was temporarily requisitioned during the Second World War. The Supreme Court held: (1) "impossible" in s.56 is not used in the sense of physical or literal impossibility; performance may be impracticable and useless from the point of view of the object the parties had in view; (2) s.56 lays down a positive rule of law and is exhaustive in India, so English theories are only of persuasive value; (3) on the facts the requisition was temporary and the contract was not frustrated.

  • Failure of one of several objects: Herne Bay Steam Boat Co v Hutton (1903), where the naval review was cancelled but the cruise round the fleet was still possible.

A rise in the price of imported coal due to change in Indonesian law did not frustrate power purchase agreements. Where a contract has a force majeure clause, the matter is governed by s.32 (contingent contracts) and the clause; s.56 applies only to events outside the contract.

Application to leases

CaseRaja Dhruv Dev Chand v Raja Harmohinder Singh (1968)

The Supreme Court held that s.56 does not apply to a lease, because a lease is a completed conveyance of an interest in property, not a mere executory contract. Relief is under s.108(B)(e) of the Transfer of Property Act, which lets the lessee avoid the lease if the property is wholly destroyed or rendered substantially unfit by fire, tempest, flood, violence of an army or mob, or other irresistible force.

Theories of frustration (English law)

  • Implied term theory: the parties impliedly agreed that the contract would end if the foundation disappeared (Blackburn J in Taylor v Caldwell).
  • Foundation of contract: the basis on which both contracted has disappeared (Krell v Henry).
  • Just and reasonable solution: the court imposes a fair result (Lord Wright).
  • Radical change in the obligation: performance would be a thing radically different from that undertaken (Davis Contractors v Fareham UDC, 1956, Lord Radcliffe). This is now the accepted English test.

India: s.56 is exhaustive and the radical-change idea is used in applying it (Satyabrata Ghose; Naihati Jute Mills v Khyaliram Jagannath, 1968).

Effect of frustration and restitution

  • The contract becomes void from the moment of impossibility; future obligations end.
Statutes.65

When an agreement is discovered to be void, or a contract becomes void, any person who has received any advantage under it is bound to restore it, or to make compensation for it, to the person from whom he received it.

  • So money advanced or benefits received must be returned. English law reached the same result through the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act, 1943, after Fibrosa v Fairbairn (1943).
Current law

During the COVID-19 lockdowns, courts examined force majeure clauses first and s.56 second. In Halliburton Offshore Services v Vedanta (Delhi High Court, 2020) the court said the pandemic could not be a blanket excuse for non-performance that began before it.

8.4 Period of limitation

  • The Limitation Act, 1963 bars the remedy, not the right. A time-barred debt remains a debt: it can be paid, adjusted by the creditor under s.60, or revived by a written promise under s.25(3).
  • For breach of contract, the period is generally three years from the date of breach (Article 55 of the Schedule).

8.5 Discharge by agreement

Statutes.62

If the parties agree to substitute a new contract for it, or to rescind or alter it, the original contract need not be performed.

  • Novation: substitution of a new contract between the same or different parties. It must be agreed before the original is broken.
  • Rescission: cancellation of the contract by agreement (also s.64, rescission of voidable contracts).
  • Alteration: a change of terms by mutual consent; the old terms are discharged.
Statutes.63

Every promisee may dispense with or remit, wholly or in part, the performance of the promise made to him, or may extend the time for such performance, or may accept instead of it any satisfaction which he thinks fit.

  • Remission: accepting less than what was due. Unlike English law (Pinnel's Case, 1602; Foakes v Beer, 1884), in India no consideration is needed for remission: illustration (b) to s.63, A owes ₹5,000 and B accepts ₹2,000 in full satisfaction.
  • Waiver: abandonment of a right, expressly or by conduct.
  • Extension of time.
  • Accord and satisfaction: accepting something different in full discharge of the obligation (the accord is the agreement, the satisfaction is the consideration).
  • Merger: an inferior right merges into a superior one, for example an oral agreement into a written deed.
Exam tip

For frustration questions in India always quote s.56, say Satyabrata Ghose made it exhaustive, apply the "radical change" test, list what is not frustration (higher cost, self-induced), and end with s.65 restitution. If the contract has a force majeure clause, mention Energy Watchdog and s.32.

Points to remember

  • Valid tender (s.38) discharges the promisor except for debts.
  • Time is presumed not to be essential in sales of land (Chand Rani) and construction contracts (Hind Construction).
  • Anticipatory breach lets the promisee sue at once (s.39; Hochster v De la Tour), but if he keeps the contract alive he takes the risk of later events (Avery v Bowden).
  • s.56 is exhaustive; "impossible" includes impracticable from the point of view of the object (Satyabrata Ghose); higher cost is not frustration (Alopi Parshad).
  • s.56 does not apply to leases (Dhruv Dev Chand); force majeure clauses come under s.32 (Energy Watchdog).
  • s.63 allows remission without consideration, unlike Pinnel's rule in England.

Unit 9

Quasi-contracts: certain relations resembling contract

The law imposes an obligation to restore benefits that it would be unjust to keep, even though no one promised anything.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Basis of quasi-contractual obligation
  • Necessaries supplied to incapable persons
  • Payment by an interested person
  • Benefit of non-gratuitous act
  • Finder of goods
  • Money paid by mistake or under coercion

Nature and basis

Chapter V of the Act (ss.68 to 72) is headed "Of certain relations resembling those created by contract". These obligations do not arise from agreement but from the principle of unjust enrichment: no one should be enriched at the expense of another without legal justification.

CaseMoses v Macferlan (1760)

Lord Mansfield explained that the action for money had and received lies where the defendant is obliged by the ties of natural justice and equity to refund the money.

CaseFibrosa Spolka Akcyjna v Fairbairn Lawson Combe Barbour (1943)

Lord Wright said that any civilised system of law is bound to provide remedies for cases of unjust enrichment, that is, to prevent a man from retaining money or a benefit derived from another which it is against conscience that he should keep.

English law once explained these obligations by an "implied contract" fiction. Indian law treats them as statutory obligations under ss.68 to 72. The remedy is compensation under the last paragraph of s.73.

The five quasi-contracts

1. Necessaries supplied to an incapable person: s.68

A person who supplies necessaries suited to the condition in life of a minor or person of unsound mind, or of anyone whom he is legally bound to support, is entitled to be reimbursed from the property of the incapable person (Unit 5). There is no personal liability.

2. Payment by an interested person: s.69

Statutes.69

A person who is interested in the payment of money which another is bound by law to pay, and who therefore pays it, is entitled to be reimbursed by the other.

Elements: the plaintiff was interested in making the payment (to protect his own interest), the defendant was bound by law to pay, and the plaintiff paid. Illustration: B holds land on lease from A, the zamindar; the revenue payable by A to the government is in arrears and the land is advertised for sale. B pays to prevent the sale and can recover from A.

3. Obligation to pay for a non-gratuitous act: s.70

Statutes.70

Where a person lawfully does anything for another person, or delivers anything to him, not intending to do so gratuitously, and such other person enjoys the benefit thereof, the latter is bound to make compensation to the former, or to restore the thing so done or delivered.

CaseState of West Bengal v B.K. Mondal and Sons (1962)

A contractor built a kitchen and other structures for the State at the request of an officer, but there was no valid contract because Article 299 (formalities of government contracts) was not complied with. The State used the buildings. The Supreme Court held the State liable under s.70. Three conditions: (1) the act was done lawfully; (2) it was not intended to be gratuitous; (3) the other person enjoyed the benefit. The State had a choice to accept or reject the work, and having accepted it must pay.

CaseMulamchand v State of Madhya Pradesh (1968)

Even where a contract with the government is void for non-compliance with Article 299, a person who supplied goods or services can claim compensation under s.70 if the government accepted the benefit.

4. Finder of goods: s.71

Statutes.71

A person who finds goods belonging to another and takes them into his custody is subject to the same responsibility as a bailee.

The finder:

  • must take reasonable care of the goods (s.151);
  • must try to find the owner;
  • may retain the goods against everyone except the true owner (Armory v Delamirie, 1722);
  • may sue for a specific reward offered by the owner and may retain the goods until he receives it (s.168);
  • may sell the goods in specified cases, for example when they are perishable or the lawful charges amount to two-thirds of their value (s.169).

5. Money paid by mistake or under coercion: s.72

Statutes.72

A person to whom money has been paid, or anything delivered, by mistake or under coercion, must repay or return it.

CaseSales Tax Officer, Banaras v Kanhaiya Lal Mukundlal Saraf (1959)

A firm paid sales tax on forward contracts that was later held unconstitutional. The Supreme Court held that "mistake" in s.72 includes mistake of law as well as of fact, so the tax was refundable. There is no distinction between a mistake of law and of fact for this purpose, and s.21 does not limit s.72.

  • "Coercion" in s.72 is used in its general sense, not limited to s.15 (Seth Kanhaya Lal, Unit 6).
  • Defences: the payee has changed position in good faith, or the payer has passed on the burden to others (in tax refund cases, the doctrine of unjust enrichment bars refund where the tax was passed on to consumers: Mafatlal Industries v Union of India, 1997).

Quantum meruit

"As much as he has earned." Where a contract is discharged or void, a party who has done part of the work can recover reasonable payment for what he has done, if the other party has taken the benefit.

CasePlanche v Colburn (1831)

An author was engaged to write a book for a periodical; the publishers abandoned the periodical. He recovered a reasonable sum for the work done, on quantum meruit.

Quantum meruit applies where: the contract is discovered to be void (s.65); the contract is discharged by the defendant's breach; or work is done under an agreement that does not fix the price. It does not help a party who has himself abandoned the contract half-way where the contract was entire (Sumpter v Hedges, 1898), unless the other voluntarily accepted the benefit.

10.1 Damages

Statutes.73

When a contract has been broken, the party who suffers by the breach is entitled to receive, from the party who has broken it, compensation for any loss or damage caused to him thereby, which naturally arose in the usual course of things from such breach, or which the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result from the breach. Such compensation is not to be given for any remote and indirect loss or damage sustained by reason of the breach. Explanation: in estimating the loss, the means which existed of remedying the inconvenience caused by non-performance must be taken into account.

Remoteness: the rule in Hadley v Baxendale

CaseHadley v Baxendale (1854)

A mill's crankshaft broke and was sent for repair through carriers, who delayed delivery. The mill stood idle. The carriers were not told the mill had no spare shaft. Held: loss of profits was not recoverable. Damages are recoverable (1) for loss arising naturally, in the usual course of things, from the breach; or (2) for loss that may reasonably be supposed to have been in the contemplation of both parties at the time of contracting as the probable result of breach. Section 73 embodies both limbs.

CaseVictoria Laundry v Newman Industries (1949)

A boiler was delivered late. The laundry recovered ordinary profits it would have made, which the sellers must have known about, but not exceptionally lucrative dyeing contracts the sellers did not know of.

CaseKoufos v C. Czarnikow, The Heron II (1969)

The House of Lords held that the test in contract is stricter than in tort: loss is recoverable if it was "not unlikely" to result, or a "real danger" or "serious possibility", in the contemplation of the parties.

Kinds of damages

  • Ordinary (general): loss arising naturally; for sale of goods, the difference between contract price and market price on the date of breach.
  • Special: loss from special circumstances communicated at the time of contracting.
  • Nominal: a token sum where a right is infringed but no loss is proved.
  • Exemplary (punitive): not normally awarded in contract (Addis v Gramophone Co, 1909, no damages for injured feelings on wrongful dismissal). Exceptions recognised: breach of promise of marriage; and in consumer law, compensation for harassment and mental agony (Ghaziabad Development Authority v Balbir Singh, 2004).
  • Damages for inconvenience and discomfort: allowed where physical inconvenience is caused, for example a ruined holiday (Jarvis v Swans Tours, 1973).

Duty to mitigate

The Explanation to s.73 requires the plaintiff to take reasonable steps to reduce his loss. He cannot recover for loss he could reasonably have avoided (British Westinghouse v Underground Electric Railways, 1912).

Liquidated damages and penalties: s.74

Statutes.74

When a contract has been broken, if a sum is named in the contract as the amount to be paid in case of such breach, or if the contract contains any other stipulation by way of penalty, the party complaining of the breach is entitled, whether or not actual damage or loss is proved to have been caused, to receive from the party who has broken the contract reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount so named or the penalty stipulated.

  • English law distinguishes a genuine pre-estimate of loss (enforceable) from a penalty (unenforceable). Indian law does away with the distinction: in both cases the court awards reasonable compensation up to the named sum.
CaseFateh Chand v Balkishan Das (1963)

The Supreme Court held that s.74 cuts across the English distinction between liquidated damages and penalties. The court will award reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount stated, whatever the label.

CaseONGC Ltd v Saw Pipes Ltd (2003)

Where the parties have pre-estimated damages and the loss is difficult to prove, the pre-estimate is presumed reasonable and can be awarded without proof of actual loss.

CaseKailash Nath Associates v Delhi Development Authority (2015)

Reasonable compensation is the ceiling, and where actual loss can be proved, it must be proved. Where no loss is caused at all, nothing is payable; the phrase "whether or not actual damage or loss is proved" only dispenses with proof where loss is difficult or impossible to prove.

s.75: compensation on rescission

A person who rightfully rescinds a contract is entitled to compensation for any damage sustained through non-fulfilment of the contract.

10.2 to 10.4 Specific performance

Specific performance is an equitable remedy: the court orders the defendant to do what he promised. The Specific Relief Act, 1963 was significantly amended by the Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018.

Personal bars to relief: s.16

Specific performance cannot be enforced in favour of a person:

  • who has obtained substituted performance under s.20;

10.6 Rescission: ss.27 to 30, SRA

A party may sue to have a contract rescinded where it is voidable or terminable by the plaintiff, or unlawful for causes not apparent on its face and the defendant is more to blame. Rescission is refused where the plaintiff has ratified the contract, where parties cannot be substantially restored to their original position, where third parties have acquired rights in good faith for value, or where only part of the contract is sought to be rescinded and that part is not severable (s.27(2)).

10.7 Cancellation of instruments: ss.31 to 33, SRA

Statutes.31

Any person against whom a written instrument is void or voidable, and who has reasonable apprehension that such instrument, if left outstanding, may cause him serious injury, may sue to have it adjudged void or voidable, and the court may in its discretion so adjudge it and order it to be delivered up and cancelled.

If the instrument is registered, a copy of the decree is sent to the registering officer. Partial cancellation is possible (s.32). On cancellation, the court may require the plaintiff to restore benefits (s.33).

10.8 Declaratory decrees: ss.34 and 35, SRA

Statutes.34

Any person entitled to any legal character, or to any right as to any property, may institute a suit against any person denying or interested to deny his title to such character or right, and the court may in its discretion declare that he is so entitled. Proviso: no court shall make such a declaration where the plaintiff, being able to seek further relief than a mere declaration of title, omits to do so.

A declaration binds only the parties and those claiming through them (s.35).

10.9 Preventive relief: s.36, SRA

Preventive relief is granted at the discretion of the court by injunction, temporary or perpetual. It prevents a wrong rather than compensating for it.

10.10 Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseGodrej Projects Development Ltd v Anil Karlekar (2025)

Forfeiture of earnest money on cancellation of a flat booking must be reasonable. A one-sided clause forfeiting an excessive amount operates as a penalty under s.74; only a reasonable deposit falls outside s.74 (applying Maula Bux).

CaseBPL Ltd v Morgan Securities and Credits Pvt Ltd (2025)

In a dispute over a high contractual interest rate agreed between sophisticated commercial parties, the Court stressed party autonomy and asked whether the stipulation protected a legitimate interest, distinguishing the older approach in Fateh Chand.

CaseSaisudhir Energy Ltd v NTPC Vidyut Vyapar Nigam Ltd (2026)

In public utility projects (here, solar power under the National Solar Mission), where loss is difficult to prove, the employer need not prove actual loss before claiming liquidated damages under s.74. An appellate court under s.37 of the Arbitration Act cannot re-work the 'reasonable compensation' fixed by the tribunal.

CaseMohammed Khaleel v Jayamma (2026)

For specific performance, readiness (financial capacity) and willingness (conduct) must be shown continuously from the date of the agreement to the decree. Bank deposits made after filing the suit do not prove readiness for the earlier period.

Points to remember

  • s.74: reasonableness of forfeiture (Godrej, 2025); no proof of loss in public utility contracts (Saisudhir, 2026); continuous readiness for specific performance (Mohammed Khaleel, 2026).
  • Damages under s.73 cover natural and contemplated losses, not remote ones (Hadley v Baxendale); the plaintiff must mitigate.
  • s.74: reasonable compensation up to the named sum; no penalty distinction (Fateh Chand); actual loss must be proved where possible (Kailash Nath).
  • After 2018, specific performance is the rule, not a discretion; substituted performance under s.20 is new; the amendment is prospective (Katta Sujatha Reddy).
  • s.16(c): plaintiff must prove continuous readiness and willingness.
  • Negative covenants can be enforced by injunction under s.42 (Lumley v Wagner).
  • Rescission, cancellation and declarations are distinct remedies under the SRA.

1.Invitation to treat
Model answer

An invitation to treat invites others to make offers; it is not itself an offer, so a "yes" to it does not create a contract.

  • Examples: goods displayed in a shop (Pharmaceutical Society v Boots, 1953), price lists, catalogues, advertisements, tenders and auction notices.
  • In Harvey v Facey (1893), a reply stating the lowest price was only information, not an offer.
  • The person who responds makes the offer (s.2(a)), and the other side is free to accept or reject it.

Importance: it fixes who the proposer is, and therefore when the contract is concluded.

2.Privity of contract
Model answer

Only parties to a contract can sue on it (Tweddle v Atkinson, 1861; Dunlop v Selfridge, 1915). In India, consideration may move from a third person (s.2(d); Chinnaya v Ramayya, 1882), but a stranger to the contract still cannot sue.

Exceptions:

  • trusts or charges created in favour of a beneficiary (Khwaja Muhammad Khan v Husaini Begum, 1910);
  • family arrangements and marriage settlements;
  • acknowledgment or estoppel by the promisor;
  • assignment of rights and agency;
  • covenants running with land.
3.Doctrine of frustration
Model answer

Section 56 says a contract to do an act that afterwards becomes impossible or unlawful becomes void.

  • "Impossible" includes impracticable and useless from the point of view of the object the parties had in mind (Satyabrata Ghose v Mugneeram Bangur, 1954).
  • The supervening event must be beyond the parties' control, and not self-induced.
  • Mere increase in cost or difficulty does not frustrate a contract (Naihati Jute Mills v Hyaliram Jagannath, 1968).
  • Effect: the contract becomes void, and any benefit received must be restored under s.65.
4.Undue influence
Model answer

Section 16 defines it. It exists where:

  • one party is in a position to dominate the will of the other, through real or apparent authority, a fiduciary relation, or the other's mental or bodily distress; and
  • that party uses the position to obtain an unfair advantage.

Presumed relationships include parent and child, guardian and ward, doctor and patient, and spiritual adviser and disciple. Where the transaction appears unconscionable, the burden shifts to the dominant party to prove there was no undue influence (s.16(3)).

  • Effect: the contract is voidable at the option of the influenced party.
  • The court may set it aside absolutely or on just terms (s.19A).
5.Agreements in restraint of trade
Model answer

Under s.27, every agreement restraining anyone from carrying on a lawful profession, trade or business is void to that extent. The only statutory exception is a sale of goodwill, which allows a reasonable local restraint.

  • Restraints operating during employment are valid; post-employment restraints are void (Niranjan Shankar Golikari v Century Spinning, 1967; Percept D'Mark v Zaheer Khan, 2006).
  • In Vijaya Bank v Prashant B. Narnaware (2025), a minimum-service bond enforced by liquidated damages was held not to be a restraint of trade, because it operates only during employment.
6.Quantum meruit
Model answer

"As much as is earned." A person who has done work under a contract that is later discharged or found void, or where no price was fixed, can recover a reasonable sum for what was done. Instances:

  • the contract is discharged by the other party's breach midway (Planche v Colburn, 1831);
  • the contract is void, and benefit was received under s.65 or s.70;
  • the other party accepts partial or substitute performance.

The claim is restitutionary: it compensates for a benefit conferred, and does not award the contract price.

7.Contingent contract
Model answer

A contingent contract is a contract to do or not to do something if some event, collateral to the contract, does or does not happen (s.31). Insurance and indemnity contracts are examples. Rules:

  • s.32: enforceable only when the uncertain event happens; void if it becomes impossible;
  • s.33: on the event not happening, enforceable once its happening becomes impossible;
  • s.34: an event depending on a person's future conduct becomes impossible when that person acts so as to make it impossible;
  • s.35: events within a fixed time;
  • s.36: contracts contingent on impossible events are void.
8.Standard form contracts
Model answer

These are printed contracts drafted by one party, usually a large enterprise, and offered on a "take it or leave it" basis. Examples are insurance policies, tickets, bank forms and builder–buyer agreements. Safeguards:

  • notice of the terms must be reasonable (Parker v South Eastern Railway, 1877);
  • the terms are construed against the drafter (contra proferentem);
  • unconscionable terms between unequal parties are void under s.23 (Central Inland Water Transport v Brojo Nath Ganguly, 1986);
  • one-sided builder clauses are unfair trade practices (Pioneer Urban Land v Govindan Raghavan, 2019).
9.Liquidated damages and penalty
Model answer

Section 74 abolishes the English distinction between liquidated damages and penalties. Where a contract names a sum, or contains a penalty stipulation, the injured party gets reasonable compensation not exceeding the sum named, whether or not actual loss is proved.

  • Fateh Chand v Balkishan Dass (1963) and Kailash Nath Associates v DDA (2015): loss is ordinarily needed, but proof can be dispensed with where it is difficult to prove.
  • Saisudhir Energy v NTPC Vidyut Vyapar Nigam (2026): in public utility projects, the employer need not prove actual loss.
  • Godrej Projects v Anil Karlekar (2025): an excessive forfeiture of earnest money is a penalty.

10.Define consideration. Discuss its essential elements and the exceptions to the rule "no consideration, no contract".
Model answer

Definition

Section 2(d): when, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or abstain from doing something, that act, abstinence or promise is called consideration for the promise. Consideration is the price paid for the promise. Under s.25, an agreement without consideration is void, subject to exceptions.

Essential elements

  1. At the desire of the promisor. An act done at a third person's request, or voluntarily, is not consideration (Durga Prasad v Baldeo, 1880).
  2. May move from the promisee or any other person. Indian law, unlike English law, allows consideration from a stranger (Chinnaya v Ramayya, 1882). A stranger to the contract still cannot sue (privity).
  3. May be past, present or future. Section 2(d) expressly recognises past consideration ("has done"), unlike English law.
  4. Must be real, but need not be adequate. Inadequacy does not void a contract (Explanation 2 to s.25), but it is evidence on whether consent was free. Illusory or impossible consideration is no consideration.
  5. Must not be something the promisor is already bound to do. Performance of an existing legal or public duty is not consideration.
  6. Must be lawful (s.23). It must not be forbidden by law, fraudulent, injurious to person or property, immoral or opposed to public policy.

Exceptions (s.25 and others)

  • Natural love and affection (s.25(1)): a written, registered agreement between near relatives (Rajlukhy Dabee v Bhootnath Mookerjee, 1900, where the promise failed for want of love and affection).
  • Compensation for past voluntary services (s.25(2)).
  • Promise to pay a time-barred debt (s.25(3)): it must be in writing and signed.
  • Completed gifts (Explanation 1 to s.25).
  • Agency: no consideration is needed to create an agency (s.185).
  • Bailment: gratuitous bailment (s.148).
  • Charity: where the promisee has acted on the promise and incurred liability (Kedarnath Bhattacharji v Gorie Mahomed, 1886).

Conclusion

Consideration in India is wider than in English law. It may move from anyone and may be past. The statutory exceptions, together with promissory estoppel in public law (Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v State of UP, 1979), soften the rigour of the rule.

11.Discuss the nature and effect of a minor's agreement.
Model answer

Capacity

Under s.11, a person is competent to contract if of the age of majority under the Majority Act, 1875 (18 years), of sound mind, and not disqualified by law.

Minor's agreement is void ab initio

In Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose (1903), the Privy Council held that a minor's agreement is absolutely void, not voidable. The facts: a minor mortgaged his property, and the lender's agent knew he was a minor. The mortgage was void, and the lender could not recover the advance.

Consequences

  1. No estoppel. A minor who misrepresents his age is not estopped from pleading minority; there can be no estoppel against a statute.
  2. No ratification. A minor cannot ratify the agreement on attaining majority; a fresh contract with fresh consideration is needed.
  3. Restitution. Under s.65 there is no "contract", so the section does not strictly apply (Mohori Bibee). Under ss.33 and 41 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, however, the court may direct the minor to restore benefits received when the minor seeks relief or sets up minority as a defence. Equity prevents a minor from using minority as a sword (Leslie v Sheill, 1914; Khan Gul v Lakha Singh, 1928).
  4. Necessaries. Under s.68, a person who supplies necessaries suited to the minor's condition in life can be reimbursed from the minor's property. The minor is not personally liable.
  5. Minor as promisee or beneficiary. A minor can be a promisee, an agent (s.184), a payee or endorsee of a negotiable instrument, or admitted to the benefits of a partnership (s.30, Partnership Act).
  6. Specific performance. A contract entered into by a guardian within his competence and for the minor's benefit may be enforced by or against the minor (Subramanyam v Subba Rao, 1948).
  7. Tort. A minor cannot be sued in tort where the tort is in substance a breach of contract.

Conclusion

The law protects minors from their own improvidence while preventing unjust enrichment through restitution and the necessaries rule.

12.What is free consent? Explain the factors that vitiate consent and their effect on a contract.
Model answer

Consent and free consent

Consent is agreeing upon the same thing in the same sense (consensus ad idem, s.13). Consent is free when it is not caused by coercion (s.15), undue influence (s.16), fraud (s.17), misrepresentation (s.18) or mistake (ss.20 to 22) (s.14). Free consent is an essential of a valid contract (s.10).

Coercion (s.15)

Committing or threatening any act forbidden by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, or unlawfully detaining or threatening to detain property, to make a person enter into an agreement. A threat to commit suicide was held to be coercion (Chikkam Ammiraju v Chikkam Seshamma, 1917). Effect: voidable (s.19). A person who receives money under coercion must repay it (s.72).

Undue influence (s.16)

Domination of will used to obtain an unfair advantage. There are presumptions in fiduciary relationships, and the burden lies on the dominant party for unconscionable bargains (s.16(3)). Effect: voidable; the court may set aside the contract on terms (s.19A).

Fraud (s.17)

Fraud means acts done with intent to deceive or to induce a contract: a false suggestion, active concealment, a promise made without intention to perform, or any act or omission declared fraudulent. Mere silence is not fraud unless there is a duty to speak or silence is equivalent to speech (Explanation). Effect: voidable, with damages; the party may insist on performance in the position they would have been in (s.19).

Misrepresentation (s.18)

An innocent false statement, a breach of duty that misleads, or causing a mistake about the subject matter. Effect: voidable, but the party cannot avoid the contract if they could have discovered the truth with ordinary diligence (exception to s.19).

Mistake

  • Bilateral mistake of fact essential to the agreement makes it void (s.20). Example: subject matter that has ceased to exist (Couturier v Hastie, 1856).
  • Mistake of law in force in India does not affect the contract (s.21).
  • Unilateral mistake does not make a contract voidable (s.22). The exception is mistake as to identity or nature of the document (non est factum) (Cundy v Lindsay, 1878; Lewis v Averay, 1971).

Conclusion

All the factors except mistake make the contract voidable at the option of the aggrieved party. Bilateral mistake makes it void. The aggrieved party must avoid the contract within a reasonable time and before third-party rights intervene.

13.Explain the remedies available for breach of contract.
Model answer

On breach, the injured party may treat the contract as ended and refuse further performance (s.39). The remedies are as follows.

1. Damages (s.73)

Compensation for loss or damage that naturally arose in the usual course of things from the breach, or that the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result from it (Hadley v Baxendale, 1854). Remote and indirect loss is not recoverable. The explanation to s.73 requires the plaintiff to mitigate loss.

  • Kinds of damages: ordinary, special (on notice), nominal, exemplary (rare; for example, wrongful dishonour of a cheque), and for mental distress in limited cases.
  • Market rule: in sale contracts, damages are the difference between the contract price and the market price.

2. Liquidated damages and penalty (s.74)

Reasonable compensation up to the stipulated sum. Kailash Nath (2015) requires loss to be shown unless it is hard to prove. Saisudhir (2026) dispenses with proof of actual loss in public utility projects. Godrej Projects (2025) holds that excessive forfeiture of earnest money is a penalty.

3. Specific performance (Specific Relief Act, 1963)

After the 2018 amendment, specific performance is enforced as a rule, not at discretion, subject to the bars in ss.11(2), 14 and 16.

  • The plaintiff must prove continuous readiness and willingness (s.16(c); Mohammed Khaleel v Jayamma, 2026).
  • Substituted performance is available (s.20).

4. Injunction

  • Prohibitory injunction to enforce a negative covenant (s.42 SRA; Lumley v Wagner, 1852).
  • No injunction is granted against a contract the court cannot supervise.

5. Quantum meruit

Reasonable payment for work done before the breach.

6. Rescission and restitution

On rescission, benefits received must be restored (s.64, s.65).

Conclusion

Damages are the primary common-law remedy. Statutory reform has made specific performance a routine equitable remedy, and the courts continue to police unfair penalty and forfeiture clauses.

14.A, by letter dated 1 March, offers to sell his house to B for ₹50 lakh. B posts a letter of acceptance on 3 March. On 4 March, before B's letter reaches him, A sends a telegram revoking the offer, which reaches B on 5 March. Is there a contract?
Model answer

Issue: Can A revoke the proposal after B has posted the acceptance?

Law:

  • Under s.4, communication of acceptance is complete as against the proposer when it is put in course of transmission to him, so as to be out of the power of the acceptor.
  • Under s.5, a proposal may be revoked at any time before communication of acceptance is complete as against the proposer, but not afterwards.
  • The postal rule applies (Adams v Lindsell, 1818; Household Fire Insurance v Grant, 1879).

Application: B posted the acceptance on 3 March. At that moment, acceptance became complete as against A, and A became bound. A's revocation on 4 March came too late.

Conclusion: There is a binding contract. A must perform it or pay damages, and B may sue for specific performance. (B, the acceptor, could have revoked his acceptance before it reached A, because communication is complete as against the acceptor only on receipt.)

15.X, aged 17, represents himself as 19 and borrows ₹2 lakh from Y by mortgaging his house. On attaining majority, X refuses to repay, pleading minority. Y argues that X is estopped by his misrepresentation. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Is a minor's mortgage enforceable, and does a false representation of age estop the minor?

Law:

  • A minor's agreement is void ab initio (s.11; Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose, 1903).
  • There is no estoppel against the statute: a minor is not estopped from pleading minority even if he lied about his age.
  • A minor cannot ratify the agreement on attaining majority.
  • Restitution may be ordered in equity under ss.33 and 41 of the Specific Relief Act. The court can direct the minor to restore the benefit obtained by fraud (Khan Gul v Lakha Singh, 1928; Leslie v Sheill, 1914 on tracing).

Application: The mortgage is void, so Y cannot enforce it or sue on the loan. X's fraud does not create an estoppel. However, since X obtained ₹2 lakh by deceit, the court may, at its discretion, order X to restore the money if it can be traced or as equitable compensation, especially where X seeks relief such as cancellation of the mortgage deed.

Conclusion: Y cannot recover under the contract. He may obtain restitution only through the court's equitable discretion. The plea of estoppel fails.

16.A, a singer, contracts with B, a theatre owner, to sing at B's theatre on two nights in December. A week before the show, the theatre is destroyed by fire without any fault of either party. B had already paid A an advance of ₹1 lakh. Advise the parties.
Model answer

Issue: Is the contract discharged by supervening impossibility, and what happens to the advance?

Law:

  • Section 56 (para 2): a contract to do an act which, after it is made, becomes impossible becomes void when the act becomes impossible.
  • Destruction of the subject matter, without fault, frustrates the contract (Taylor v Caldwell, 1863; Satyabrata Ghose, 1954).
  • Section 65: when a contract becomes void, any person who has received an advantage under it must restore it.

Application: The theatre was the foundation of the contract. Its destruction without fault makes performance impossible, so the contract is void from that moment. Neither party is liable for non-performance. A received ₹1 lakh as advance and must return it under s.65, subject to any agreed term on expenses already incurred.

Conclusion: The contract is frustrated. No damages are payable, and A must refund the advance.

17.P books a flat with a builder for ₹1 crore. The builder–buyer agreement allows the builder to forfeit 20% of the price if the buyer cancels for any reason. P cancels after paying ₹25 lakh, and the builder forfeits ₹20 lakh. Advise P.
Model answer

Issue: Can the builder forfeit 20% of the price under a one-sided forfeiture clause?

Law:

  • Earnest money may be forfeited if it is reasonable; an excessive forfeiture is a penalty governed by s.74, and only reasonable compensation is payable (Maula Bux v Union of India, 1969; Kailash Nath Associates v DDA, 2015).
  • One-sided clauses in builder–buyer agreements are unfair and not binding (Pioneer Urban Land v Govindan Raghavan, 2019).
  • Godrej Projects Development v Anil Karlekar (2025): forfeiture on cancellation must be reasonable and proportionate; an excessive amount is a penalty.

Application: A forfeiture of ₹20 lakh, 20% of the price, is excessive for a cancellation that leaves the builder free to resell the flat. Courts have commonly treated a modest earnest (around 10% of the basic price) as reasonable. The builder can retain only a reasonable earnest or prove actual loss.

Conclusion: P can claim a refund of the excess, with interest, before the consumer commission or a civil court. The builder may keep only a reasonable sum.
