Unit 1

The paper and its assessment

The paper tests practical skills through a record of three components and a viva. Planning early (choosing courts to observe, finding a chamber to intern with, and preparing for moots) is the key to good marks.

ComponentMarksWhat you submit
---------
A. Moot court30 (2 × 15)For each moot: written submissions (10) and oral advocacy (5)
B. Observance of trial30A record of two cases (at least one civil), stage by stage
C. Interviewing and pre-trial preparation30Records of client interviews and preparation of a case file

  • Moot evaluation: by the Principal or Head, an advocate and the teacher concerned.
  • Written submissions must include a brief summary of facts, issues involved, provisions of law and agreements, citations, and the prayer.

1.2 Planning the semester

  1. In the first week, get your moot problems and note the dates.
  2. Arrange an internship with an advocate (or a legal aid clinic) early; get permission to accompany them to court and to client meetings.
  3. Choose two cases to follow, at least one civil, where you can attend several hearings.
  4. Keep a dated diary from the first day; write up each visit the same evening.
  5. Prepare a neat, indexed, signed record; get each entry countersigned where required.

Points to remember

  • Three components of 30 marks, plus a viva of 10.
  • Moots: 10 marks written, 5 oral, each; evaluated by a three-member panel.
  • Plan early: problems, internship, two cases, a daily diary.

Unit 2

Moot court: analysing the problem and research

A moot is won in the library before it is argued in court. Read the problem many times, identify the issues precisely, and research the law on both sides.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Assigned moot problem
  • Identifying issues
  • Legal research

2.1 Analysing the problem

  • Read the problem slowly, at least three times; list the facts chronologically; note the parties, dates, documents and statutes mentioned; separate facts that are agreed, disputed and unknown.
  • Identify the forum (which court, under what jurisdiction) and the procedural posture (suit, appeal, writ, criminal appeal or SLP).
  • Frame issues as questions that can be answered "yes" or "no", for example: "Whether the termination of the agreement by the respondent was valid under clause 12."
  • Note any clues the drafters planted (dates for limitation, notice periods, statutory amendments).

2.2 Legal research

  • Sources: bare acts (current versions on India Code); commentaries; Supreme Court and High Court judgments (through SCC Online, Manupatra, or free sources such as Indian Kanoon and the Supreme Court's e-SCR); law commission reports; parliamentary debates; and foreign cases for persuasive value.
  • Read the ratio of each case, not only the headnote; check that it has not been overruled or distinguished (use citators).
  • Prepare a research table: issue, argument, authority (statute or case), citation, the exact paragraph relied on, and the counter-argument with its answer.
  • Research both sides: you may be asked to argue either, and you must anticipate the opponent's best cases.

2.3 Recent judgments (2025–26)

CasePooja Ramesh Singh v J&K Bank (2026)

Orders resting on AI-generated fake precedents were set aside. In moot research, check every authority in a reliable reporter or the court's own website.

Points to remember

  • Never cite an unverified authority; AI tools can invent cases (2026).
  • Read the problem repeatedly; chronology; forum and procedural posture; precise issues.
  • Research from current bare acts and authoritative reports; check that cases are still good law.
  • Research table for both sides.

Unit 3

Moot court: written submissions (memorial)

The memorial is the written argument. It must be accurate, well organised and persuasive, with every proposition supported by authority, and must follow the prescribed format.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Written submission: summary of facts, issues, provisions of law, citations, prayer

3.1 Structure of a memorial

PartContent
------
Cover pageName of court, case title, side (Petitioner or Respondent), name of the team or student
Table of contentsWith page numbers
List of abbreviationsAIR, SCC, Art., s., etc.
Index of authoritiesStatutes, cases (with citations), books, reports
Statement of jurisdictionThe provision under which the court hears the matter
Statement of factsA neutral, concise summary in chronological order
Issues raisedNumbered questions
Summary of argumentsA paragraph for each issue
Arguments advancedDetailed, with headings for each issue and sub-issue, applying law to facts, with citations
PrayerSpecific reliefs sought

3.2 Writing persuasively

  • Use headings that argue ("The termination was invalid because no notice was given"), not neutral headings ("Termination").
  • Follow a clear structure for each issue: state the rule, cite the authority, apply it to the facts, and conclude (IRAC: issue, rule, application, conclusion).
  • Deal with the opponent's strongest argument and answer it.
  • Cite accurately and uniformly (for example, Maneka Gandhi v Union of India, (1978) 1 SCC 248). Use a consistent citation style throughout.
  • Do not misstate facts or law; do not add facts not in the problem.
  • Keep within the page limit; proofread carefully.

Points to remember

  • Memorial structure: cover, contents, abbreviations, authorities, jurisdiction, facts, issues, summary, arguments, prayer.
  • Argumentative headings; IRAC for each issue; answer the opponent; accurate, uniform citations.
  • No new facts; respect page limits; proofread.

Next: Unit 4, Moot court: oral advocacy and court manners
Unit 4

Moot court: oral advocacy and court manners

Oral argument tests clarity, command of the law and composure under questions. The best advocates speak simply, answer the bench directly, and stay courteous.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Oral advocacy: communication, presentation, language, law, authorities, court manners

4.1 Preparing the oral argument

  • Prepare a roadmap: the issues you will argue and the order, with time allocated to each.
  • Prepare a short outline (not a script) and a compendium of key authorities with the relevant paragraphs flagged.
  • Practise aloud, with friends acting as judges who interrupt with questions.

4.2 Structure in court

  1. Introduction: "May it please Your Lordships. I, [name], appear on behalf of the Petitioner. With Your Lordships' permission, I will address three issues..."
  2. Facts: only if the bench wishes; otherwise a brief reference.
  3. Arguments: issue by issue; state your proposition, cite the authority, apply it to the facts.
  4. Rebuttal (if allowed): answer the opponent's key points briefly.
  5. Prayer: "For these reasons, I pray that Your Lordships may be pleased to..."

4.3 Answering questions

  • Stop speaking when a judge asks a question; listen fully; answer directly ("Yes, My Lord, because...") and then return to your argument.
  • If you do not know, say so honestly and offer to assist the court later. Never bluff or misstate a case.
  • Concede points that cannot be defended and show why they do not affect your conclusion.

4.4 Court manners

  • Address judges of the High Court and Supreme Court as "My Lord" or "Your Lordship" (or "Your Honour", as many courts now prefer, and as the Bar Council has suggested); address subordinate courts as "Your Honour" or "Sir/Madam".
  • Stand when addressing the court; wear the proper dress (black coat and band; white shirt; appropriate attire under the BCI Rules); seek permission before handing up documents; do not interrupt the opponent; refer to the opponent as "my learned friend".
  • Speak clearly and at a moderate pace; maintain eye contact with the bench; avoid reading.

Points to remember

  • Prepare a roadmap, outline and compendium; practise with interruptions.
  • Introduction, arguments, rebuttal, prayer.
  • Answer questions directly and honestly; concede where necessary.
  • Court manners: forms of address, dress, courtesy to opponents, clear delivery.

CaseSalem Advocate Bar Association v Union of India (2005)

Upholding the 1999 and 2002 CPC amendments, the Supreme Court approved the recording of examination-in-chief by affidavit (Order XVIII rule 4) and of cross-examination by a commissioner, to save court time. It also approved case-management rules to curb adjournments, which is why you will often see evidence recorded before commissioners when you observe civil trials.

Unit 7

Client interviewing techniques

The client interview is where the lawyer learns the facts, the client's goals and the documents. Good interviewing builds trust, gathers complete information and sets realistic expectations.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Interviewing techniques

7.1 Stages of a client interview

  1. Preparation: review any documents sent; prepare a checklist of questions; ensure privacy.
  2. Opening and rapport: introduce yourself; explain confidentiality (s.132 BSA and BCI Rules); put the client at ease.
  3. Client's narrative: let the client tell the story in their own words using open questions ("Tell me what happened"). Listen actively and do not interrupt.
  4. Clarification and detail: use closed and specific questions to fill gaps (dates, amounts, witnesses, documents); build a chronology.
  5. Identifying goals: what the client wants (money, possession, a stop to harassment, a quick settlement).
  6. Preliminary advice: options (negotiation, mediation, litigation), likely time and cost, risks, and what more is needed. Avoid over-promising.
  7. Closing: agree on next steps, documents to bring, fees, and the next meeting; send a follow-up note.

7.2 Techniques

  • Questioning: start with open questions, then use focused and closed questions; avoid leading questions that may distort the client's account.
  • Active listening: paraphrasing, summarising and reflecting feelings.
  • Funnel technique: from broad to narrow questions.
  • Timeline: draw a chronology with the client.
  • Dealing with difficult clients: emotional, uncooperative or dishonest clients; remain calm and professional; explain the duty to the court.
  • Ethics: conflict-of-interest check before the interview; confidentiality; not taking a brief where you may be a witness; no promise of results; transparency on fees; no contingency fees.
CaseBar of Indian Lawyers v D.K. Gandhi PS National Institute of Communicable Diseases (2024)

The Supreme Court described the advocate-client relationship as one of personal service with a high degree of trust, in which the client retains substantial control and the advocate is bound by duties to the court. This is why the interview must respect the client's instructions and autonomy while giving honest advice.

Points to remember

  • Stages: preparation, rapport, narrative, clarification, goals, advice, closing.
  • Techniques: open then closed questions, active listening, funnel, timeline; handling difficult clients.
  • Ethics: conflict check, confidentiality, no promises, fee transparency.

Unit 8

Pre-trial preparation

Cases are won by preparation: a complete case file, a clear theory of the case, organised evidence, and well-prepared witnesses.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Pre-trial preparations

8.1 Building the case file

  • Index of documents; chronology; list of dates and events; parties and witnesses with contact details; copies of pleadings and orders; research notes; and a running record of hearings.

8.2 Developing the theory of the case

  • A short, persuasive explanation of what happened and why the client should win, consistent with the evidence and the law. Every step (pleadings, evidence, cross-examination, arguments) should support it.

8.3 Evidence and documents

  • Identify the facts to be proved and the evidence for each (oral, documentary, electronic).
  • Obtain certified copies of public documents; arrange for the s.63 BSA certificate for electronic records; issue notices to produce (Order XII rule 8, CPC) and summonses for witnesses or documents.
  • Check limitation, court fees, jurisdiction, and the need for notice (s.80 CPC or contractual notice).

8.4 Witness preparation

  • Interview witnesses separately; review their statements; explain the court procedure and cross-examination.
  • Never coach a witness to give false evidence (that would be misconduct and an offence of fabricating false evidence).

8.5 Settlement and ADR

  • Assess the strengths and weaknesses; advise the client about settlement, mediation or Lok Adalat; prepare a negotiating position.

8.6 Trial notebook

  • An outline of the opening, the order of witnesses, examination-in-chief questions, cross-examination plans for opposing witnesses, objections to anticipate, and closing arguments.
CaseMahender Chawla v Union of India (2018)

The Supreme Court approved the Witness Protection Scheme, 2018 and directed all States to implement it. Witnesses facing threats can apply to a competent authority for protection measures such as concealment of identity and police escorts. Pre-trial preparation should consider whether a witness needs such protection.

Points to remember

  • Case file: index, chronology, parties, documents, research, hearings.
  • Theory of the case.
  • Evidence planning: certified copies, s.63 certificates, summonses; check limitation, fees, jurisdiction, notice.
  • Witness preparation without coaching; settlement assessment; trial notebook.

Unit 9

Internship and the viva-voce

The internship links what you have learned to real practice. A careful record and confident understanding of it are what the viva tests.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Internship
  • Record keeping
  • Viva-voce

9.1 Making the most of the internship

  • Attend court with your advocate every day possible; read the files before hearings; help with drafting, research and client meetings (with the client's consent).
  • Maintain professional conduct: punctuality, dress, confidentiality, and respect for court staff.
  • Observe different courts: civil, criminal, family, and tribunals (MACT, consumer commissions), and a Lok Adalat if possible.

9.2 The record

  • Organise it in three parts (A, B, C) with an index; date and sign every entry; attach copies of memorials and drafts; get certificates from the advocate and the court where required.
  • Write neatly and legibly; avoid copying from others or from published sources; keep it factual and reflective.

9.3 Preparing for the viva

  • Be ready to explain: the moot problems and your arguments; the procedural stages and provisions in your observed cases; why the court passed particular orders; your interview techniques and what you learned; and the ethics involved.
  • Expect questions on basic procedure (CPC, BNSS, BSA), on the provisions behind your drafts, and on professional conduct.
Exam tip

Most viva questions start with your own record: "Tell us about the civil case you observed. What stage was it at? Which Order of the CPC applies?" Know every case and provision in your record.

Points to remember

  • Use the internship to see varied courts and to practise drafting and research.
  • An organised, signed, original record in three parts.
  • The viva tests your understanding of your own record and basic procedure and ethics.

Exam practice

Viva-voce practice with model answers

This paper is assessed on record work (90 marks: moot court, trial observation, and interviewing and pre-trial preparation) and a viva-voce (10 marks), not a written exam. These are the questions most often asked in the viva, with model answers.

Try each question first, then open it to compare with the model answer.

Part A Moot courtViva

Questions on the memorial and oral rounds.

1.What are the parts of a moot memorial?
Model answer
  1. cover page: the court, the case name, the side, and the team code;
  2. table of contents;
  3. list of abbreviations;
  4. index of authorities: cases, statutes and books;
  5. statement of jurisdiction;
  6. statement of facts;
  7. issues raised (questions presented);
  8. summary of arguments;
  9. arguments advanced, issue by issue, using the rule, application and conclusion structure; and
  10. prayer.
2.How should you structure an argument?
Model answer

Use the "IRAC" or "CRAC" structure:

  • state the conclusion or proposition;
  • set out the rule (statute and binding precedent);
  • apply the rule to the facts; and
  • conclude.

Lead with your strongest point. Anticipate and answer the other side's best argument. Cite binding authority first: the Supreme Court, then the High Court of your jurisdiction, then persuasive authority.

3.How do you verify authorities?
Model answer

Check every case in a reliable source: SCC, AIR, SCC Online, Manupatra, or the court's website and neutral citation. Read the judgment to confirm that the holding supports your point, and check whether the decision was later overruled or modified.

AI tools can invent cases, and courts have set aside orders relying on fake, AI-generated precedents (Pooja Ramesh Singh v J&K Bank, 2026). Never cite what you have not read.

4.How do you address the bench and handle questions?
Model answer
  • Address the Supreme Court and High Courts as "My Lord / Your Lordship" or "Your Honour", following the court's practice. Address subordinate courts as "Your Honour" or "Sir/Madam".
  • Begin with "May it please the Court".
  • Seek permission before proceeding, and manage time.
  • When a judge asks a question, stop, listen, answer directly, and then link back to your argument.
  • Concede weak points gracefully.
  • Never interrupt the bench or opposing counsel.
5.What is the difference between the statement of jurisdiction and the issues?
Model answer
  • The statement of jurisdiction states the provision under which the court hears the matter, for example Art. 32, Art. 136 or s.96 CPC, and the respondent may reserve the right to contest it.
  • The issues are the precise legal questions the court must decide, framed neutrally or persuasively, and they mirror the structure of the arguments.
6.What is rebuttal?
Model answer

Rebuttal is the short reply by the petitioner or appellant after the respondent's arguments. It is limited to points raised by the respondent. It should be crisp, on two or three key points. No new arguments may be raised.

Part B Trial observationViva

Questions on civil and criminal trials you observed.

7.Describe the stages of a civil suit you observed.
Model answer
  1. institution of the plaint;
  2. summons to the defendant;
  3. appearance and written statement (30 days, extendable to 90; strict in commercial suits);
  4. documents: admission and denial;
  5. framing of issues (Order XIV);
  6. referral to ADR under s.89, where suitable;
  7. plaintiff's evidence: examination-in-chief by affidavit, then cross-examination;
  8. defendant's evidence;
  9. arguments;
  10. judgment and decree; and
  11. execution.

Mention the case you observed, its stage and what you learnt.

8.Describe the stages of a criminal trial in a sessions case.
Model answer
  1. FIR and investigation;
  2. police report (chargesheet);
  3. cognizance;
  4. committal to the Sessions Court;
  5. hearing on charge: discharge (s.250 BNSS) or framing of charge (s.251), within 60 days;
  6. plea of the accused;
  7. prosecution evidence;
  8. examination of the accused (s.351 [313]);
  9. defence evidence;
  10. arguments;
  11. judgment;
  12. hearing on sentence (s.258); and
  13. sentence.

Mention the safeguards you observed, such as legal aid counsel, interpreters and video-conferencing.

9.What is examination-in-chief, cross-examination and re-examination?
Model answer
  • Examination-in-chief: the party calling the witness examines him. Leading questions are not allowed, except on undisputed matters.
  • Cross-examination: by the adverse party, to test credibility and elicit facts. Leading questions are permitted.
  • Re-examination: to explain matters arising in cross-examination. New matters may be introduced only with permission.

The order is laid down in the Evidence Act (s.138 IEA, carried into the BSA). A hostile witness may be cross-examined by the party calling him, with the court's permission.

10.What did you learn about court etiquette?
Model answer
  • dress code: black coat and gown, white bands;
  • punctuality;
  • bowing to the court;
  • standing while addressing the court;
  • not speaking out of turn;
  • respect for opposing counsel; and
  • keeping mobile phones silent.

Mention the practices you observed, such as passovers, adjournment requests and the cause list.

Part C Client interviewing and pre-trial preparationViva

Questions on interviewing and case preparation.

11.What are the stages of a client interview?
Model answer
  1. Preparation: review documents and keep a checklist.
  2. Greeting and rapport: introduce yourself and explain confidentiality and fees.
  3. Narrative: let the client tell the story with open questions.
  4. Clarification: closed questions, a timeline and documents.
  5. Identifying the client's goals.
  6. Legal analysis and options: explain the options with pros, cons, costs and time.
  7. Decision: the client decides.
  8. Closing: next steps, documents needed and the engagement letter.
  9. Record: a file note.
12.What ethical rules apply in client interviews?
Model answer
  • confidentiality and privilege (s.132 BSA);
  • no conflict of interest, including with former clients (Rehana Khan, 2026);
  • honest advice and no false promises;
  • no contingency fees;
  • act only on the client's instructions; and
  • informed consent for settlement.

Respect the client's autonomy and dignity, and use an interpreter if needed.

13.What steps are involved in pre-trial preparation in a civil case?
Model answer
  • collect and organise documents and evidence;
  • check limitation and jurisdiction;
  • send a legal notice (s.80 CPC for the government; pre-institution mediation in commercial suits);
  • draft the plaint and interlocutory applications;
  • prepare a list of witnesses and documents;
  • consider ADR; and
  • prepare a case file with chronology and issues.

Assess the risks and costs with the client.

14.What is a case theory?
Model answer

A case theory is a coherent, persuasive account of the facts and law that explains why your client should win. It must be consistent with the undisputed facts and the evidence. Every step, from pleading and evidence to cross-examination and argument, should support it.
