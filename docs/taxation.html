The syllabus is framed around the Income-tax Act, 1961, the Wealth Tax Act, the Central Sales Tax Act and the A.P. General Sales Tax Act. The law has moved on, and each unit says where things now stand. The Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act from 1 April 2026, introducing a single "tax year" in place of "previous year" and "assessment year". Wealth tax was abolished from 2015-16. The A.P. General Sales Tax Act gave way to APVAT (2005) and then GST (2017). The CST Act survives only for petroleum products and alcoholic liquor. Section numbers in this book are those of the 1961 Act, which the syllabus uses and which still governs income up to 31 March 2026. Pull a unit off the shelf, or use the contents. Unit 1

Constitutional provisions

No tax can be levied without the authority of law. The Constitution divides taxing powers between the Union and the States through the legislative lists, and since 2016 shares GST power concurrently.

What the syllabus lists for this unit

  • Scope of tax laws
  • Tax and fee
  • Capital receipt and revenue receipt

  • Art. 246A (2016): Parliament and State legislatures have concurrent power to make laws on GST; Parliament has exclusive power over inter-State supplies.
CaseUnion of India v Mohit Minerals Pvt Ltd (2022)

The recommendations of the GST Council under Art. 279A have only persuasive value. They are the product of collaborative federalism, and Parliament and the States are not bound by them. Integrated GST on ocean freight under reverse charge on importers was struck down.

CaseMineral Area Development Authority v Steel Authority of India (2024)

A nine-judge bench (8:1) held that royalty on minerals is not a tax. States have legislative competence under Entry 49 of List II to tax mineral-bearing lands, and Parliament's law under Entry 54 of List I (MMDR Act) does not limit that power. It overruled India Cement v State of Tamil Nadu (1989) to the extent it held royalty to be a tax.

1.2 Tax and fee

  • Tax: a compulsory exaction for public purposes, without any reference to special benefit to the payer.
  • Fee: a charge for a special service rendered to individuals, with an element of quid pro quo (correspondence between the levy and the service), though not mathematical equivalence.
CaseCommissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments, Madras v Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt (1954)

The Court distinguished tax and fee: a tax is imposed for public purposes without reference to special benefit; a fee is a charge for special services rendered to a class, and its proceeds must go towards those services. A levy on religious institutions to meet general administration was held to be a tax in the guise of a fee, beyond the State's power as framed.

1.3 Capital receipt and revenue receipt

  • Revenue receipts are taxable unless exempt; capital receipts are generally not taxable unless specifically made taxable (for example, capital gains).
  • Tests: whether the receipt is for the loss of a source of income (capital) or in the course of business (revenue); fixed versus circulating capital; the nature of the asset in the recipient's hands.
CaseKettlewell Bullen and Co Ltd v Commissioner of Income Tax (1964)

Compensation received for termination of a managing agency was held to be a capital receipt, because it was paid for the loss of an enduring source of income, the structure of the assessee's profit-making apparatus, and not in the ordinary course of business.

Points to remember

  • Art. 265: no tax without authority of law; distribution of taxing powers through the Lists and Arts. 268 to 289.
  • GST: Art. 246A (concurrent), Art. 269A (IGST), Art. 279A (GST Council, recommendations persuasive: Mohit Minerals).
  • Royalty is not a tax (Mineral Area Development Authority, 2024).
  • Tax versus fee (Shirur Mutt); capital versus revenue receipt (Kettlewell Bullen).

Unit 2

Income tax: basic concepts, heads of income, assessment and recovery

Income tax is charged on the total income of every person for a year, at rates fixed by the annual Finance Act. Residence decides what income is taxed; heads of income decide how it is computed.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Historical outline
  • Definitions: income, agricultural income, previous year, assessee
  • Incidence of tax and residential status
  • Statutory exemptions
  • Heads of income
  • Assessment
  • Collection and recovery
  • Double taxation relief
  • Clubbing of incomes

2.1 Historical outline

  • Introduced in 1860 by James Wilson after the 1857 revolt; the Income Tax Act 1886; the Act of 1922; the Income-tax Act, 1961 (on the Law Commission and Tyagi Committee's work).
  • The Direct Taxes Code Bills of 2009 and 2010 lapsed.
  • The Income-tax Act, 2025 was passed in August 2025 and came into force on 1 April 2026. It is revenue-neutral (no change in rates) and restructured, with the single "tax year" concept.

2.2 Definitions

  • Income (s.2(24)): an inclusive definition covering profits, dividends, voluntary contributions to trusts, perquisites, capital gains, winnings from lotteries and so on. The courts have held "income" is to be given its widest meaning.
  • Agricultural income (s.2(1A)): rent or revenue from land in India used for agricultural purposes, income from agriculture or from processes to make produce marketable, and income from farm buildings. It is exempt from Union income tax (s.10(1)), because taxation of agricultural income is a State subject (Entry 46, List II).
CaseCommissioner of Income Tax v Raja Benoy Kumar Sahas Roy (1957)

"Agriculture" requires basic operations on the land, such as tilling, sowing and planting, involving human skill and labour, followed by subsequent operations. Income from forest trees of spontaneous growth, where no basic operations were performed, was held not to be agricultural income.

  • Previous year (s.3): the financial year immediately preceding the assessment year. Income of the previous year is assessed in the assessment year.
  • Assessee (s.2(7)): a person by whom tax or any other sum is payable, including deemed assessees and assessees in default.
  • Person (s.2(31)): individual, HUF, company, firm, association of persons or body of individuals, local authority, and every artificial juridical person.
Current law

Current law. Under the Income-tax Act, 2025, "previous year" and "assessment year" are replaced by a single tax year (1 April to 31 March). Income earned up to 31 March 2026 is still assessed under the 1961 Act.

2.3 Incidence of tax and residential status (ss.5 and 6)

  • An individual is resident if in India for 182 days or more in the year, or for 60 days or more in the year and 365 days or more in the four preceding years. The 60-day rule is replaced by 120 days for certain citizens and persons of Indian origin with Indian income over ₹15 lakh, and a citizen with such income who is not liable to tax elsewhere is deemed resident.
  • Resident but not ordinarily resident (RNOR): a resident who was non-resident in nine of the ten preceding years, or was in India for 729 days or less in the seven preceding years, plus the special categories above.
  • Scope of total income (s.5): a resident is taxed on worldwide income; an RNOR on income received or accruing in India and income from a business controlled or profession set up in India; a non-resident only on income received, accruing or deemed to accrue in India (s.9).
  • A company is resident if it is Indian or its place of effective management is in India.
CaseVodafone International Holdings BV v Union of India (2012)

The transfer of shares of a Cayman Islands company between two non-residents, which indirectly transferred control of an Indian company, was held not taxable in India under s.9 as it then stood. Parliament then amended s.9 retrospectively in 2012 (Explanation 5, indirect transfers). The Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021 withdrew the retrospective demands for transfers before 28 May 2012.

2.4 Statutory exemptions (ss.10 to 13A)

  • Agricultural income; share of profit from a firm; certain allowances; gratuity and leave encashment within limits; scholarships; income of local authorities, educational and medical institutions, and charitable trusts (ss.11 to 13, subject to conditions); income of political parties (s.13A, subject to accounts and audit).

2.6 Clubbing of income (ss.60 to 64)

  • Income of another person is included in the assessee's income to prevent avoidance: transfer of income without transfer of the asset (s.60); revocable transfers (s.61); income of a spouse from the assessee's concern, from assets transferred without adequate consideration, and income of a minor child (s.64).

2.7 Assessment

  • Return of income (s.139): due dates; belated and revised returns; updated return (s.139(8A)).
  • Kinds of assessment: summary processing (s.143(1)); scrutiny assessment (s.143(3)); faceless assessment (s.144B, since 2020); best judgment assessment (s.144); income escaping assessment and reassessment (ss.147 to 151, overhauled in 2021); search assessment (s.153A, later replaced by block assessment provisions).
CaseUnion of India v Ashish Agarwal (2022)

Around 90,000 reassessment notices issued under the old s.148 after 1 April 2021 (when the new regime took effect) were treated as show-cause notices under the new s.148A, to be dealt with under the new procedure. The Court used Art. 142 to balance the revenue's interest with the taxpayers' rights.

2.8 Collection and recovery

  • Tax is collected by deduction at source (TDS, ss.192 to 206), collection at source (TCS, s.206C), advance tax (ss.207 to 219), and self-assessment tax (s.140A).
  • Recovery: an assessee who does not pay is treated as an assessee in default (s.220); the Tax Recovery Officer recovers by attachment and sale of property (ss.222 to 232); interest is charged under ss.234A, 234B and 234C.

2.9 Double taxation relief (ss.90 and 91)

  • Bilateral relief (s.90) under a Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement (DTAA): the assessee may choose the treaty or the Act, whichever is more beneficial.
  • Unilateral relief (s.91) where there is no treaty.
CaseUnion of India v Azadi Bachao Andolan (2003)

The CBDT circular accepting Mauritius tax residency certificates as sufficient proof of residence under the India-Mauritius DTAA was upheld. Treaty shopping was not illegal in itself, and legitimate tax planning within the law is permissible. (The treaty was amended in 2016 to give India source-based taxation of capital gains on shares, and a principal purpose test was proposed later.)

2.10 Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseAuthority for Advance Rulings v Tiger Global International II Holdings (2026)

Gains of Mauritius entities from selling shares of Flipkart Singapore, which derived its value from India, were held taxable in India. A Tax Residency Certificate is not conclusive; treaty benefits can be denied where the structure lacks commercial substance, and GAAR may apply.

CaseMEPCO Industries Ltd v CIT (2026)

Whether a subsidy is capital or revenue depends on its purpose. An electricity tariff subsidy to support running operations is a revenue receipt.

Points to remember

  • TRC not conclusive; treaty shopping denied (Tiger Global, 2026); purpose test for subsidies (MEPCO, 2026).
  • The Income-tax Act 2025, in force from 1 April 2026, introduced the tax year.
  • Income is defined widely; agricultural income is exempt (Benoy Kumar Sahas Roy).
  • Residential status: 182 days, or 60 plus 365; RNOR; scope of income (Vodafone).
  • Five heads of income; clubbing (ss.60 to 64).
  • Assessment: summary, scrutiny, faceless, best judgment, reassessment (Ashish Agarwal).
  • Collection: TDS, TCS, advance tax; recovery; DTAA relief (Azadi Bachao).

Unit 3

Income tax authorities, settlement, appeals and penalties

Tax disputes pass through a hierarchy of authorities and appeals ending in the Supreme Court. Penalties and prosecution enforce compliance; settlement was offered as an alternative route.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Income tax authorities
  • Settlement of cases
  • Appeals and revisions
  • Penalties, offences and prosecution

3.1 Income tax authorities (s.116)

  • The Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT, under the Central Boards of Revenue Act 1963), Principal Chief Commissioners and Chief Commissioners, Principal Commissioners and Commissioners, Commissioners (Appeals), Joint and Additional Commissioners, Deputy and Assistant Commissioners, Income Tax Officers, Tax Recovery Officers and Inspectors.
  • Powers include discovery, production of evidence, search and seizure (s.132), requisition (s.132A), survey (s.133A), and calling for information (s.133).

3.2 Settlement of cases (s.245A onwards)

  • The Income Tax Settlement Commission allowed an assessee to make full and true disclosure of undisclosed income and settle the case once in a lifetime, with immunity from penalty and prosecution.
  • It was wound up from 1 February 2021 by the Finance Act, 2021, and pending cases were transferred to Interim Boards for Settlement. The Dispute Resolution Committee (s.245MA) for small taxpayers and the Vivad se Vishwas schemes (2020 and 2024) are alternative routes.

3.3 Appeals and revisions (ss.246 to 269)

  • First appeal to the Commissioner (Appeals), or to the Joint Commissioner (Appeals) for smaller cases, under s.246A; faceless appeals since 2020.
  • Second appeal to the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (s.253), the final fact-finding authority.
  • Appeal to the High Court (s.260A) on a substantial question of law; appeal to the Supreme Court (s.261).
  • Revision by the Commissioner: s.263 (orders erroneous and prejudicial to the revenue) and s.264 (on the assessee's application or suo motu, in favour of the assessee).
CaseMalabar Industrial Co Ltd v Commissioner of Income Tax (2000)

Under s.263, the Commissioner may revise an order only if it is both erroneous and prejudicial to the interests of the revenue. Where the officer adopts one of two possible views and the Commissioner disagrees, the order cannot be treated as erroneous unless the view taken is unsustainable in law.

3.4 Penalties, offences and prosecution (ss.270 to 280)

  • Penalty for under-reporting and misreporting income (s.270A, from 2017): 50% of the tax on under-reported income, and 200% for misreporting (such as misrepresentation or suppression of facts). Immunity is possible under s.270AA if tax is paid and no appeal is filed.
  • Other penalties: failure to keep books (s.271A), failure to get accounts audited (s.271B), and failure to deduct tax (s.271C).
  • Prosecution (ss.275A to 280): wilful attempt to evade tax (s.276C), failure to file returns (s.276CC), false statements in verification (s.277), and abetment (s.278). There is a presumption of culpable mental state (s.278E). Offences may be compounded (s.279(2)); special courts are provided (s.280A).

Points to remember

  • The CBDT heads a hierarchy of authorities with wide investigative powers.
  • The Settlement Commission was wound up in 2021; Interim Boards, DRC and Vivad se Vishwas replaced it.
  • Appeals: CIT(A), ITAT, High Court (substantial question of law), Supreme Court; revisions under ss.263 and 264 (Malabar Industrial).
  • Penalties: s.270A (50% and 200%); prosecution for wilful evasion, with a presumption of mens rea.

Unit 4

Wealth Tax Act

Wealth tax was an annual levy on net wealth of individuals, HUFs and companies. It was abolished from assessment year 2016-17 and replaced by a higher surcharge on the very rich.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Definitions: asset, net wealth
  • Valuation date
  • Deemed assets
  • Exempted assets

4.1 The Wealth Tax Act, 1957

  • Charged on the net wealth of an individual, HUF or company on the valuation date, at 1% on net wealth above ₹30 lakh (the final position before abolition).

4.2 Definitions

  • Assets (s.2(ea), after 1993): a closed list of unproductive assets: residential and commercial buildings (with exceptions such as one house and property used for business), motor cars (other than those used in hiring business or stock-in-trade), jewellery, bullion and furniture of gold, silver and platinum, yachts, boats and aircraft (other than for commercial purposes), urban land, and cash in hand above ₹50,000 (for individuals and HUFs).
  • Net wealth (s.2(m)): the aggregate value of assets on the valuation date, minus debts owed that were incurred in relation to those assets.
  • Valuation date (s.2(q)): the last day of the previous year, i.e. 31 March.

4.3 Deemed assets (s.4)

  • Assets transferred to a spouse or minor child (other than a married daughter) without adequate consideration, assets held by an AOP or firm (the partner's interest), and assets transferred under revocable transfers are included in the transferor's net wealth. This prevents avoidance by splitting.

4.4 Exempted assets (s.5)

  • Property held under trust for public charitable or religious purposes; the interest in coparcenary property of an HUF member; one house or part of a house (or a plot of land up to 500 square metres); jewellery of a former ruler declared as heirloom; assets of a non-resident outside India; and some others.

4.5 Abolition

Current law

Current law. The Finance Act, 2015 abolished wealth tax from assessment year 2016-17. The reasons given were that its collection was small compared to the cost of administration. It was replaced by an additional surcharge on individuals and companies with very high incomes. Information on high-value assets is now collected through income-tax returns (Schedule AL) instead.

Points to remember

  • The Wealth Tax Act 1957 taxed net wealth on the valuation date (31 March).
  • Assets: a closed list of unproductive assets after 1993.
  • Deemed assets (s.4) prevent avoidance; exemptions in s.5.
  • Abolished from assessment year 2016-17, replaced by surcharge.

Unit 5

Indirect tax laws

Indirect taxes are levied on goods and services and passed on to the consumer. India moved from a cascade of Central and State levies to a unified GST in 2017.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Indirect taxes: an overview

5.1 Direct and indirect taxes

  • A direct tax falls on the person who pays it and cannot be shifted (income tax).
  • An indirect tax is collected from one person but passed on to another, ultimately the consumer (customs, excise, sales tax, service tax, GST).

5.2 The pre-GST structure

  • Union: customs duty (Entry 83, List I), central excise (Entry 84), service tax (Entry 92C, 2003), and Central Sales Tax on inter-State sales (Entry 92A).
  • States: sales tax or VAT (Entry 54, List II), entry tax, entertainment tax, luxury tax, taxes on advertisements, and electricity duty.
  • Problems: cascading (tax on tax), multiple rates and laws, no input credit across Centre and State taxes, inter-State barriers.

5.3 Key cases on sale

CaseState of Madras v Gannon Dunkerley and Co (Madras) Ltd (1958)

A building contract was held to be an indivisible works contract, not a "sale of goods" within Entry 48 of List II (now Entry 54), so the State could not tax the value of materials used. This led to the 46th Amendment (1982), which inserted Art. 366(29A) defining "tax on the sale or purchase of goods" to include works contracts, hire purchase, delivery of goods on hire, and supply of food in restaurants (deemed sales).

CaseBharat Sanchar Nigam Ltd v Union of India (2006)

Mobile phone service was held to be a service, not a sale of goods; the electromagnetic waves were not goods. The Court applied the dominant nature test to composite transactions other than those covered by Art. 366(29A).

Points to remember

  • Direct taxes are not shifted; indirect taxes are passed on.
  • The pre-GST structure split indirect taxes between Union and States, causing cascading.
  • Works contracts were not sales (Gannon Dunkerley), until the 46th Amendment created deemed sales; mobile service is not a sale (BSNL).

Unit 6

Central Sales Tax Act, 1956

The CST Act defines when a sale takes place in the course of inter-State trade, outside a State, or in the course of import or export, and levies tax on inter-State sales. After GST, it applies only to a few goods kept outside GST.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Historical outline
  • Definitions
  • Sections 4 to 6A
  • Registration of dealers
  • Liability in special cases

6.1 Historical outline

  • Before 1956, States taxed sales with an outside element, leading to multiple taxation. Art. 286 (1950) restricted this, and the Bengal Immunity Co v State of Bihar (1955) decision held that no State could tax inter-State sales until Parliament provided otherwise.
  • The Sixth Amendment (1956) inserted Entry 92A in List I and Art. 269(1)(g), and the CST Act was enacted.

6.2 Definitions (s.2)

  • "Dealer" (any person carrying on the business of buying, selling, supplying or distributing goods), "declared goods" (goods of special importance in inter-State trade under s.14), "goods", "sale" (any transfer of property in goods for cash or deferred payment or other valuable consideration, including deemed sales), "turnover", and "place of business".

6.3 Sections 3 to 6A

  • Section 3: inter-State sale: a sale is in the course of inter-State trade if it (a) occasions the movement of goods from one State to another, or (b) is effected by transfer of documents of title during their movement.
  • Section 4: sale outside a State: a sale is inside the State where the goods are at the time of the contract (specific or ascertained goods) or at the time of appropriation (unascertained goods). A sale inside one State is outside all other States.
  • Section 5: sale in the course of import or export: a sale occasions export (or is by transfer of documents after goods cross the customs frontier), or occasions import (or is by transfer of documents before goods cross the frontier). Penultimate sales to fulfil an export order are also deemed to be in the course of export (s.5(3)).
  • Section 6: liability to tax: every dealer is liable to pay tax on all inter-State sales. Section 6(2) exempts subsequent sales by transfer of documents during movement, with prescribed certificates (E-I and E-II).
  • Section 6A: burden of proof in stock transfers: a dealer claiming that a movement of goods was a stock transfer to his own branch or agent, not a sale, must prove it by furnishing Form F.
CaseTata Iron and Steel Co Ltd v S.R. Sarkar (1960)

Clauses (a) and (b) of s.3 were held to be mutually exclusive. A sale that occasions the movement of goods falls under clause (a); a later sale effected by transfer of documents of title during the movement falls under clause (b). Under s.9, the tax is collected by the State from which the movement of goods commences.

6.4 Registration of dealers (s.7)

  • A dealer liable to tax must register; a dealer may also voluntarily register. The certificate of registration specifies the classes of goods that may be purchased at the concessional rate against Form C.
  • Rates: 2% against Form C for sales to registered dealers (reduced from 4% by 2008); otherwise the local VAT rate.

6.5 Liability in special cases

The Act makes special provision for liability on transfer of business, of companies in liquidation, of directors of private companies in liquidation, of partners of firms, and of guardians, trustees and agents. These make the relevant persons jointly and severally liable for unpaid tax.

Current law

Current law. After GST (1 July 2017), the CST Act applies only to the six goods outside GST: petroleum crude, high-speed diesel, motor spirit (petrol), natural gas, aviation turbine fuel, and alcoholic liquor for human consumption. Inter-State supply of all other goods and services is subject to IGST.

Points to remember

  • The CST Act followed Bengal Immunity and the Sixth Amendment.
  • Sections 3, 4 and 5 define inter-State sales, sales outside a State, and import and export sales.
  • Section 6 imposes liability; s.6A puts the burden of proving stock transfer on the dealer (Form F).
  • Registration and Form C; liability in special cases.
  • After GST, the CST Act applies only to six goods.

Unit 7

A.P. General Sales Tax Act, 1957

The APGST Act taxed the sale of goods within Andhra Pradesh. It was replaced by the AP Value Added Tax Act, 2005 and then, for most goods, by the AP GST Act, 2017.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Definitions: business, dealer, casual dealer, total turnover
  • Registration of dealers

7.1 Definitions

  • Business: any trade, commerce or manufacture, or any adventure or concern in the nature of trade, commerce or manufacture, whether or not carried on with a profit motive and whether or not profit accrues. It includes transactions incidental to or ancillary to such trade.
  • Dealer: any person who carries on the business of buying, selling, supplying or distributing goods, directly or otherwise, whether for cash, deferred payment, commission, remuneration or other valuable consideration. It includes local authorities, companies, firms, societies, clubs, commission agents, brokers, auctioneers and government departments that carry on such business.
  • Casual dealer: a person who, whether as principal, agent or in any other capacity, carries on occasional transactions of a business nature involving buying, selling, supplying or distributing goods in the State, whether for cash, deferred payment or other valuable consideration.
  • Total turnover: the aggregate turnover of all goods bought or sold by a dealer, whether liable to tax or not, including sales in the course of inter-State trade and in the course of export.

7.2 Registration of dealers

  • Every dealer whose total turnover exceeded the prescribed limit in a year, and every casual dealer, had to register with the assessing authority. Registration certificates had to be displayed at the place of business. Carrying on business without registration was an offence.
Current law

Current law. The APGST Act, 1957 was repealed by the AP Value Added Tax Act, 2005 (from 1 April 2005). APVAT now applies only to goods outside GST (petroleum products and liquor); all other intra-State supplies are governed by the AP Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017. Concepts like "business", "dealer" and "turnover" survive in modified form in the GST laws ("business", "taxable person", "aggregate turnover").

Points to remember

  • APGST definitions of business (profit motive irrelevant), dealer, casual dealer and total turnover.
  • Registration above the turnover limit, and of all casual dealers.
  • APGST was replaced by APVAT (2005), and then APGST 2017 for most goods.

Unit 8

Reforms in law: VAT and GST

VAT taxed only the value added at each stage within a State. GST extends the same principle across goods and services and across Centre and States, with input tax credit throughout the supply chain.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • GST
  • VAT
  • GST and VAT problems and prospects in application

8.1 Value Added Tax

  • VAT is a multi-stage tax on the value added at each stage, collected by allowing credit for tax paid on inputs. It avoids cascading.
  • States introduced VAT from 1 April 2005 (Andhra Pradesh among the first), following the Empowered Committee of State Finance Ministers' White Paper (2005).
  • Limits: it did not cover services or Central taxes; there was no credit for CST on inter-State purchases; and rates varied among States.

8.2 Goods and Services Tax

  • 101st Constitutional Amendment (2016): Arts. 246A, 269A, 279A; subsumed Central excise, service tax, additional customs duties, CST, State VAT, entry tax, entertainment tax (except by local bodies), luxury tax and others.
  • In force from 1 July 2017.
  • Statutes: CGST Act 2017, SGST Acts (including the AP GST Act 2017), IGST Act 2017 for inter-State supplies, UTGST Act, and the GST (Compensation to States) Act 2017.
  • Key concepts: "supply" (s.7 CGST), which includes sale, transfer, barter, exchange, licence, rental, lease or disposal for consideration in the course of business; levy (s.9); time, place and value of supply; input tax credit (s.16 to s.21); reverse charge (s.9(3) and (4)); composition levy (s.10); registration above the threshold (s.22, ₹40 lakh for goods in most States, ₹20 lakh for services).
  • GST 2.0 (from 22 September 2025): rates were rationalised from four slabs (5%, 12%, 18%, 28%) into two main slabs of 5% and 18%, with a special 40% rate on select luxury and sin goods.
  • Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT) began functioning from late 2025, providing a long-awaited second appellate forum.
CaseChief Commissioner of Central Goods and Services Tax v Safari Retreats Pvt Ltd (2024)

The Court held that the expression "plant or machinery" in s.17(5)(d) of the CGST Act (which blocks input tax credit for construction of immovable property on one's own account) must be read with a functionality test. A building, such as a shopping mall let out on rent, may qualify as a "plant" depending on its function in the business, allowing input tax credit. Parliament then amended s.17(5)(d) retrospectively in 2025 to replace "plant or machinery" with "plant and machinery", which overrides the ruling.

8.3 Problems and prospects

  • Problems: complexity of compliance, especially for small businesses; frequent changes in rates and rules; disputes on classification; delays in refunds; litigation over input tax credit; and fiscal federalism concerns about loss of State autonomy and the end of compensation (which ran until 2022, with the cess continued to repay borrowings).
  • Prospects: a unified national market, an end to cascading, better compliance through e-invoicing and e-way bills, and a broader tax base. Future questions include bringing petroleum, electricity and alcohol under GST.

8.4 Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseRadhika Agarwal v Union of India (2025)

Safeguards of criminal procedure on arrest apply to arrests under the GST and Customs laws; officers must have reasons to believe and must follow arrest safeguards.

CaseUnion of India v Sunil Biyani (2026)

The grounds of arrest under s.69 of the CGST Act must be communicated to the person arrested.

Points to remember

  • Arrest safeguards apply under GST (Radhika Agarwal, 2025; Sunil Biyani, 2026).
  • VAT: multi-stage tax with input credit, introduced by States in 2005.
  • GST: 101st Amendment, in force 1 July 2017; CGST, SGST, IGST; supply, ITC, reverse charge.
  • GST 2.0 from 22 September 2025: 5% and 18%, plus 40%; GSTAT functioning.
  • Safari Retreats (2024) and the 2025 retrospective amendment.
  • Problems: compliance, refunds, federalism; prospects: national market.

Unit 9

Sales tax authorities

Each sales tax law sets up a hierarchy of authorities to register dealers, assess and collect tax, and hear appeals.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Sales tax authorities (ss.3 and 4)

9.1 Authorities under the APGST Act

  • Section 3 provided for the appointment of the Commissioner of Commercial Taxes and other officers (Joint, Deputy and Assistant Commissioners, Commercial Tax Officers and Deputy Commercial Tax Officers) to carry out the Act.
  • Section 4 dealt with the delegation of powers and the jurisdiction of these authorities.
  • Appeals lay to the Appellate Deputy Commissioner, then to the Sales Tax Appellate Tribunal, and then to the High Court on a question of law (revision).

9.2 Authorities under GST

  • Central tax officers under the CGST Act (s.3: Principal Chief Commissioners, Chief Commissioners, Commissioners and others) and State tax officers under the SGST Acts. Cross-empowerment allows both to act, but a taxpayer is assigned to one administration.
  • Appellate structure: Appellate Authority (s.107), GSTAT (s.109 and s.112), High Court (s.117) and Supreme Court (s.118).
  • Advance Ruling Authorities (s.95 onwards) give binding rulings on questions referred by applicants.

Points to remember

  • APGST: Commissioner and subordinate officers (ss.3 and 4); appeals to the Deputy Commissioner, Tribunal and High Court.
  • GST: Central and State officers; Appellate Authority, GSTAT, High Court, Supreme Court; Advance Rulings.

Practice paper with model answers

Part A Short answer questions5 × 4 = 20 marks

Answer any FIVE of the following.

1.Article 265
Model answer

"No tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law." The tax must be imposed by a valid law made by a competent legislature, and must not violate fundamental rights or any constitutional provision. Executive action alone cannot impose a tax.

  • Tax collected without authority of law must be refunded (Mafatlal Industries v Union of India, 1997, subject to unjust enrichment).
  • Art. 265 extends to both the levy and the collection of tax.
2.Residential status of an individual
Model answer

Under s.6 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, an individual is resident if he:

  • is in India for 182 days or more in the previous year; or
  • is in India for 60 days or more in the year and 365 days or more in the preceding four years.

The 60 days is extended to 182 days, or 120 days in some cases, for Indian citizens and persons of Indian origin visiting India.

  • "Not ordinarily resident" status applies in specified cases.
  • Deemed residency applies to Indian citizens with income over ₹15 lakh who are not liable to tax elsewhere (2020).
  • Scope of income (s.5): residents are taxed on world income; non-residents only on income received or accruing in India.
  • The Income-tax Act, 2025 (from 1 April 2026) retains these tests.
3.Agricultural income
Model answer

Agricultural income is exempt from central income tax (s.10(1)). Under s.2(1A), it includes:

  • rent or revenue from agricultural land in India;
  • income from agriculture, or from processes to make the produce marketable; and
  • income from farm buildings.

Agricultural income is a State subject (Entry 46, List II). Partial integration with non-agricultural income determines the rate. Example: CIT v Raja Benoy Kumar Sahas Roy (1957), which required basic operations on the land.

4.Capital and revenue receipts
Model answer
  • Capital receipts are not taxable unless specifically taxed, for example as capital gains. Revenue receipts are taxable as income.
  • Tests:
  • fixed versus circulating capital;
  • the "enduring benefit" test;
  • whether the receipt replaces a source of income or income itself.
  • For subsidies, the purpose test applies (Sahney Steel and Press Works, 1997; CIT v Ponni Sugars, 2008). An electricity subsidy to meet operating costs is revenue (MEPCO Industries v CIT, 2026).
5.Tax avoidance and tax evasion
Model answer
  • Evasion: illegal, by concealment, false records or suppression; it is punishable.
  • Avoidance: using legal means to reduce tax. It was traditionally permitted (IRC v Duke of Westminster, 1936; Azadi Bachao Andolan, 2003). Colourable devices are not (McDowell & Co, 1985).
  • Planning: legitimate tax planning within the law.
  • GAAR (ss.95 to 102): denies benefits of impermissible avoidance arrangements lacking commercial substance. Applied in AAR v Tiger Global (2026).
6.GAAR
Model answer

The General Anti-Avoidance Rule (Chapter X-A, 1961 Act, effective 1 April 2017) applies to impermissible avoidance arrangements. These are arrangements whose main purpose is a tax benefit and which:

  • create rights not at arm's length;
  • misuse the provisions of the Act;
  • lack commercial substance; or
  • are carried out by means not ordinarily used for bona fide purposes.

Consequences include disregarding entities, re-characterising transactions and denying treaty benefits (s.98). Approving Panel procedure applies. There is a threshold of a ₹3 crore tax benefit.

  • Tiger Global (2026): a Tax Residency Certificate does not bar a GAAR inquiry.
7.GST Council
Model answer

Art. 279A, inserted by the 101st Amendment (2016), creates the GST Council: the Union Finance Minister as chair, the Union Minister of State for Finance, and the State Finance Ministers. It recommends:

  • taxes to be subsumed;
  • rates and exemptions;
  • model laws;
  • threshold limits; and
  • special provisions.

Voting: the Centre holds one-third of the weight and the States two-thirds; decisions need a three-fourths majority.

  • Union of India v Mohit Minerals (2022): recommendations are persuasive, not binding, and Parliament and the States have simultaneous legislative power (Art. 246A).
8.Input tax credit
Model answer

Input tax credit is credit for GST paid on inputs, input services and capital goods used in the course of business. It is set off against output tax (ss.16 to 21, CGST Act).

Conditions:

  • possession of a tax invoice;
  • receipt of the goods or services;
  • the tax actually paid to the government by the supplier; and
  • a return filed.

It is blocked for certain items (s.17(5)), such as motor vehicles and food.

  • Chief Commissioner of CGST v Safari Retreats (2024): construction of a building used for leasing may be "plant" (the functionality test).
9.Abolition of wealth tax
Model answer

The Wealth Tax Act, 1957 levied tax on the net wealth of individuals, HUFs and companies above a threshold, on specified unproductive assets. It was abolished by the Finance Act, 2015, from the 2015-16 financial year (assessment year 2016-17), and replaced by a 2% surcharge on super-rich incomes, because it yielded little revenue. Its purposes were equity and discouraging the hoarding of idle assets.

10.Discuss the constitutional framework of taxation in India, including the changes made by the 101st Amendment.
Model answer

Basic provisions

  • Art. 265: no tax without authority of law.
  • Art. 246 and the Seventh Schedule: taxing entries are separate from general entries. Union taxes include income other than agricultural income (Entry 82), customs and corporation tax. State taxes include agricultural income, land and buildings, alcoholic liquor, and entertainment by local bodies.
  • Taxes versus fees: the quid pro quo test for fees (Commissioner, HRE v Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar, 1954).

Limitations

  • Art. 285: Union property is exempt from State taxes.
  • Art. 289: State property is exempt from Union taxes, except trade.
  • Art. 286: States cannot tax inter-State sales or imports and exports.
  • Arts. 301 to 304: freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse (Jindal Stainless v State of Haryana, 2016, nine judges: non-discriminatory taxes are not hit by Art. 301).
  • Taxes are subject to Arts. 14 and 19.

Distribution of revenue

  • the Finance Commission (Art. 280);
  • sharing of taxes (Art. 270);
  • grants-in-aid (Art. 275); and
  • the divisible pool.

The 101st Amendment (2016): GST

  • Art. 246A: concurrent power of Parliament and the States to levy GST. Parliament alone legislates on inter-State supply.
  • Art. 269A: IGST on inter-State supply, apportioned between the Union and the States.
  • Art. 279A: the GST Council.
  • Art. 366(12A): "goods and services tax" means a tax on supply, except alcoholic liquor for human consumption.
  • Petroleum products: GST from a date to be notified.
  • Compensation: to States for five years (the GST Compensation Act).
  • Entries amended or deleted: Entries 92 and 92C (List I) and 52 and 55 (List II) were deleted, and Entry 54 (List II) was restricted.

Judicial interpretation

  • Mohit Minerals (2022): Council recommendations are persuasive; cooperative federalism.
  • Mineral Area Development Authority v SAIL (2024, nine judges): royalty is not a tax, and States can tax mineral rights.

Conclusion

The framework balances federal fiscal autonomy with a common market. GST pooled sovereignty through the Council, with ongoing debates on fiscal federalism.

11.Explain the heads of income under the Income-tax Act and the concept of total income.
Model answer

Scheme

Income tax is charged on the total income of the previous year (s.4). Under the Income-tax Act, 2025, from 1 April 2026, this becomes the "tax year". Section 14 classifies income into five heads.

  1. Salaries (ss.15 to 17): salary, perquisites and profits in lieu of salary. There must be an employer–employee relationship. A standard deduction is available.
  2. Income from house property (ss.22 to 27): annual value of property owned and not used for one's own business. Deductions are a 30% standard deduction and interest on borrowed capital.
  3. Profits and gains of business or profession (ss.28 to 44): deductions for expenditure wholly and exclusively for business (s.37) and depreciation (s.32). Disallowances apply (s.40). Presumptive taxation is available (ss.44AD and 44ADA).
  4. Capital gains (ss.45 to 55A):
  • the transfer of a capital asset (s.2(47));
  • short-term and long-term gains;
  • indexation, now largely removed after 2024;
  • exemptions for reinvestment (ss.54 to 54F); and
  • deemed income for indirect transfers (s.9(1)(i), Explanation 5, after Vodafone).
  1. Income from other sources (s.56): dividends, interest, lottery winnings, and gifts above ₹50,000 from non-relatives.

Computation of total income

  • clubbing of income (ss.60 to 64);
  • set-off and carry-forward of losses (ss.70 to 80);
  • deductions under Chapter VI-A (ss.80C onwards); and
  • rebates.

The new regime (s.115BAC) is the default, with lower rates and fewer deductions.

Principles

  • The real income theory.
  • Diversion of income by overriding title is not income (CIT v Sitaldas Tirathdas, 1961).
  • Income is taxed in the year of accrual or receipt, depending on the method of accounting.
  • Capital versus revenue: the purpose test for subsidies (MEPCO Industries v CIT, 2026).
  • Business income versus capital gain: substitution of shares on amalgamation was taxed as business income in Jindal Equipment Leasing (2026).

Conclusion

Total income is the aggregate of the five heads, computed under the specific rules of each head. The 2025 Act reorganises but keeps this structure.

12.Explain the scheme of assessment, appeals and penalties under the Income-tax Act.
Model answer

Returns (s.139)

Returns must be filed by the due dates, with revised and updated returns allowed (s.139(8A)).

Kinds of assessment

  • Summary processing (s.143(1)): arithmetical adjustments.
  • Scrutiny assessment (s.143(3)): after notice under s.143(2), conducted as faceless assessment (s.144B).
  • Best-judgement assessment (s.144): where there is non-compliance.
  • Reassessment (ss.147 to 151): where income has escaped assessment. Under the 2021 regime, there is a s.148A inquiry, and time limits of three years, or up to five years for escaped income above ₹50 lakh (Union of India v Ashish Agarwal, 2022; Union of India v Rajeev Bansal, 2024).
  • Search assessments.

Authorities

  • the Central Board of Direct Taxes;
  • Principal Chief Commissioners and Commissioners; and
  • Assessing Officers and faceless units.

Appeals

  1. Commissioner (Appeals) (s.246A): within 30 days, now faceless; also the Joint Commissioner (Appeals).
  2. Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (s.253): within 60 days. It is the final fact-finding body.
  3. High Court (s.260A): on a substantial question of law.
  4. Supreme Court (s.261).
  • Revision: by the Commissioner, prejudicial to revenue (s.263) or in favour of the assessee (s.264).
  • Dispute Resolution Panel: for transfer pricing and foreign companies.
  • Settlement: the Interim Board for Settlement (after 2021); Vivad se Vishwas schemes (2020, 2024).

Penalties

  • under-reporting and misreporting of income (s.270A): 50% or 200% of the tax;
  • failure to maintain accounts and audit;
  • penalties in search cases; and
  • immunity on conditions (s.270AA).

Prosecution (ss.275A to 280)

Wilful evasion (s.276C), failure to file returns, and compounding.

Conclusion

The system has moved to faceless, technology-driven processes, with a multi-tier appeal structure. The 2025 Act restructures the provisions, but the architecture continues.

13.Explain the main features of the Goods and Services Tax.
Model answer

Concept

GST is a destination-based, multi-stage, comprehensive tax on the supply of goods and services, with seamless input tax credit. It replaced excise, service tax, VAT, CST, entry tax and others from 1 July 2017.

Structure (dual GST)

  • CGST and SGST/UTGST: on intra-State supply.
  • IGST: on inter-State supply and imports (apportioned under Art. 269A).
  • Compensation cess: on specified goods, being wound down after the compensation period.

Taxable event

Supply (s.7, CGST Act), including:

  • sale, transfer, barter, exchange, licence, rental, lease or disposal;
  • imports of services; and
  • deemed supplies (Schedule I).

Schedule III lists activities that are neither goods nor services.

Key concepts

  • time of supply (ss.12 and 13);
  • place of supply (IGST Act);
  • valuation (s.15);
  • composite and mixed supply (s.8);
  • input tax credit (ss.16 to 21);
  • reverse charge (s.9(3) and (4));
  • registration threshold: ₹40 lakh for goods (most States) and ₹20 lakh for services;
  • composition scheme (s.10);
  • e-way bills and e-invoicing.

Rates

Under GST 2.0 (from 22 September 2025), there are two main rates (5% and 18%) and a special 40% rate for luxury and sin goods. There are also special rates for gold and other items.

Administration

  • the GST Council (Art. 279A);
  • the GST Network;
  • returns (GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B);
  • assessments, demands (s.73 for non-fraud, s.74 for fraud) and appeals to the GST Appellate Tribunal; and
  • arrest powers (s.69). Arrest needs "reasons to believe", and safeguards apply (Radhika Agarwal v Union of India, 2025). The grounds of arrest must be communicated (Union of India v Sunil Biyani, 2026).

Exclusions

Alcoholic liquor for human consumption. Petroleum products await notification.

Conclusion

GST created a national common market and reduced cascading of taxes. Issues remain over input tax credit, compliance burdens and Centre–State fiscal relations.

Part C Problems2 × 10 = 20 marks

Answer any TWO of the following.

14.Mauritius-based companies with valid Tax Residency Certificates invested in a Singapore company whose value came mainly from Indian assets. In 2018, they sold the shares and claimed exemption from Indian capital gains tax under the India–Mauritius treaty. The Revenue alleges that the companies were controlled from the USA and lacked substance. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Is a TRC conclusive for treaty benefits, and can GAAR deny them?

Law:

  • Azadi Bachao Andolan (2003) treated a TRC as sufficient evidence.
  • After amendments (indirect transfer provisions, s.90(4), GAAR, and the 2016 protocol to the India–Mauritius treaty), the Supreme Court in AAR v Tiger Global International II Holdings (15 January 2026) held:
  • a TRC is not conclusive;
  • the tax authorities may examine commercial substance and control;
  • the gains from an indirect transfer of Indian assets were taxable; and
  • the Delhi High Court decision was set aside, and the AAR's rejection was upheld.

Application: The companies were controlled from the USA and were conduits lacking substance.

Conclusion: Treaty exemption is denied, and the gains are taxable in India.

15.The State gives an industrial unit a subsidy on electricity charges for five years, to support its running costs during operations. The assessee claims it is a capital receipt, not taxable. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Is the subsidy a capital or revenue receipt?

Law:

  • The purpose test: the character of a subsidy depends on its purpose, not its source, form or timing (Sahney Steel, 1997; Ponni Sugars, 2008).
  • A subsidy to set up or expand the business is capital. A subsidy to meet running expenses is revenue.
  • MEPCO Industries v CIT (2026) applied this test to an electricity subsidy.

Application: The subsidy reduces operating costs during running operations.

Conclusion: It is a revenue receipt and is taxable.

16.GST officers arrest the director of a company for alleged fake invoicing. They give him no written grounds of arrest. He is produced before a magistrate the next day. Advise him.
Model answer

Issue: The validity of a GST arrest without written grounds.

Law:

  • Section 69 of the CGST Act: arrest requires the Commissioner's "reasons to believe" of a specified offence.
  • Radhika Agarwal v Union of India (2025): the safeguards of criminal procedure apply to GST and customs arrests.
  • Union of India v Sunil Biyani (2026): grounds of arrest under s.69 must be communicated to the arrested person.
  • Art. 22(1); Mihir Rajesh Shah (2025): written grounds are required.

Application: No written grounds were supplied.

Conclusion: The arrest is vitiated. The director may seek release and bail, and challenge the arrest under Art. 226, while the proceedings may continue lawfully.

17.A State levies sales tax on a sale of goods in the course of inter-State trade. The dealer challenges the levy. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Can a State tax inter-State sales?

Law:

  • Art. 286 bars a State from taxing sales outside the State, or in the course of import or export.
  • Before GST, only Parliament could tax inter-State sales (Entry 92A, List I; the CST Act).
  • After GST: Art. 269A (IGST) and Art. 246A(2) give Parliament exclusive power over inter-State supplies of goods and services. The CST survives only for petroleum and liquor.

Application: A State levy on an inter-State sale is beyond its competence.

Conclusion: The levy is unconstitutional, and the tax is refundable (subject to unjust enrichment).
