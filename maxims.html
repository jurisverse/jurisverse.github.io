Unit 1

Using maxims in answers and in court

A maxim is a compressed rule. It carries weight because courts have applied it for centuries, but it never overrides the words of a statute or the Constitution.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • What a maxim is
  • Sources
  • Authority of maxims
  • How to use maxims in exam answers

1.1 What a maxim is

A legal maxim is an established principle stated in a short, memorable form. Most come from Roman law and the medieval common law, and are in Latin. Some, such as the maxims of equity, are in English. A few Indian maxims come from the Sanskrit and Islamic legal traditions, but the exam syllabus uses the Latin and equity maxims.

1.2 How much weight a maxim carries

  • A maxim states a principle; it is not itself a rule of law. Courts apply it where it fits and the statute is silent.
  • A maxim cannot override the express words of a statute. Ignorantia juris non excusat, for example, yields where a statute requires knowledge of the law.
  • Indian courts treat many maxims as part of the law through decisions: audi alteram partem is read into Art. 14 and Art. 21; nemo debet bis vexari is codified in s.11 CPC, Art. 20(2) and s.337 BNSS.
  • Some English maxims have been rejected or diluted in India. Falsus in uno, falsus in omnibus is not applied (Nisar Ali v State of UP, 1957); the King-can-do-no-wrong rule never applied as such because of Art. 300.

1.3 Using maxims in exam answers

  • Name the maxim, translate it, state the rule in plain words, then give the leading case.
  • Show the limits or exceptions; examiners reward this.
  • Do not string maxims together for decoration. One correctly used maxim with a case is worth more than five without.
  • Spell the maxim correctly and italicise or underline it in a handwritten answer.
Exam tip

A good short note on a maxim has four lines: the maxim and its translation, the rule, the leading case, and an exception or statutory provision.

Points to remember

  • Maxims state settled principles in short form; mostly Latin, some English (equity).
  • They yield to statute; many are codified (s.11 CPC, Art. 20(2), s.337 BNSS).
  • Some are not applied in India (falsus in uno falsus in omnibus).
  • Exam method: maxim, translation, rule, case, limit.

Unit 2

General principles of law and justice

These maxims express the foundations of any legal system: every right has a remedy, the law does not demand the impossible, no one may profit from his own wrong, and litigation must end.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Maxims of general application: rights and remedies
  • knowledge of law
  • wrongdoing and benefit
  • finality
  • necessity
  • the act of the court

2.1 Rights and remedies

MaximUbi jus ibi remedium

Where there is a right, there is a remedy.

A legal right without a remedy is meaningless; the law provides a remedy for every invasion of a right. It underlies Arts. 32 and 226 and the tort of injuria sine damno (Ashby v White, 1703). Public-law compensation for violation of Art. 21 rests on it (Nilabati Behera v State of Orissa, 1993).

MaximUbi societas ibi jus

Where there is society, there is law.

Law is a social necessity; wherever people live together, rules arise to govern them. Used in jurisprudence to explain the origin of law.

MaximVigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt

The law assists the vigilant, not those who sleep over their rights.

The basis of the law of limitation and of the doctrine of laches in writ jurisdiction. Courts refuse relief to a party who delays without explanation (State of Odisha v Namatara Girls High School, 2026, an 11-year delay not condoned).

MaximInterest reipublicae ut sit finis litium

It is in the interest of the State that there be an end to litigation.

The basis of res judicata, limitation and finality of judgments. Together with nemo debet bis vexari, it underlies s.11 CPC (Daryao v State of UP, 1961).

2.2 Knowledge of the law and of facts

MaximIgnorantia juris non excusat

Ignorance of the law is no excuse.

Every person is presumed to know the law; a mistake of law is no defence in crime (s.14 BNS protects only mistakes of fact) and does not avoid a contract (s.21 Contract Act). State of Maharashtra v M.H. George (1965): a foreigner was liable under a notification he did not know of. The rule is softened where the law was not published (Harla v State of Rajasthan, 1951).

MaximIgnorantia facti excusat

Ignorance of fact excuses.

A bona fide mistake of fact, not law, is a defence: ss.14 and 17 BNS [76 and 79 IPC]. R v Tolson (1889): a woman who remarried believing on reasonable grounds that her husband was dead was not guilty of bigamy.

2.3 Wrongdoing and benefit

MaximNullus commodum capere potest de injuria sua propria

No one can take advantage of his own wrong.

A party cannot rely on his own breach or illegality to gain a benefit. A murderer cannot inherit from the victim (s.25 Hindu Succession Act; R v Sigsworth, 1935); a party cannot plead the absence of a contract it prevented from being completed.

MaximEx turpi causa non oritur actio

No action arises from a base cause.

Courts will not help a party whose claim rests on an illegal or immoral act. It underlies s.23 of the Contract Act. Holman v Johnson (1775) explains that the defence is allowed not for the defendant's sake but because courts will not lend aid to illegality.

MaximIn pari delicto potior est conditio defendentis

Where both parties are equally at fault, the position of the defendant is stronger.

Money paid under an illegal contract cannot generally be recovered where both parties are equally guilty. Exceptions: the parties are not in pari delicto (one was oppressed or defrauded), or the plaintiff repents before the illegal purpose is carried out (Immani Appa Rao v Gollapalli Ramalingamurthi, 1962).

MaximFraus omnia corrumpit

Fraud vitiates everything.

A judgment, decree or order obtained by fraud is a nullity and can be challenged in any proceeding, even collaterally (S.P. Chengalvaraya Naidu v Jagannath, 1994). Fraud also vitiates consent (s.17 Contract Act).

2.4 Necessity and the impossible

MaximLex non cogit ad impossibilia

The law does not compel the impossible.

A person is excused where performance of a legal duty is impossible. It underlies frustration of contracts (s.56) and excuses non-compliance with a time limit where compliance was impossible (Raj Kumar Dey v Tarapada Dey, 1987).

MaximImpotentia excusat legem

Inability excuses the law.

Closely related to the previous maxim: where the law requires an act that a person is unable to perform without fault, the failure is excused.

MaximNecessitas non habet legem

Necessity knows no law.

The basis of the defence of necessity: an act done in good faith to avoid greater harm is excused (s.19 BNS [81 IPC]). It is not a defence to murder (R v Dudley and Stephens, 1884).

MaximSalus populi suprema lex

The welfare of the people is the supreme law.

Individual interest yields to public welfare. It justifies police powers, eminent domain and reasonable restrictions on rights, and the defence of necessity in tort (destroying property to stop the spread of fire).

2.5 The act of the court and the law's trivia

MaximActus curiae neminem gravabit

An act of the court shall prejudice no one.

A party should not suffer because of the court's delay or mistake. Courts use it to restore parties to the position they would have held (A.R. Antulay v R.S. Nayak, 1988; Jang Singh v Brij Lal, 1966).

MaximDe minimis non curat lex

The law does not concern itself with trifles.

Trivial violations are not punished. It is codified in s.33 BNS [95 IPC]: nothing is an offence if the harm is so slight that a person of ordinary sense would not complain (Veeda Menezes v Yusuf Khan, 1966).

MaximFiat justitia ruat caelum

Let justice be done though the heavens fall.

Justice must be done whatever the consequences. Used rhetorically in judgments to emphasise judicial independence.

Points to remember

  • Ubi jus ibi remedium: right means remedy (Arts. 32 and 226; Nilabati Behera).
  • Ignorantia juris non excusat; ignorantia facti excusat (ss.14, 17 BNS).
  • No benefit from own wrong; ex turpi causa; in pari delicto (Immani Appa Rao).
  • Fraus omnia corrumpit (S.P. Chengalvaraya Naidu).
  • Lex non cogit ad impossibilia; necessitas non habet legem (not for murder: Dudley and Stephens).
  • Actus curiae neminem gravabit (Antulay); de minimis (s.33 BNS).

Unit 3

Constitutional and administrative law

Natural justice, the limits of delegated power and the meaning of the writs are all carried in Latin maxims that Indian courts have absorbed into Articles 14, 21 and 32.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Natural justice
  • delegation
  • sovereign immunity
  • legality and retrospectivity
  • the writs

3.1 Natural justice

MaximAudi alteram partem

Hear the other side.

No one should be condemned unheard. A person affected by a decision must get notice and a fair hearing. It is part of Art. 14 and Art. 21 (Maneka Gandhi v Union of India, 1978; A.K. Kraipak v Union of India, 1969). It may be excluded in emergencies, with a post-decisional hearing.

MaximNemo judex in causa sua

No one should be a judge in his own cause.

The rule against bias: pecuniary, personal or official bias disqualifies the decision-maker. The test is a real likelihood or reasonable apprehension of bias (A.K. Kraipak, 1969; Ranjit Thakur v Union of India, 1987). Justice must not only be done but must be seen to be done (R v Sussex Justices, ex parte McCarthy, 1924).

MaximQui aliquid statuerit parte inaudita altera, aequum licet dixerit, haud aequum fecerit

He who decides without hearing the other side, though he decides rightly, has not acted justly.

The longer form of audi alteram partem. It explains why a right decision reached without a hearing is still set aside.

3.2 Delegation and power

MaximDelegatus non potest delegare

A delegate cannot further delegate.

A person to whom power is delegated must exercise it himself and cannot sub-delegate it, unless the parent law permits expressly or by necessary implication (Ganpati Sitaram Balvalkar v Waman Shripad Mage, 1981). Also applies to agents (s.190 Contract Act).

MaximUltra vires

Beyond the powers.

An act beyond the legal power of the authority or company is void. Applies to delegated legislation, administrative action and companies (Ashbury Railway Carriage v Riche, 1875).

MaximRex non potest peccare

The King can do no wrong.

The English basis of sovereign immunity. It never applied as such in India: under Art. 300 the State is liable like the East India Company, subject to the sovereign-function exception (Kasturi Lal v State of UP, 1965), now largely eroded (N. Nagendra Rao v State of AP, 1994).

MaximQuod principi placuit legis habet vigorem

What pleases the ruler has the force of law.

The Roman maxim of absolute power. It is the opposite of the rule of law and is cited to contrast arbitrary rule with constitutional government (Art. 14; E.P. Royappa, 1974).

3.3 Legality and retrospectivity

MaximNullum crimen sine lege, nulla poena sine lege

No crime without law, no punishment without law.

A person can be punished only for an act that was an offence under a law in force when it was done, and only with the penalty then prescribed: Art. 20(1). It bars retrospective criminal laws, but not retrospective changes of procedure (Rao Shiv Bahadur Singh v State of Vindhya Pradesh, 1953).

MaximLex prospicit non respicit

The law looks forward, not backward.

Statutes are presumed to be prospective unless the legislature clearly makes them retrospective; procedural laws may apply to pending matters (Hitendra Vishnu Thakur v State of Maharashtra, 1994).

3.4 The writs (meanings to remember)

MaximHabeas corpus

You may have the body.

The writ to produce a person detained before the court and to release him if the detention is illegal. The most important safeguard of personal liberty; any person may apply (Sunil Batra v Delhi Administration, 1980).

MaximMandamus

We command.

The writ commanding a public authority to perform a public legal duty it has failed to perform. Not available against a private person or for a purely contractual duty.

MaximCertiorari

To be certified (informed).

The writ quashing a decision of an inferior court, tribunal or authority for want or excess of jurisdiction, error of law on the face of the record, or violation of natural justice (Hari Vishnu Kamath v Ahmad Ishaque, 1955).

MaximQuo warranto

By what authority?

The writ requiring the holder of a public office to show by what authority he holds it; the appointment is ousted if illegal (University of Mysore v C.D. Govinda Rao, 1964).

MaximLocus standi

A place to stand.

The right to bring an action. Relaxed in public interest litigation so that any public-spirited person may move the court for those who cannot (S.P. Gupta v Union of India, 1981).

Points to remember

  • Audi alteram partem and nemo judex in causa sua (Kraipak; Maneka Gandhi).
  • Delegatus non potest delegare unless the Act permits (Ganpati Sitaram).
  • Rex non potest peccare never applied as such in India (Art. 300; Nagendra Rao).
  • Nullum crimen sine lege: Art. 20(1); procedure may be retrospective.
  • Writs: habeas corpus, mandamus, certiorari, quo warranto (and prohibition).

Unit 4

Law of contract

Contract law's maxims express its core ideas: a meeting of minds, promises must be kept, the buyer must take care, no one can pass a better title than he has, and the injured party is put in the position performance would have produced.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Agreement and consent
  • consideration
  • illegality
  • performance and impossibility
  • remedies
  • agency
  • sale of goods

4.1 Formation

MaximConsensus ad idem

Agreement on the same thing (in the same sense).

There is no contract unless the parties agree on the same thing in the same sense (s.13 Contract Act). A fundamental mistake about the subject matter negatives consent (Raffles v Wichelhaus, 1864, the two ships "Peerless").

MaximPacta sunt servanda

Agreements must be kept.

The foundation of the binding force of contracts and treaties (Art. 26, Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties).

MaximEx nudo pacto non oritur actio

No action arises from a bare promise.

A promise without consideration is not enforceable (s.25 Contract Act), subject to the statutory exceptions: natural love and affection, past voluntary services, time-barred debts, and completed gifts.

MaximQuantum meruit

As much as he has earned.

A party who has done work under a contract that is discharged, or where no price was fixed, can recover reasonable payment for what was done (Planche v Colburn, 1831; ss.65 and 70 Contract Act).

4.2 Interpretation and fairness

MaximVerba chartarum fortius accipiuntur contra proferentem

The words of a document are construed most strongly against the party who drafted them.

Ambiguous terms, especially exclusion clauses in standard form contracts, are read against the drafter (Central Inland Water Transport v Brojo Nath Ganguly, 1986, on unconscionable terms; insurance policies are read against the insurer).

MaximUberrimae fidei

Of the utmost good faith.

Contracts such as insurance require full disclosure of all material facts by the proposer; non-disclosure allows the insurer to avoid the policy (Manmohan Nanda v United India Assurance, 2022). Ordinary contracts do not require such disclosure (s.17 Explanation).

4.3 Performance, impossibility and remedies

MaximLex non cogit ad impossibilia

The law does not compel the impossible.

Underlies s.56 (supervening impossibility). "Impossible" means impracticable from the point of view of the object, not merely more costly (Satyabrata Ghose v Mugneeram Bangur, 1954).

MaximRestitutio in integrum

Restoration to the original position.

On rescission, the parties must be restored to their positions before the contract, as far as possible (s.64 Contract Act). In damages for tort, the aim is to put the plaintiff in the position he would have been in had the wrong not occurred.

In contract, damages follow breach: a breach gives at least nominal damages even without proof of loss, while loss caused without any breach is not compensable. The parallel tort maxims, injuria sine damno and damnum sine injuria, are explained in Unit 5.

4.4 Agency and title

MaximQui facit per alium facit per se

He who acts through another does it himself.

The principal is bound by acts of the agent within authority (s.226 Contract Act), and is vicariously liable for torts in the course of the agency. See also respondeat superior in torts.

MaximNemo dat quod non habet

No one gives what he does not have.

A seller who is not the owner cannot pass a better title than he has (s.27 Sale of Goods Act). Exceptions: estoppel, sale by a mercantile agent, a seller or buyer in possession, a voidable title not yet rescinded (ss.27 to 30), and sale by a co-owner in possession.

MaximCaveat emptor

Let the buyer beware.

The buyer must satisfy himself about the quality and fitness of goods; there is no implied condition of quality (s.16 Sale of Goods Act). Exceptions: fitness for a purpose made known, merchantable quality in a sale by description, sale by sample, and fraud (Priest v Last, 1903). Consumer protection law has heavily eroded the rule.

MaximCaveat venditor

Let the seller beware.

The modern counterpart: under consumer protection law and product liability (Chapter VI, Consumer Protection Act, 2019) the seller and manufacturer bear responsibility for defects.

Points to remember

  • Consensus ad idem (s.13); pacta sunt servanda; ex nudo pacto (s.25).
  • Contra proferentem against the drafter; uberrimae fidei in insurance.
  • Lex non cogit ad impossibilia (s.56; Satyabrata Ghose); restitutio in integrum (s.64).
  • Qui facit per alium facit per se (s.226); nemo dat quod non habet (s.27 SGA).
  • Caveat emptor (s.16 SGA) and its exceptions; caveat venditor under consumer law.

Unit 5

Law of torts

Torts is full of maxims, and examiners expect them: injuria sine damno, damnum sine injuria, volenti non fit injuria, res ipsa loquitur, respondeat superior and sic utere tuo.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Legal injury and damage
  • consent
  • proof of negligence
  • vicarious liability
  • causation and remoteness
  • use of property
  • death of a party

5.1 Legal injury and damage

MaximInjuria sine damno

Legal injury without actual damage.

Violation of a legal right is actionable even without loss. Ashby v White (1703): a voter wrongfully refused his vote recovered damages; Bhim Singh v State of J&K (1985): compensation for illegal detention of an MLA.

MaximDamnum sine injuria

Damage without legal injury.

Actual loss without violation of a legal right is not actionable. Gloucester Grammar School case (1410): a rival school took pupils; Mogul Steamship Co v McGregor, Gow and Co (1892): lawful trade competition.

5.2 Consent

MaximVolenti non fit injuria

To one who consents, no injury is done.

A person who voluntarily accepts a known risk cannot sue for harm from it (Hall v Brooklands Auto Racing Club, 1933). Knowledge is not consent (Smith v Baker, 1891); it does not apply to rescuers (Haynes v Harwood, 1935) or where consent is obtained by fraud. In crime, consent is a limited defence (ss.25 to 28 BNS).

5.3 Proof of negligence

MaximRes ipsa loquitur

The thing speaks for itself.

Where the thing was under the defendant's control and the accident would not ordinarily happen without negligence, negligence is inferred and the burden shifts to the defendant (Scott v London and St Katherine Docks, 1865; Municipal Corporation of Delhi v Subhagwanti, 1966, collapse of a clock tower; Achutrao Khodwa v State of Maharashtra, 1996, a mop left in the abdomen).

5.4 Vicarious liability

MaximRespondeat superior

Let the master answer.

The master is liable for torts committed by the servant in the course of employment (Century Insurance v Northern Ireland Road Transport Board, 1942; Lloyd v Grace Smith and Co, 1912). The State is liable under Art. 300 (State of Rajasthan v Vidyawati, 1962).

MaximQui facit per alium facit per se

He who acts through another does it himself.

The second basis of vicarious liability: an act authorised by the principal is the principal's own act.

5.5 Causation and remoteness

MaximCausa causans

The immediate (operative) cause.

The cause that actually produces the damage, as opposed to a mere condition (causa sine qua non). Liability attaches to the causa causans.

MaximIn jure non remota causa sed proxima spectatur

In law, the immediate and not the remote cause is considered.

Damages are recoverable only for consequences that are not too remote. The test is reasonable foreseeability (The Wagon Mound, 1961), which replaced the directness test (Re Polemis, 1921). Also applied in insurance law.

MaximNovus actus interveniens

A new intervening act.

A free, deliberate and unforeseeable act of a third party breaks the chain of causation and relieves the original wrongdoer. A foreseeable intervention does not (Haynes v Harwood, 1935).

5.6 Use of property

MaximSic utere tuo ut alienum non laedas

Use your own property so as not to injure another's.

The basis of the law of nuisance and of Rylands v Fletcher (1868). The absolute liability of hazardous enterprises (M.C. Mehta v Union of India, 1987) extends the same idea.

5.7 Death of a party

MaximActio personalis moritur cum persona

A personal action dies with the person.

At common law, tort actions ended with the death of either party. Largely abrogated by statute: the Legal Representatives' Suits Act, 1855, the Fatal Accidents Act, 1855, and s.306 Indian Succession Act, which keeps alive most causes of action except defamation, assault and other personal injuries not causing death (M. Veerappa v Evelyn Sequeira, 1988).

Points to remember

  • Injuria sine damno (Ashby v White) v damnum sine injuria (Gloucester Grammar School).
  • Volenti non fit injuria: knowledge is not consent (Smith v Baker); not for rescuers.
  • Res ipsa loquitur shifts the burden (Subhagwanti; Achutrao Khodwa).
  • Respondeat superior and qui facit per alium: vicarious liability.
  • Remoteness: proxima causa; Wagon Mound foreseeability; novus actus interveniens.
  • Sic utere tuo: nuisance and Rylands; actio personalis moritur cum persona, modified by statute.

Unit 6

Criminal law

The criminal law maxims protect the accused: no guilt without a guilty mind, no second trial for the same offence, no compulsion to confess, and the benefit of reasonable doubt.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Guilty mind
  • incapacity
  • double jeopardy
  • self-incrimination
  • proof and doubt
  • the body of the crime

6.1 The guilty mind

MaximActus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea

The act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty.

Criminal liability ordinarily needs both a prohibited act (actus reus) and a guilty mind (mens rea). Mens rea is presumed to be required unless a statute excludes it expressly or by necessary implication (Nathulal v State of MP, 1966). Strict-liability offences in public-welfare statutes are exceptions (State of Maharashtra v M.H. George, 1965).

MaximActus me invito factus non est meus actus

An act done by me against my will is not my act.

Involuntary acts do not attract liability. It underlies the defences of compulsion by threats (s.32 BNS [94 IPC]) and accident (s.18 BNS [80 IPC]).

MaximFuriosi nulla voluntas est

A madman has no will.

A person of unsound mind, who cannot know the nature of the act or that it is wrong, is not criminally responsible: s.22 BNS [84 IPC] (M'Naghten rules).

MaximDoli incapax

Incapable of crime.

A child under seven is conclusively presumed incapable of committing an offence (s.20 BNS [82 IPC]). A child of seven to twelve is presumed incapable unless shown to have attained sufficient maturity of understanding (s.21 BNS [83 IPC]).

MaximQui peccat ebrius luat sobrius

He who sins when drunk shall be punished when sober.

Voluntary intoxication is no defence, though it may be relevant to specific intent: ss.23 and 24 BNS [85 and 86 IPC] (Basdev v State of PEPSU, 1956).

6.2 No second trial, no compelled confession

MaximNemo debet bis vexari pro una et eadem causa

No one should be vexed twice for the same cause.

The basis of double jeopardy and res judicata. In criminal law: Art. 20(2) (no prosecution and punishment twice for the same offence) and s.337 BNSS [300 CrPC], covering autrefois acquit and autrefois convict (Maqbool Hussain v State of Bombay, 1953: departmental confiscation is not "prosecution"). In civil law: s.11 CPC.

MaximNemo tenetur se ipsum accusare

No one is bound to accuse himself.

The right against self-incrimination: Art. 20(3). It covers testimonial compulsion, not physical evidence such as fingerprints or specimen handwriting (State of Bombay v Kathi Kalu Oghad, 1961). Involuntary narco-analysis, polygraph and brain-mapping violate it (Selvi v State of Karnataka, 2010; Amlesh Kumar v State of Bihar, 2025).

6.3 Proof and doubt

MaximEi incumbit probatio qui dicit, non qui negat

The burden of proof lies on the one who asserts, not on the one who denies.

The prosecution must prove the charge beyond reasonable doubt (s.104 BSA [101 IEA]); the accused need not prove innocence, except for exceptions he pleads (s.108 BSA [105 IEA]).

MaximIn dubio pro reo

When in doubt, for the accused.

Where the evidence leaves a reasonable doubt, the accused gets the benefit of it. The doubt must be reasonable, not fanciful (Kali Ram v State of HP, 1973).

MaximCorpus delicti

The body of the crime.

The facts showing that a crime was committed. In murder, recovery of the dead body is not essential if death is proved by other reliable evidence (Rama Nand v State of HP, 1981).

MaximFalsus in uno, falsus in omnibus

False in one thing, false in everything.

Not a rule of law in India. Courts separate the grain from the chaff and may accept the reliable part of a witness's testimony (Nisar Ali v State of UP, 1957; Gangadhar Behera v State of Orissa, 2002).

MaximAlibi

Elsewhere.

The plea that the accused was elsewhere when the offence was committed; facts inconsistent with the prosecution case are relevant under the BSA. The burden of proving it is on the accused, and it must be strictly proved (Binay Kumar Singh v State of Bihar, 1997).

Points to remember

  • Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea (Nathulal); exceptions for strict liability.
  • Incapacity: furiosi nulla voluntas (s.22 BNS); doli incapax (ss.20, 21 BNS); intoxication (ss.23, 24 BNS).
  • Nemo debet bis vexari: Art. 20(2), s.337 BNSS; nemo tenetur: Art. 20(3) (Kathi Kalu Oghad; Selvi).
  • Burden on prosecution; in dubio pro reo; falsus in uno not applied in India.

Unit 7

Evidence and procedure

Procedure and evidence rest on finality, presumptions of regularity and fairness to parties not before the court. Several Latin terms are also simply names for procedural ideas.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Finality and pending proceedings
  • burden and presumptions
  • dying declarations and res gestae
  • precedent
  • procedural terms

7.1 Finality

MaximRes judicata pro veritate accipitur

A thing adjudged is accepted as the truth.

A final decision between the same parties on the same matter binds them in later proceedings (s.11 CPC), including matters that might and ought to have been raised (constructive res judicata). It also applies to writ petitions (Daryao v State of UP, 1961).

MaximRes sub judice

A matter under judicial consideration.

A court shall not proceed with a suit where the same matter is directly and substantially in issue in an earlier suit pending between the same parties (s.10 CPC). Only the trial is stayed.

MaximLis pendens

A pending suit.

A transfer of immovable property during a suit in which the right to it is in question cannot affect the rights of the other party under the decree (s.52 TPA; Bellamy v Sabine, 1857).

7.2 Burden and presumptions

MaximActori incumbit onus probandi

The burden of proof lies on the plaintiff.

The party who wants judgment on facts must prove them (s.104 BSA [101 IEA]); the party who would fail if no evidence were led bears the burden (s.105 BSA [102 IEA]).

MaximOmnia praesumuntur rite esse acta

All acts are presumed to have been done rightly and regularly.

Official and judicial acts are presumed to have been regularly performed (illustration (e) to s.119 BSA [114 IEA]), until the contrary is shown.

MaximRes inter alios acta alteri nocere non debet

A transaction between others should not prejudice one who is not a party.

Acts, statements or judgments between other parties are generally not evidence against a stranger. Judgments in rem are an exception.

7.3 Statements of the dying and of the moment

MaximNemo moriturus praesumitur mentire

A dying person is presumed not to lie.

The basis of the admissibility of dying declarations (s.26(a) BSA [32(1) IEA]). In India, expectation of death is not needed, and a dying declaration can found a conviction without corroboration if it is true and voluntary (Khushal Rao v State of Bombay, 1958; Laxman v State of Maharashtra, 2002).

MaximRes gestae

Things done (part of the transaction).

Facts and statements so connected with the fact in issue as to form part of the same transaction are relevant (s.4 BSA [6 IEA]). They must be contemporaneous, leaving no time for fabrication (Sukhar v State of UP, 1999).

7.4 Precedent

MaximStare decisis et non quieta movere

To stand by decisions and not to disturb settled matters.

Courts follow precedent to ensure certainty. In India, Art. 141 makes Supreme Court decisions binding on all courts; larger benches bind smaller ones (Central Board of Dawoodi Bohra Community v State of Maharashtra, 2005).

MaximRatio decidendi

The reason for the decision.

The principle of law on which a case is decided, which is the binding part of a precedent.

MaximObiter dictum

A remark by the way.

A statement of law not necessary for the decision; persuasive, not binding, though considered Supreme Court obiter is generally followed by lower courts.

MaximPer incuriam

Through lack of care.

A decision given in ignorance of a binding statute or precedent is not binding (State of UP v Synthetics and Chemicals, 1991). A High Court cannot declare a Supreme Court decision per incuriam.

7.5 Common procedural terms

MaximEx parte

On behalf of one side only.

A proceeding or order made in the absence of the other party (Order IX CPC); the defendant may apply to set aside an ex parte decree (Order IX Rule 13).

MaximAmicus curiae

A friend of the court.

A lawyer appointed by the court to assist it on a point, often in public interest litigation or for an unrepresented accused.

MaximForum non conveniens

An inconvenient forum.

A court with jurisdiction may decline to exercise it where another forum is more appropriate. Applied to writs under Art. 226 where only a small part of the cause of action arose (Kusum Ingots v Union of India, 2004).

MaximNunc pro tunc

Now for then.

An order given retrospective effect from an earlier date, to correct an omission.

Points to remember

  • Res judicata (s.11 CPC; Daryao), res sub judice (s.10 CPC), lis pendens (s.52 TPA).
  • Actori incumbit onus probandi (ss.104, 105 BSA); omnia praesumuntur rite esse acta (s.119 BSA).
  • Nemo moriturus praesumitur mentire (s.26 BSA; Khushal Rao); res gestae (s.4 BSA).
  • Stare decisis (Art. 141); ratio, obiter, per incuriam.

Unit 8

Property, family and succession

Property maxims explain who owns what is attached to land, why the earlier right prevails, and why a living person has no heirs. Family law adds the presumption of paternity and the State as parent of those who cannot protect themselves.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Ownership of land
  • title
  • priority
  • succession
  • guardianship and legitimacy
  • conflict of laws

8.1 Land and what it carries

MaximCujus est solum ejus est usque ad coelum et ad inferos

Whose is the soil, his is it up to the sky and down to the depths.

The owner of land owns the space above and the minerals below. Modified by statute (aviation law; mineral rights vested in the State). Thressiamma Jacob v Geologist (2013): sub-soil rights belong to the landowner unless the law vests them elsewhere.

MaximQuicquid plantatur solo, solo cedit

Whatever is affixed to the soil belongs to the soil.

Fixtures become part of the land. Not fully applied in India: a person who builds on another's land in good faith may remove the building or be compensated (s.51 TPA, improvements by bona fide holders; s.108(h) for lessees).

8.2 Title and priority

MaximNemo dat quod non habet

No one gives what he does not have.

A transferor cannot pass a better title than his own. Registration does not cure the absence of title (Mehnoor Fatima v Vishveshka Infrastructure, 2025). Exceptions in property law include the ostensible owner (s.41 TPA) and feeding the grant by estoppel (s.43 TPA).

MaximQui prior est tempore, potior est jure

He who is first in time is stronger in right.

Between competing interests in the same property, the earlier prevails (s.48 TPA), subject to registration and notice rules.

MaximPendente lite nihil innovetur

During litigation nothing should be changed.

The basis of lis pendens (s.52 TPA): the subject of a pending suit must not be altered to prejudice the other party.

MaximJus tertii

The right of a third party.

A defendant in possession cannot usually defeat a possessory claim by showing that a third person, not himself, has a better title.

8.3 Succession

MaximNemo est heres viventis

No one is the heir of a living person.

An heir apparent has only a hope of succession (spes successionis), which cannot be transferred (s.6(a) TPA) or relinquished by contract in a way that binds him later, except by estoppel.

MaximSpes successionis

A hope of succession.

The chance of an heir apparent or a relation obtaining an estate. It is not property and cannot be transferred (s.6(a) TPA).

MaximDonatio mortis causa

A gift in contemplation of death.

A gift of movable property made in expectation of imminent death, conditional on death occurring (s.191 Indian Succession Act; Muslim law treats such gifts as wills limited to one-third).

8.4 Family and guardianship

MaximPater est quem nuptiae demonstrant

The father is he whom the marriage indicates.

A child born during a valid marriage is presumed legitimate (s.116 BSA [112 IEA]), rebuttable only by proof of non-access. DNA tests are ordered only in cases of eminent need (Goutam Kundu v State of WB, 1993; R. Rajendran v Kamar Nisha, 2025).

MaximParens patriae

Parent of the country.

The State as guardian of those who cannot protect themselves: minors, persons of unsound mind, and victims of disasters. Used in the Bhopal Gas Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act, 1985 (Charan Lal Sahu v Union of India, 1990) and in decisions on persons in a vegetative state (Aruna Shanbaug, 2011).

MaximFactum valet quod fieri non debuit

What ought not to have been done is valid once done.

A Hindu law maxim (also Latin form) that an act done in breach of a directory precept, but not of a mandatory rule, is valid. Used for marriages and adoptions with irregularities that do not go to their essence.

8.5 Conflict of laws

MaximMobilia sequuntur personam

Movables follow the person.

Succession to movable property is governed by the law of the deceased's domicile (s.5 Indian Succession Act).

MaximLex situs

The law of the place where the property is situated.

Succession to and transfer of immovable property are governed by the law of the place where it is situated.

Points to remember

  • Cujus est solum (modified by statute; Thressiamma Jacob); quicquid plantatur (s.51 TPA).
  • Nemo dat; qui prior est tempore (s.48 TPA); pendente lite (s.52 TPA).
  • Nemo est heres viventis; spes successionis (s.6(a) TPA).
  • Pater est quem nuptiae demonstrant (s.116 BSA); parens patriae (Charan Lal Sahu); factum valet.
  • Mobilia sequuntur personam; lex situs for immovables.

Next: Unit 9, Interpretation of statutes
Unit 9

Interpretation of statutes

The interpretation maxims are tested in every Interpretation of Statutes paper. Learn each with its conditions and one leading case.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Construction to give effect
  • associated words
  • general and specific provisions
  • later and earlier laws
  • contemporaneous exposition

9.1 Making the law work

MaximUt res magis valeat quam pereat

It is better for a thing to have effect than to be void.

Where two constructions are possible, the court adopts the one that makes the provision workable rather than futile (CIT v Teja Singh, 1959; Tinsukhia Electric Supply v State of Assam, 1989).

MaximA verbis legis non est recedendum

From the words of the law there must be no departure.

The literal rule: where the words are clear, they must be applied (Nelson Motis v Union of India, 1992).

MaximCasus omissus

A case omitted.

A situation the legislature did not provide for cannot be supplied by the court, except in rare cases of clear necessity apparent from the statute itself (Padmasundara Rao v State of TN, 2002).

9.2 Associated words

MaximNoscitur a sociis

A word is known by the company it keeps.

A doubtful word takes its meaning from the words associated with it (Rainbow Steels v CST, 1981; limited in State of Bombay v Hospital Mazdoor Sabha, 1960).

MaximEjusdem generis

Of the same kind.

General words following specific words of a distinct genus are confined to that genus. Conditions: the specific words form a genus, the general words follow them, and no contrary intention appears (Amar Chandra Chakraborty v Collector of Excise, 1972). Not applied to "other authorities" in Art. 12 (Rajasthan SEB v Mohan Lal, 1967).

MaximReddendo singula singulis

Referring each to each.

Where several words are followed by several objects, each word is read with its appropriate object.

MaximExpressio unius est exclusio alterius

The express mention of one thing excludes others.

An aid to be used with caution, since the mention may be for abundant caution.

MaximExpressum facit cessare tacitum

What is expressed makes what is implied cease.

Where a statute expressly provides for something, there is no room for implying something different on the same matter.

9.3 Conflicting provisions

MaximGeneralia specialibus non derogant

General provisions do not derogate from special ones.

A special provision prevails over a general one on the same subject, even if the general law is later (J.K. Cotton Spinning and Weaving Mills v State of UP, 1961).

MaximLeges posteriores priores contrarias abrogant

Later laws repeal earlier contrary laws.

Where two laws of equal rank conflict and cannot be harmonised, the later prevails (implied repeal), subject to the generalia specialibus rule.

9.4 Context and history

MaximContemporanea expositio est optima et fortissima in lege

A contemporaneous exposition is the best and strongest in law.

The understanding of a statute at the time it was made, and long official practice, is a useful aid for old statutes, but not where the words are clear (Desh Bandhu Gupta v Delhi Stock Exchange, 1979).

MaximEx visceribus actus

From the inside of the Act.

A statute must be read as a whole; every part throws light on the others (Philips India v Labour Court, 1985).

MaximIn pari materia

On the same subject matter.

Statutes dealing with the same subject are read together as one system (Daivshala v Oriental Insurance, 2025, reading the Employees' Compensation Act with the ESI Act).

Points to remember

  • Ut res magis valeat; literal rule (a verbis legis); casus omissus not supplied.
  • Noscitur a sociis; ejusdem generis (conditions: Amar Chandra Chakraborty); reddendo singula singulis.
  • Expressio unius (caution); expressum facit cessare tacitum.
  • Generalia specialibus non derogant (J.K. Cotton); later laws repeal earlier contrary laws.
  • Contemporanea expositio; ex visceribus actus; in pari materia.

Unit 10

Maxims of equity

Equity developed in the English Court of Chancery to soften the rigidity of the common law. Its maxims are in English and guide discretionary relief in India, such as specific performance and injunctions.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • The twelve maxims of equity and their use in Indian law, especially the Specific Relief Act and the Trusts Act

10.1 The maxims

MaximEquity will not suffer a wrong to be without a remedy

Equity's counterpart of ubi jus ibi remedium.

Equity intervenes where the common law gives no adequate remedy, for example by enforcing trusts and granting injunctions.

MaximEquity follows the law

Equity does not override legal rules.

Equity supplements the law; it does not contradict statutes or settled legal rights.

MaximHe who seeks equity must do equity

A claimant for equitable relief must be ready to act fairly towards the other side.

A plaintiff seeking specific performance must be ready and willing to perform his part (s.16(c) Specific Relief Act; Mohammed Khaleel v Jayamma, 2026, on continuous readiness).

MaximHe who comes into equity must come with clean hands

Equitable relief is refused to a party guilty of misconduct in the same matter.

A litigant who suppresses material facts or acts unfairly can be denied relief (S.P. Chengalvaraya Naidu v Jagannath, 1994; K.D. Sharma v SAIL, 2008, writ refused for suppression).

MaximDelay defeats equity

Equity aids the vigilant.

Unexplained delay (laches) can bar equitable relief such as specific performance and writs, even within limitation.

MaximEquality is equity

Equity treats like claimants equally.

Where there is no basis for unequal division, property is divided equally; for example, contribution among co-sureties (s.146 Contract Act).

MaximEquity looks to the intent rather than the form

Substance prevails over form.

A transaction is judged by its real nature; for example, a sale with a condition of repurchase may be treated as a mortgage ("once a mortgage, always a mortgage"; no clog on redemption).

MaximEquity regards as done that which ought to be done

An agreement that equity will enforce is treated as performed.

The basis of equitable conversion and the rights of a purchaser under a contract of sale before conveyance.

MaximEquity imputes an intention to fulfil an obligation

Acts are presumed done in performance of an existing duty.

Where a person under an obligation does an act that could fulfil it, equity presumes it was done for that purpose.

MaximEquity acts in personam

Equity acts on the person.

Equitable orders bind the conscience of the party, enforced by contempt or attachment, which is why courts can act on persons within jurisdiction even about property outside it.

MaximWhere the equities are equal, the law prevails

Between equally innocent parties, the legal title wins.

A bona fide purchaser of the legal estate for value without notice takes free of prior equitable interests.

MaximWhere the equities are equal, the first in time prevails

Between competing equitable interests, the earlier prevails.

Equity's form of qui prior est tempore potior est jure (s.48 TPA).

Points to remember

  • Equity supplements the law; it follows the law and does not contradict statute.
  • He who seeks equity must do equity (s.16(c) SRA); clean hands (Chengalvaraya Naidu; K.D. Sharma).
  • Delay defeats equity (laches); equality is equity (s.146 Contract Act).
  • Substance over form (no clog on redemption); regards as done what ought to be done; acts in personam.
  • Equities equal: the law prevails; the first in time prevails.

Unit 11

International law and jurisprudence

Public international law uses Latin for its foundational ideas, and jurisprudence uses maxims to frame debates about the nature of law.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Treaties and change of circumstances
  • peremptory norms
  • sovereignty and immunity
  • territory
  • extradition
  • nature of law

11.1 Treaties

MaximPacta sunt servanda

Treaties must be kept.

Every treaty in force binds the parties and must be performed in good faith (Art. 26, VCLT); internal law is no excuse (Art. 27).

MaximRebus sic stantibus

Things standing thus.

A fundamental change of circumstances may justify terminating or withdrawing from a treaty, but only narrowly: the change must be fundamental, unforeseen, and radically transform the obligations (Art. 62, VCLT; Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros, 1997).

MaximJus cogens

Compelling law.

Peremptory norms from which no derogation is permitted, such as the prohibitions of genocide, slavery, torture and aggression. A treaty conflicting with jus cogens is void (Arts. 53 and 64, VCLT).

MaximOpinio juris sive necessitatis

An opinion of law or necessity.

The belief that a practice is legally required; with State practice, it creates customary international law (North Sea Continental Shelf, 1969).

MaximEx injuria jus non oritur

A right does not arise from a wrong.

An illegal act, such as aggression, cannot create legal rights or valid title (for example, non-recognition of territory acquired by force).

11.2 Sovereignty and territory

MaximPar in parem non habet imperium

An equal has no authority over an equal.

The basis of sovereign immunity: one State cannot be sued in the courts of another for sovereign acts (acta jure imperii). In India, s.86 CPC requires Central Government consent to sue a foreign State (Ethiopian Airlines v Ganesh Narain Saboo, 2011, no immunity for commercial acts).

MaximUti possidetis juris

As you possess under law.

New States inherit the colonial administrative boundaries at independence (Frontier Dispute, Burkina Faso v Mali, 1986).

MaximTerra nullius

Land belonging to no one.

Territory not under any State's sovereignty, which could be acquired by occupation. Inhabited territory with social and political organisation is not terra nullius (Western Sahara advisory opinion, 1975).

MaximMare liberum

The free sea.

Grotius's principle of the freedom of the high seas, now in UNCLOS Art. 87.

MaximAut dedere aut judicare

Either extradite or prosecute.

A State holding a person accused of certain international crimes must either extradite or prosecute him (Belgium v Senegal, 2012, on torture).

11.3 Jurisprudence

MaximLex injusta non est lex

An unjust law is not law.

The natural law view (Augustine, Aquinas), revived by Radbruch's formula for extreme injustice, and contested by positivists such as Hart.

MaximSalus populi suprema lex

The welfare of the people is the supreme law.

Also used in jurisprudence and constitutional law to justify State power exercised for the common good, within constitutional limits.

Points to remember

  • Pacta sunt servanda; rebus sic stantibus (narrow: Gabčíkovo); jus cogens (Arts. 53, 64 VCLT).
  • Opinio juris plus practice makes custom; ex injuria jus non oritur.
  • Par in parem non habet imperium (s.86 CPC; Ethiopian Airlines).
  • Uti possidetis; terra nullius (Western Sahara); mare liberum; aut dedere aut judicare.
  • Lex injusta non est lex (natural law v positivism).
