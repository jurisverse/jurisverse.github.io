Indemnity and guarantee

Indemnity is a promise to save another from loss; guarantee is a promise to answer for another's default. Both are contracts of security, but they differ in the number of parties and the nature of liability.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Indemnity and guarantee (ss.124 to 147, Indian Contract Act)
  • Contract of indemnity: definition, rights of indemnity holder, liability of indemnifier
  • Contract of guarantee: definition, essentials, distinction from indemnity, kinds, rights and liabilities of surety, discharge of surety

Contract of indemnity

Statutes.124

A contract by which one party promises to save the other from loss caused to him by the conduct of the promisor himself, or by the conduct of any other person, is called a contract of indemnity.

  • The promisor is the indemnifier; the promisee is the indemnity holder (indemnified).
  • The Indian definition is narrower than English law: it covers loss caused by human conduct, not loss from accidents or natural events. Indian courts, however, apply wider equitable principles, and contracts of insurance (other than life) are contracts of indemnity.

Rights of the indemnity holder: s.125

When sued, the indemnity holder is entitled to recover from the indemnifier:

  1. all damages he may be compelled to pay in any suit in respect of any matter covered by the promise;
  2. all costs he may be compelled to pay in such a suit, if he acted as a prudent person or with the indemnifier's authority;
  3. all sums he paid under a compromise of the suit, if the compromise was prudent or authorised.

When does the indemnifier's liability begin?

CaseGajanan Moreshwar Parelkar v Moreshwar Madan Mantri (1942)

Chagla J of the Bombay High Court held that an indemnity is not necessarily given by repayment after payment. If the indemnified person has incurred an absolute liability, he can call on the indemnifier to save him from it, even before he has paid anything. Otherwise an indemnity would often be worthless.

Contract of guarantee

Statutes.126

A contract of guarantee is a contract to perform the promise, or discharge the liability, of a third person in case of his default. The person who gives the guarantee is the surety; the person in respect of whose default it is given is the principal debtor; the person to whom it is given is the creditor. A guarantee may be oral or written.

Statutes.127

Anything done, or any promise made, for the benefit of the principal debtor may be a sufficient consideration to the surety for giving the guarantee.

Essentials

  • Three parties and three contracts (creditor and principal debtor; creditor and surety; surety and principal debtor, implied).
  • A primary liability of the principal debtor: if the principal's liability is void (other than for minority), the guarantee usually fails.
  • Consideration, which may be benefit to the principal debtor (s.127).
  • Consent of the surety must not be obtained by misrepresentation or concealment by the creditor (ss.142, 143).
  • All essentials of a valid contract.

Kinds of guarantee

  • Specific guarantee: for a single debt or transaction; ends when that is discharged.
  • Continuing guarantee (s.129): extends to a series of transactions (for example, an overdraft limit). It may be revoked as to future transactions by notice (s.130), and is revoked by the surety's death as to future transactions unless the contract provides otherwise (s.131).
  • Bank guarantees and performance guarantees: independent contracts between the bank and the beneficiary.
CaseU.P. State Sugar Corporation v Sumac International Ltd (1997)

A bank guarantee is an independent contract; the bank must honour it according to its terms, irrespective of disputes between the beneficiary and the party at whose instance it was given. Courts will restrain encashment only in cases of fraud of an egregious nature or irretrievable injustice.

Rights and liabilities of the surety

Liability: s.128

Statutes.128

The liability of the surety is co-extensive with that of the principal debtor, unless it is otherwise provided by the contract.

  • The creditor need not first exhaust remedies against the principal debtor.
CaseBank of Bihar v Damodar Prasad (1969)

The Supreme Court held that the surety's liability is immediate; the creditor can sue the surety without first suing the principal debtor, unless the contract says otherwise. Requiring the creditor to pursue the debtor first would defeat the purpose of a guarantee.

CaseLalit Kumar Jain v Union of India (2021)

Approval of a resolution plan for a corporate debtor under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code does not automatically discharge a personal guarantor's liability; the guarantor's liability arises from an independent contract.

Points to remember

  • Indemnity (s.124) is narrower in India: loss by human conduct; rights of the indemnity holder in s.125; liability may arise before payment (Gajanan Moreshwar).
  • Guarantee (s.126) has three parties; consideration may be benefit to the debtor (s.127).
  • Surety's liability is co-extensive and immediate (s.128; Bank of Bihar).
  • Rights: subrogation (s.140), securities (s.141), indemnity (s.145), contribution (ss.146, 147).
  • Discharge: variance, release, giving time, impairing remedy, loss of security, misrepresentation.
  • Bank guarantees are independent; injunction only for egregious fraud or irretrievable injustice (Sumac).

Bailment and pledge

In bailment, goods move from one person to another for a purpose, on the condition that they will be returned or disposed of as directed. Pledge is bailment as security for a debt.

  • Rights and duties of bailor and bailee
  • Termination

Definition and essentials

Statutes.148

A bailment is the delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose, upon a contract that they shall, when the purpose is accomplished, be returned or otherwise disposed of according to the directions of the person delivering them. The person delivering is the bailor; the person to whom they are delivered is the bailee. Explanation: if a person already in possession of goods of another contracts to hold them as bailee, he becomes the bailee though the goods were not delivered by way of bailment.

Essentials:

  1. Delivery of possession (actual or constructive), not ownership.
  2. Of movable goods (not money, unless specific coins or notes).
  3. For a purpose.
  4. Under a contract (express or implied).
  5. Return of the same goods (in original or altered form) or disposal according to directions.
  • Delivery to a servant is not bailment (the master retains possession). Keeping a coat on a hook in a restaurant without handing it to a servant may not be bailment (Ultzen v Nicols, 1894, held the restaurant liable because a waiter took the coat).
CaseState of Gujarat v Memon Mahomed Haji Hasam (1967)

Vehicles seized by customs officers and kept in their custody deteriorated and were later ordered to be returned. The Supreme Court held that the State was a bailee and liable to return them or pay their value; bailment can arise without a contract, from the possession of goods belonging to another.

Duties of the bailor

  • Disclose known faults in the goods that materially interfere with their use or expose the bailee to extraordinary risks (s.150). In bailment for hire, the bailor is liable for faults whether or not he knew of them.
  • Bear necessary expenses in gratuitous bailments (s.158).
  • Indemnify the bailee for loss due to the bailor's defective title (s.164).
  • Receive back the goods when the purpose is accomplished.

The railway, as bailee of goods, failed to show that it had taken reasonable care of goods lost in transit. The Supreme Court held it liable under ss.151 and 152.

Rights of the bailee

  • Particular lien (s.170): where the bailee has rendered services involving labour or skill in respect of the goods, he may retain them until paid for those services.
  • General lien (s.171): bankers, factors, wharfingers, attorneys of a High Court and policy brokers may retain goods bailed to them as security for a general balance of account, unless there is a contract to the contrary.
  • Compensation from the bailor for faults and defective title; necessary expenses; right to sue third parties for wrongful deprivation or injury (s.180).

Termination of bailment

  • On expiry of the period or accomplishment of the purpose.
  • By an act of the bailee inconsistent with the conditions of the bailment: the bailor may terminate (s.153).
  • A gratuitous bailment may be terminated by the bailor at any time, and on the death of either party (s.159, s.162).

Finder of goods

A finder is subject to the responsibility of a bailee (s.71). He may retain the goods until compensated for expenses (s.168) and may sell them if the owner cannot be found, or the goods are perishable, or lawful charges reach two-thirds of their value (s.169).

Pledge

Statutes.172

The bailment of goods as security for payment of a debt or performance of a promise is called "pledge". The bailor is the "pawnor" and the bailee the "pawnee".

Rights of the pawnee

  • Retainer for the debt, interest and necessary expenses (s.173); not for other debts unless agreed (s.174).
  • Extraordinary expenses incurred for preservation (s.175).
  • On default by the pawnor: sue on the debt and retain the goods as collateral, or sell the goods after giving reasonable notice of sale (s.176). He may recover any deficiency from the pawnor and must pay any surplus to him.
CaseLallan Prasad v Rahmat Ali (1967)

A pawnee who could not return the pledged goods (having disposed of them without notice) could not sue for the debt. A pawnee suing for the debt must be in a position to return the goods on payment.

Rights and duties of the pawnor

  • Right to redeem at any time before actual sale, on paying the debt and expenses (s.177).
  • Duties corresponding to those of a bailor (disclosure, compensation for defective title).

Contract of agency

An agent brings his principal into legal relations with third persons. What the agent does within his authority, the principal does himself.

What the syllabus lists for this unit

  • Creation of agency
  • Rights and duties of agent
  • Delegation of authority
  • Personal liability of agent
  • Relations of principal with third parties
  • Termination of agency (ss.182 to 238)

Definition

Statutes.182

An "agent" is a person employed to do any act for another, or to represent another in dealings with third persons. The person for whom such act is done, or who is so represented, is called the "principal".

  • Any person of age of majority and sound mind may employ an agent (s.183); any person may become an agent, but a minor or person of unsound mind agent is not responsible to the principal (s.184).
  • No consideration is necessary to create an agency (s.185).

CaseLakshminarayan Ram Gopal and Son v Government of Hyderabad (1954)

The distinction between an agent and a servant was explained: an agent is subject to the principal's directions but not to his control as to the manner of doing the work; a servant acts under the direct control and supervision of the master.

Creation of agency

  1. Express agreement: oral or written, for example a power of attorney.
  2. Implied agency (s.187): inferred from the circumstances, conduct or relationship. Includes agency by holding out and by estoppel (s.237): where the principal by his words or conduct induces third persons to believe that acts were within the agent's authority, he is bound.
  3. Agency of necessity (s.189): an agent has authority, in an emergency, to do all acts to protect his principal from loss as would be done by a person of ordinary prudence in his own case under similar circumstances.
  4. Ratification (ss.196 to 200): where acts are done by one person on behalf of another without his knowledge or authority, the latter may ratify or disown them. Ratification relates back to the date of the act.
CaseGreat Northern Railway Co v Swaffield (1874)

A horse sent by rail was not collected at the destination. The railway paid for its stabling. Held: the railway was an agent of necessity and could recover the charges from the owner.

Conditions for valid ratification

  • The act must have been done on behalf of the principal (Keighley, Maxsted and Co v Durant, 1901: an undisclosed principal cannot ratify).
  • The principal must have been in existence and competent at the time of the act and at ratification.
  • Ratification must be with full knowledge of the facts (s.198).
  • The whole transaction must be ratified, not a part (s.199).
  • It cannot subject a third person to damages or terminate his rights (s.200).
  • A void or illegal act cannot be ratified.

Delegation of authority: sub-agents (ss.190 to 195)

  • Delegatus non potest delegare: an agent cannot delegate acts he has expressly or impliedly undertaken to perform personally, unless by custom of trade or the nature of the agency (s.190).
  • Sub-agent (s.191): a person employed by and acting under the control of the original agent. If properly appointed, the principal is bound by his acts; the agent is responsible to the principal for him, and the sub-agent is responsible to the agent (s.192).
  • Substituted agent (s.194): a person named by the agent with the express or implied authority of the principal to act for the principal; he becomes the principal's agent directly.

Duties of the agent (ss.211 to 218)

  • Act according to the principal's directions or, in their absence, according to custom (s.211).
  • Act with reasonable skill and diligence (s.212).
  • Render proper accounts (s.213).
  • Communicate with the principal in cases of difficulty (s.214).
  • Not deal on his own account without the principal's consent; the principal may repudiate such a transaction and claim any benefit (ss.215, 216).
  • Pay to the principal sums received on his account (s.218).

Rights of the agent

  • Remuneration, which becomes due on completion of the act (ss.219, 220; no remuneration for misconduct in the business).
  • Lien on the principal's property received by him, for remuneration and expenses (s.221).
  • Indemnity for lawful acts done in exercise of authority (s.222), for acts done in good faith (s.223), but not for criminal acts (s.224).
  • Compensation for injury caused by the principal's neglect (s.225).

Relations of principal with third parties

  • Contracts entered into by an agent within his authority bind the principal as if he had made them himself (s.226).
  • Where the agent exceeds authority and the excess is separable, only the authorised part binds the principal (s.227); if not separable, the principal is not bound (s.228).
  • Notice to the agent in the course of business is notice to the principal (s.229).
  • Misrepresentation or fraud by the agent in the course of business has the same effect as if made by the principal (s.238).

Personal liability of the agent

Statutes.230

In the absence of any contract to that effect, an agent cannot personally enforce contracts entered into by him on behalf of his principal, nor is he personally bound by them. Such a contract is presumed to exist where the contract is made by an agent for the sale or purchase of goods for a merchant resident abroad; where the agent does not disclose the name of his principal; or where the principal, though disclosed, cannot be sued.

  • An agent is also personally liable when he acts without authority (breach of warranty of authority, s.235), when he signs a negotiable instrument without indicating he signs as agent (s.28, NI Act), and when the principal is undisclosed (the third party may sue either, s.233).

Undisclosed principal

Where the agent contracts without disclosing that he is an agent, the principal may require performance, but the third party has the same rights against the principal as he would have had against the agent (s.231). The third party may elect to hold either the agent or the principal liable (s.233).

Termination of agency

Statutes.201

An agency is terminated by the principal revoking his authority; by the agent renouncing the business of the agency; by the business of the agency being completed; by either the principal or agent dying or becoming of unsound mind; or by the principal being adjudicated an insolvent.

  • Irrevocable agency: where the agent has himself an interest in the property forming the subject-matter of the agency, the agency cannot, in the absence of an express contract, be terminated to the prejudice of that interest (s.202), for example where an agent is authorised to sell goods and pay himself out of the proceeds a debt owed to him.
CaseSyed Abdul Khader v Rami Reddy (1979)

The Supreme Court held that where an agency is created for securing a benefit to the agent (agency coupled with interest), it cannot be revoked by the principal to the prejudice of the agent's interest, under s.202.

  • Revocation after partial exercise of authority is not effective as to acts already done (s.204); compensation is payable for revocation without sufficient cause before the end of a fixed period (s.205); reasonable notice is required (s.206).
  • Termination takes effect, as regards the agent, when it becomes known to him, and as regards third persons, when it becomes known to them (s.208).
Exam tip

In a question on creation of agency, list all five modes (express, implied, holding out or estoppel, necessity, ratification) and give one case each. For termination, always mention s.202 irrevocable agency.

Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseRamesh Chand v Suresh Chand (2025)

A General Power of Attorney only creates an agency; it does not transfer title. A GPA without an interest of the agent is revocable and ends on the principal's death.

Points to remember

  • A GPA creates agency, not title (Ramesh Chand, 2025).
  • Agent defined in s.182; no consideration needed (s.185).
  • Agency arises by express or implied agreement, estoppel (s.237), necessity (s.189; Swaffield) and ratification (ss.196 to 200).
  • Sub-agent (s.191) vs substituted agent (s.194).
  • Duties: follow directions, skill, accounts, not deal on own account; rights: remuneration, lien, indemnity.
  • Agent generally not personally liable (s.230), except foreign principal, undisclosed principal, principal who cannot be sued.
  • Agency coupled with interest is irrevocable (s.202; Syed Abdul Khader).

Contract of sale of goods

The Sale of Goods Act governs when ownership and risk pass, what the seller impliedly promises about the goods, and how an unpaid seller can protect himself.

Formation: s.4

Statutes.4

A contract of sale of goods is a contract whereby the seller transfers or agrees to transfer the property in goods to the buyer for a price. Where the property passes at once, it is a sale; where it is to pass at a future time or on a condition, it is an agreement to sell, which becomes a sale when the time elapses or the condition is fulfilled.

Essentials: two parties; goods; transfer of general property; price in money; all essentials of a contract.

Subject-matter: goods

  • "Goods" means every kind of movable property other than actionable claims and money, including stock and shares, growing crops, grass and things attached to land which are agreed to be severed before sale (s.2(7)).
  • Existing (specific or ascertained, or unascertained), future and contingent goods (s.6). A sale of future goods operates only as an agreement to sell.
  • If specific goods have perished without the seller's knowledge at the time of the contract, the contract is void (s.7); if they perish before risk passes, the agreement to sell is avoided (s.8).
  • Price may be fixed by the contract, by a course of dealing, or be a reasonable price (s.9); or fixed by valuation of a third party (s.10).

Conditions and warranties: s.12

  • Condition: a stipulation essential to the main purpose of the contract, the breach of which gives a right to treat the contract as repudiated.
  • Warranty: a stipulation collateral to the main purpose, the breach of which gives a right to damages but not to reject the goods.
  • Whether a stipulation is a condition or warranty depends on the construction of the contract, not on the words used.
  • s.13: the buyer may waive a condition or treat its breach as a breach of warranty; where the contract is not severable and the buyer has accepted the goods, breach of condition can only be treated as breach of warranty.

CaseRowland v Divall (1923)

A buyer used a car for four months before discovering that the seller had no title (it was stolen). Held: the buyer could recover the full price; there was a total failure of consideration for breach of the condition as to title.

A second-hand reaping machine described as "new the previous year and only used to cut 50 acres" turned out to be old and much used. Held: breach of the condition that goods correspond with description.

A hot water bottle bought from a chemist burst and injured the buyer's wife. Held: the purpose was obvious from the nature of the goods, so the condition as to fitness for purpose was implied.

Subject to the provisions of this Act and of any other law for the time being in force, there is no implied warranty or condition as to the quality or fitness for any particular purpose of goods supplied under a contract of sale.

Exceptions: fitness for purpose (s.16(1)), merchantable quality (s.16(2)), trade usage (s.16(3)), sale by sample (s.17), fraud or misrepresentation by the seller, and statutory protection under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019. Courts and consumer laws have largely shifted the rule towards caveat venditor.

Definition and nature

Statutes.4

"Partnership" is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all. Persons who have entered into partnership with one another are individually called "partners" and collectively "a firm", and the name under which their business is carried on is called the "firm name".

Essentials:

  1. An agreement (partnership arises from contract, not from status: s.5).
  2. To carry on a business (every trade, occupation and profession: s.2(b)).
  3. To share profits (sharing losses is not essential to the definition).
  4. Mutual agency: the business is carried on by all or any of them acting for all. This is the true test.

Test of partnership: s.6

In determining whether a group of persons is a firm, regard must be had to the real relation between the parties, as shown by all relevant facts taken together. Sharing of profits or gross returns is not by itself conclusive. Receipt of a share of profits by a lender, a servant or agent as remuneration, a widow or child of a deceased partner, or a seller of goodwill does not by itself make the recipient a partner.

CaseCox v Hickman (1860)

Creditors of a business who took over its management and shared profits were held not to be partners; the true test is whether the business was carried on on their behalf, that is, mutual agency.

  • Third parties can sue an unregistered firm; non-registration affects only the firm's and partners' rights to sue.

Relations of partners to one another (ss.9 to 17)

Rights

  • To take part in the conduct of business (s.12(a)).
  • To be consulted; decisions on ordinary matters by majority; no change in the nature of business without the consent of all (s.12(c)).
  • To have access to and inspect books (s.12(d)).
  • To share profits equally, in the absence of agreement (s.13(b)).
  • Interest on advances beyond capital at 6% per annum (s.13(d)); no interest on capital unless agreed.
  • To be indemnified for payments made and liabilities incurred in the ordinary and proper conduct of business (s.13(e)).
  • No remuneration for taking part in business, unless agreed (s.13(a)).

Duties

  • To carry on business to the greatest common advantage, be just and faithful, and render true accounts (s.9).
  • To indemnify the firm for loss caused by fraud (s.10) and wilful neglect (s.13(f)).
  • To attend diligently to duties (s.12(b)).
  • Not to carry on competing business; to account for private profits from firm transactions, property or name (s.16).

Property of the firm: s.14

All property, rights and interests originally brought into the stock of the firm, or acquired for the firm or in the course of its business, including goodwill, are firm property, held and used exclusively for the business (s.15).

Relations of partners to third parties

  • Partner as agent: every partner is an agent of the firm for the purpose of its business (s.18).
  • Implied authority (s.19(1)): the act of a partner done to carry on, in the usual way, business of the kind carried on by the firm binds the firm.
  • Restrictions (s.19(2)): in the absence of usage or custom, implied authority does not extend to submitting a dispute to arbitration, opening a bank account in his own name, compromising or relinquishing a claim, withdrawing a suit, admitting liability in a suit, acquiring or transferring immovable property of the firm, or entering into partnership on behalf of the firm.
  • Liability: every partner is liable, jointly with all other partners and also severally, for all acts of the firm done while he is a partner (s.25); for wrongful acts of a partner in the ordinary course of business (s.26); and for misapplication of money (s.27).
  • Holding out (s.28): anyone who represents himself, or knowingly permits himself to be represented, as a partner is liable to anyone who has on the faith of that representation given credit to the firm.

Unit 6

Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881

A negotiable instrument transfers money claims by delivery or endorsement, and gives a holder in due course a title better than his transferor's. Section 138 adds criminal liability for dishonoured cheques.

What the syllabus lists for this unit

  • holder and holder in due course
  • negotiation and endorsement
  • crossing

Meaning and features

A negotiable instrument is a written document by which a right is created in favour of some person and which is freely transferable. Features: transferable by delivery (bearer) or endorsement and delivery (order); the holder in due course gets a good title free from defects of prior parties; the holder can sue in his own name; certain presumptions apply (s.118).

Kinds

Statutes.4

A promissory note is an instrument in writing (not being a bank note or currency note) containing an unconditional undertaking, signed by the maker, to pay a certain sum of money only to, or to the order of, a certain person, or to the bearer of the instrument.

Statutes.5

A bill of exchange is an instrument in writing containing an unconditional order, signed by the maker, directing a certain person to pay a certain sum of money only to, or to the order of, a certain person or to the bearer of the instrument.

Statutes.6

A cheque is a bill of exchange drawn on a specified banker and not expressed to be payable otherwise than on demand, and it includes the electronic image of a truncated cheque and a cheque in electronic form.

  • A promissory note payable to bearer cannot be made by anyone except the RBI or the Central Government (s.31, RBI Act, 1934).

Holder and holder in due course

  • Holder (s.8): the person entitled in his own name to possession of the instrument and to receive or recover the amount due.
  • Holder in due course (s.9): a person who, for consideration, became the possessor of a bearer instrument or the payee or endorsee of an order instrument, before the amount became payable, without sufficient cause to believe that any defect existed in the title of the person from whom he derived his title.
  • Privileges of a holder in due course: he takes free from defects of title of prior parties (s.53); every prior party is liable to him until the instrument is duly satisfied (s.36); estoppel against denying the original validity of the instrument (s.120) and the payee's capacity to endorse (s.121).

Presumptions: ss.118 and 139

  • s.118: until the contrary is proved, it is presumed that every instrument was made for consideration, on the date it bears, accepted within a reasonable time, transferred before maturity, endorsed in the order they appear, stamped, and that the holder is a holder in due course.
  • s.139: the holder of a cheque is presumed to have received it in discharge of a debt or other liability.
CaseRangappa v Sri Mohan (2010)

A three-judge bench held that the presumption under s.139 includes the existence of a legally enforceable debt or liability. It is rebuttable, and the accused may rebut it on a preponderance of probabilities, including by relying on the complainant's own materials.

Negotiation and endorsement

  • Negotiation (s.14): transfer to any person so as to constitute him the holder.
  • Bearer instruments are negotiated by delivery (s.47); order instruments by endorsement and delivery (s.48).
  • Endorsement (s.15): signature of the maker or holder on the instrument (or an allonge) for the purpose of negotiation.
  • Kinds: blank, special (in full), restrictive, partial (not valid as negotiation), conditional, sans recours (without recourse to the endorser).

Crossing of cheques (ss.123 to 131)

  • General crossing (s.123): two transverse parallel lines, with or without "and company"; payable only through a bank.
  • Special crossing (s.124): the name of a banker added; payable only to that banker.
  • "Not negotiable" crossing (s.130): the holder cannot give a better title than he has.
  • Account payee: payable only to the payee's account; developed by banking practice and recognised by s.123A.
  • A paying banker who pays a crossed cheque in good faith and without negligence according to the crossing is protected (s.128); a collecting banker who receives payment in good faith without negligence for a customer is protected (s.131).

Dishonour

  • By non-acceptance (s.91) or by non-payment (s.92).
  • Notice of dishonour must be given to prior parties to make them liable (s.93), except in cases where it is dispensed with (s.98).
  • Noting and protest (ss.99, 100): certification of dishonour by a notary public.

Dishonour of cheque for insufficiency of funds: s.138

Statutes.138

Where a cheque drawn by a person on an account maintained by him with a banker for payment of money to another, for the discharge of any debt or other liability, is returned unpaid because the amount of money standing to the credit of that account is insufficient or it exceeds the arrangement made with the bank, such person shall be deemed to have committed an offence and shall be punished with imprisonment up to two years, or fine up to twice the amount of the cheque, or both, provided (a) the cheque has been presented within its validity period (three months); (b) the payee has made a demand in writing within thirty days of receiving information of dishonour; and (c) the drawer has failed to pay within fifteen days of receiving the notice.

  • Complaint must be filed within one month of the cause of action (after expiry of the fifteen days) (s.142(1)(b)); delay may be condoned.
  • Jurisdiction (s.142(2), added 2015): the court within whose local limits the payee's bank branch where the cheque was delivered for collection is situated. This reversed Dashrath Rupsingh Rathod v State of Maharashtra (2014), which had held the drawee bank's location decisive.
  • Offences by companies (s.141): every person in charge of and responsible for the conduct of business is liable.
  • Interim compensation (s.143A, 2018): up to 20% of the cheque amount during trial; and on appeal, the appellate court may order deposit of at least 20% of the fine or compensation (s.148).
  • Offence is compoundable (s.147); summary trial (s.143).
CaseBir Singh v Mukesh Kumar (2019)

A person who signs a blank cheque and hands it to the payee is liable under s.138 if it is filled in later and dishonoured, unless he proves it was not issued in discharge of a debt; the presumption under s.139 applies.

A Constitution Bench issued directions to reduce the huge pendency of cheque-dishonour cases: summary trials, one trial for multiple cheques arising from the same transaction within twelve months, issue of summons by email, and more courts.

1.Continuing guarantee
Model answer

A guarantee that extends to a series of transactions (s.129), such as a guarantee for a running account or for an agent's faithful conduct over time.

Revocation:

  • By notice, as to future transactions (s.130).
  • By the surety's death, unless otherwise agreed, as to future transactions (s.131).

The surety remains liable for transactions entered into before revocation.

2.Bailee's lien
Model answer
  • Particular lien (s.170): a bailee who has rendered services involving labour or skill on the goods may retain them until paid. Example: a tailor or repairer.
  • General lien (s.171): bankers, factors, wharfingers, attorneys of a High Court and policy brokers may retain any goods bailed to them as security for a general balance of account, unless otherwise agreed.

A lien is a right of retention only, not a right of sale, except under statute or agreement.

3.Pledge by non-owners
Model answer

A pledge by a person who is not the owner is valid in the following cases:

  • a mercantile agent in possession with the owner's consent, acting in the ordinary course of business, with a pledgee in good faith (s.178);
  • a person in possession under a voidable contract not rescinded at the time (s.178A);
  • a person with a limited interest, to the extent of that interest (s.179);
  • a seller or buyer in possession after sale (ss.30 and 30(2) of the Sale of Goods Act).
4.Sub-agent and substituted agent
Model answer
  • A sub-agent is employed by and acts under the control of the original agent (s.191). A sub-agent may be appointed only where custom or the nature of the agency permits (s.190).
  • A properly appointed sub-agent binds the principal; the agent is responsible to the principal for the sub-agent (s.192).
  • The principal can sue a sub-agent directly only for fraud or wilful wrong.
  • A substituted agent is named by the agent at the principal's authority, to act for the principal directly. Privity is created between the principal and the substitute (s.194).
5.Caveat emptor
Model answer

"Let the buyer beware" (s.16, Sale of Goods Act): there is no implied condition as to quality or fitness, subject to exceptions:

  • fitness for a particular purpose made known, where the buyer relies on the seller's skill (s.16(1));
  • merchantable quality in a sale by description (s.16(2));
  • usage of trade (s.16(3));
  • fraud; and
  • a sale by sample (s.17).

The rule has been eroded by consumer protection law.

6.Rights of an unpaid seller against the goods
Model answer

An unpaid seller (s.45) has the following rights:

  • lien: retaining possession until paid (ss.47 to 49);
  • stoppage in transit: on the buyer's insolvency (ss.50 to 52);
  • re-sale: for perishable goods, after notice, or where the right is reserved (s.54).

The seller also has rights against the buyer personally, such as a suit for the price and damages (ss.55 and 56).

7.Holder in due course
Model answer

Under s.9 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, a holder in due course is a person who:

  • became the holder for consideration;
  • before the amount became payable; and
  • without sufficient cause to believe that any defect existed in the title of the person from whom he derived his title.

Privileges:

  • He takes the instrument free from defects (s.53).
  • Prior parties remain liable to him until the instrument is duly satisfied (s.36).
  • Estoppel against denying validity (ss.120 to 122).
  • The presumption of holding in due course (s.118(g)).
8.Crossing of cheques
Model answer

A crossing is a direction to the paying banker to pay only through a banker (ss.123 to 131).

  • General crossing: two parallel transverse lines, with or without "& Co.". Payment is made only through a banker.
  • Special crossing: the name of a banker is added. Payment is made only to that banker.
  • "Not negotiable" (s.130): the transferee gets no better title than the transferor.
  • "Account payee": a banking practice that directs the collecting banker to credit only the payee's account.

A paying banker who pays in due course is protected (s.128). A collecting banker who receives payment in good faith and without negligence for a customer is protected (s.131).

9.Implied authority of a partner
Model answer

Under s.19 of the Partnership Act, the act of a partner done to carry on, in the usual way, the business of the firm binds the firm.

Under s.19(2), unless trade usage permits, a partner cannot:

  • submit disputes to arbitration;
  • open a bank account in his own name;
  • compromise or relinquish claims;
  • withdraw a suit;
  • admit liability in a suit;
  • acquire or transfer immovable property; or
  • enter into partnership on behalf of the firm.

The firm is bound by acts under implied authority, and by a partner's wrongful acts in the ordinary course of business (s.26).

10.Explain the rights of a surety and the modes of discharge of a surety.
Model answer

Contract of guarantee (s.126)

A contract to perform the promise, or discharge the liability, of a third person in case of his default. There are three parties: the creditor, the principal debtor and the surety. Anything done for the benefit of the principal debtor is sufficient consideration (s.127). The surety's liability is co-extensive with that of the principal debtor (s.128). The creditor may proceed against the surety first (Bank of Bihar v Damodar Prasad, 1969).

Rights of the surety

  1. Against the creditor:
  • the benefit of every security the creditor holds against the principal debtor, whether or not the surety knew of it (s.141);
  • to require the creditor to sue the principal first, where so agreed.
  1. Against the principal debtor:
  • subrogation: on payment, the surety is invested with all the creditor's rights against the debtor (s.140);
  • implied indemnity: for sums rightfully paid (s.145).
  1. Against co-sureties:
  • contribution in equal shares (s.146), or in proportion to the amounts guaranteed (s.147).

Discharge of the surety

  • revocation of a continuing guarantee by notice or death (ss.130 and 131);
  • variance in the terms of the contract without the surety's consent (s.133);
  • release or discharge of the principal debtor (s.134);
  • composition with, promise to give time to, or promise not to sue the principal debtor (s.135). Mere forbearance to sue does not discharge (s.137), nor does an agreement with a third person (s.136);
  • the creditor's act or omission impairing the surety's eventual remedy (s.139);
  • loss of security by the creditor, to the extent of its value (s.141);
  • a guarantee obtained by misrepresentation or by concealment (ss.142 and 143).

Insolvency context

The surety's liability survives the principal debtor's insolvency resolution. Creditors may proceed against personal guarantors (Lalit Kumar Jain v Union of India, 2021).

Conclusion

The surety is a favoured debtor in law. Because his liability is secondary, the Contract Act protects him through rights of subrogation, indemnity and contribution, and by discharge where the creditor prejudices him.

11.What are conditions and warranties? Explain the implied conditions in a contract of sale of goods.
Model answer

Definitions

  • Condition (s.12(2)): a stipulation essential to the main purpose of the contract. Its breach gives a right to treat the contract as repudiated.
  • Warranty (s.12(3)): a stipulation collateral to the main purpose. Its breach gives a right to claim damages, but not to reject the goods.
  • Whether a stipulation is a condition depends on the construction of the contract (s.12(4)).
  • A condition may be treated as a warranty, by waiver or by acceptance of the goods (s.13).

Implied conditions

  1. Title (s.14(a)): the seller has the right to sell (Rowland v Divall, 1923).
  2. Description (s.15): the goods correspond to the description (Varley v Whipp, 1900).
  3. Fitness for purpose (s.16(1)): the buyer makes the purpose known and relies on the seller's skill (Priest v Last, 1903, a hot-water bottle; Grant v Australian Knitting Mills, 1936).
  4. Merchantable quality (s.16(2)): in a sale by description by a seller dealing in such goods. Hidden defects are covered; defects that examination ought to have revealed are not.
  5. Sale by sample (s.17): the bulk corresponds to the sample, there is a reasonable opportunity to compare, and there are no hidden defects.
  6. Wholesomeness: for eatables (Frost v Aylesbury Dairy, 1905).

Implied warranties

  • quiet possession (s.14(b));
  • freedom from encumbrances (s.14(c)).

Caveat emptor

Section 16 states the rule; the conditions above are its exceptions.

Conclusion

The implied conditions protect buyers. Consumer protection law, including product liability under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, adds to this protection.

12.Explain the modes of dissolution of a partnership firm and the consequences of dissolution.
Model answer

Dissolution of a firm (s.39)

Dissolution of the partnership between all the partners of a firm. It differs from dissolution of partnership, which is a change in the relationship among partners, such as on retirement.

Modes

  1. By agreement (s.40): with the consent of all partners.
  2. Compulsory (s.41):
  • adjudication of all partners, or all but one, as insolvent;
  • the business becomes unlawful.
  1. On contingencies (s.42), unless otherwise agreed:
  • expiry of the fixed term;
  • completion of the adventure;
  • death of a partner;
  • insolvency of a partner.
  1. By notice in a partnership at will (s.43).
  2. By the court (s.44), at the suit of a partner, on grounds of:
  • unsoundness of mind;
  • permanent incapacity;
  • misconduct prejudicial to the business;
  • persistent breach of agreement;
  • transfer of the whole interest;
  • continuous losses; or
  • just and equitable grounds.

Consequences

  • Partners remain liable to third parties until public notice of dissolution is given (s.45).
  • Each partner may have the business wound up and the property applied to pay debts, with the surplus distributed (s.46).
  • Continuing authority to wind up affairs (s.47).
  • Settlement of accounts (s.48):
  • losses are paid first from profits, then capital, then partners individually in their profit-sharing ratio;
  • assets are applied to outside debts, then partners' advances, then capital, then surplus.
  • Firm debts and private debts (s.49).
  • The sale of goodwill and restraint on competition (ss.53 and 55).
  • Garner v Murray (1904): a solvent partner is not liable for an insolvent partner's capital deficiency. Indian courts generally follow this rule.

Conclusion

Dissolution ends the firm's business. The Act protects third parties through the public notice requirement and provides an orderly settlement of accounts.

13.Discuss the offence of dishonour of a cheque under section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act.
Model answer

Ingredients

  1. A cheque drawn on an account maintained by the drawer.
  2. For the discharge of a legally enforceable debt or liability, wholly or partly.
  3. Presented within its validity (three months).
  4. Returned unpaid for insufficiency of funds, or because it exceeds the arranged amount. Courts have included "account closed", "payment stopped" and "signature mismatch" (NEPC Micon v Magma Leasing, 1999; Laxmi Dyechem v State of Gujarat, 2012).
  5. A written demand notice by the payee within 30 days of information of dishonour.
  6. Failure of the drawer to pay within 15 days of receiving the notice.
  7. A complaint filed within one month of the cause of action (s.142), with delay condonable.

Presumptions

  • Sections 118 and 139: the cheque is presumed to be for a legally enforceable debt.
  • The presumption is rebuttable by a probable defence on a preponderance of probabilities (Rangappa v Sri Mohan, 2010; Basalingappa v Mudibasappa, 2019).

Companies (s.141)

Every person in charge of and responsible for the conduct of business is vicariously liable. Specific averments are needed (SMS Pharmaceuticals v Neeta Bhalla, 2005). Directors cannot be prosecuted where the company is not arraigned (Aneeta Hada, 2012).

Procedure

  • Summary trial (s.143).
  • Interim compensation of up to 20% (s.143A).
  • Deposit of up to 20% in appeal (s.148).
  • Compounding at any stage (s.147; Damodar S. Prabhu, 2010, on graded costs).
  • Punishment: up to two years, or a fine up to twice the cheque amount, or both.
  • Jurisdiction lies where the payee's bank branch is (s.142(2), 2015 amendment).
  • In re Expeditious Trial of Cases under Section 138 (2021) gave directions to speed up trials.

Conclusion

Section 138 converts a civil liability into a criminal offence to promote credibility of cheques. The courts have balanced its deterrent purpose with compounding and speedy summary trials.

14.C lends ₹5 lakh to P on S's guarantee, repayable on 1 June. On P's request, C agrees in writing to extend the time for repayment by one year, without S's knowledge. P defaults. C sues S. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Is S discharged by C's agreement to give time to P?

Law: Under s.135 of the Contract Act, a contract between the creditor and the principal debtor by which the creditor makes a composition with, or promises to give time to, or not to sue, the principal debtor discharges the surety, unless the surety assents. Mere forbearance to sue does not discharge the surety (s.137).

Application: C made a binding promise in writing to give P one year's further time, without S's consent. This is more than mere forbearance.

Conclusion: S is discharged. C can recover only from P.

15.B tells a seller that he needs a pump to lift water from a 40-metre deep well. Relying on the seller's recommendation, B buys a pump, which can lift water only from 20 metres. Advise B.
Model answer

Issue: Is there a breach of the implied condition of fitness for purpose?

Law: Under s.16(1) of the Sale of Goods Act, where the buyer expressly or by implication makes known the particular purpose and relies on the seller's skill or judgement, and the goods are of a description the seller deals in, there is an implied condition that the goods are reasonably fit for that purpose (Priest v Last, 1903).

Application: B disclosed the purpose (40 metres) and relied on the seller. The pump is unfit.

Conclusion: There is a breach of condition. B may reject the pump and recover the price with damages. If he has already accepted it, he may treat the breach as a breach of warranty and claim damages (s.59). A consumer complaint is also available.

16.A appoints B as his agent to buy cotton. B, without telling A, sells his own cotton to A at the market price. Later, A discovers this. What are A's rights?
Model answer

Issue: Can an agent deal on his own account in the business of the agency without disclosure?

Law: Under s.215 of the Contract Act, if an agent deals on his own account without the principal's consent, and without disclosing all material facts, the principal may repudiate the transaction if it was disadvantageous to him or if the agent concealed material facts. Under s.216, the principal is entitled to any benefit the agent made.

Application: B secretly sold his own cotton to A. Even at market price, A may repudiate, and if B made any profit, A may claim it.

Conclusion: A may repudiate the purchase, or affirm it and claim any secret benefit B made.

17.D issues a cheque for ₹3 lakh to E towards repayment of a loan. Before presentation, D closes his bank account. The cheque is returned with the remark "account closed". E sends a notice, and D does not pay. D argues that s.138 applies only to "insufficiency of funds". Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Does dishonour for "account closed" attract s.138?

Law: NEPC Micon v Magma Leasing (1999): "account closed" is a species of "insufficiency of funds", and the drawer cannot escape by closing the account. "Payment stopped" is also covered if the account has insufficient funds or there is no valid reason (Modi Cements v Kuchil Kumar Nandi, 1998). The presumption under s.139 applies.

Application: The cheque was issued for a debt, presented in time, and dishonoured. The notice was given and payment not made.

Conclusion: D is liable under s.138. E should file a complaint within one month of the expiry of the 15-day notice period, in the court where E's bank branch is located.
