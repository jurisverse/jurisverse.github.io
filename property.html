Unit 1

Jurisprudential contours of property

Property is not a thing but a bundle of rights over things, recognised and limited by law. Its meaning has widened from land and goods to intangible and "new" forms of wealth.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Concept and meaning of property
  • new property
  • government largesse
  • Kinds: movable and immovable, tangible and intangible, intellectual property (copyright, patents, designs, trademarks)
  • Private and public property
  • natural resources
  • privatisation
  • Capitalist and socialist analysis
  • Possession and ownership
  • finder of lost goods
  • Social functions of property

Concept and meaning

  • Salmond: property in its widest sense includes all a person's legal rights (proprietary and personal); in the narrower sense, proprietary rights in rem (ownership of material things and of incorporeal rights).
  • Bentham: property is nothing but a basis of expectation: an expectation of deriving advantages from a thing because of the relation in which we stand to it.
  • Hohfeld and the "bundle of rights": rights to possess, use, exclude, transfer, and to income and capital value.
  • Constitutional status: Art. 300A (no deprivation save by authority of law); right to property is a constitutional and human right (Vidya Devi v State of HP, 2020).

New property and government largesse

Charles Reich ("The New Property", 1964) argued that government benefits (licences, jobs, contracts, welfare, franchises) are a new form of property deserving procedural protection. Indian courts protect largesse through Art. 14: the State must not distribute it arbitrarily (Ramana Dayaram Shetty v International Airport Authority, 1979; Centre for Public Interest Litigation v Union of India, 2012 on spectrum).

A right to enter land and cut and take away trees (other than standing timber) is a benefit arising out of land and therefore immovable property; but where the contract is to cut trees as standing timber soon, it is movable.

Private and public property

  • Private property: owned by individuals, companies, associations.
  • Public property: owned by the State or held for public use (roads, parks, rivers).
  • Natural resources are held by the State in public trust for the people (M.C. Mehta v Kamal Nath, 1997); allocation must be fair and transparent.
  • Privatisation of public property (disinvestment of public sector undertakings, public-private partnerships, leasing of airports and ports) raises questions of fair value, transparency and public interest.

Capitalist and socialist analysis

  • Capitalist (liberal) view: Locke's labour theory (one who mixes his labour with a thing acquires property in it); private property encourages enterprise and protects liberty.
  • Socialist view: Proudhon's "property is theft"; Marx: private ownership of the means of production leads to exploitation; property in means of production should be social.
  • India: a mixed approach. The Directive Principles (Art. 39(b), (c)) call for distribution of material resources to subserve the common good. Property Owners Association v State of Maharashtra (2024) held that not all private resources are "material resources of the community".

Possession and ownership

  • Ownership: the most comprehensive right a person can have over a thing: right to possess, use, enjoy, alienate, destroy (Austin: a right indefinite in point of user, unrestricted in point of disposition and unlimited in point of duration).
  • Possession: physical control (corpus) plus intention to exclude others (animus possidendi). Possession is prima facie evidence of ownership; a possessor has a good title against everyone except the true owner.
  • Finder of lost goods: has a title good against everyone except the true owner.
CaseArmory v Delamirie (1722)

A chimney sweep's boy found a jewel and gave it to a goldsmith's apprentice for valuation; the goldsmith kept the stones. Held: the finder had a title good against all but the true owner and could recover the value.

  • Indian law: a finder is subject to the responsibility of a bailee (s.71, Contract Act); adverse possession for 12 years extinguishes the owner's title (s.27 and Art. 65, Limitation Act, 1963).
CaseRavinder Kaur Grewal v Manjit Kaur (2019)

A three-judge bench held that a person who has perfected title by adverse possession can sue to protect his possession or for declaration of title, not merely use adverse possession as a shield.

Social functions of property

  • Provides security and independence; incentive for work and investment; basis of credit; means of social status.
  • Duties attached: property must be used without harming others (sic utere tuo ut alienum non laedas), subject to taxation, eminent domain, zoning and environmental law.
  • Duguit's theory: property is a social function; the owner must use it for social needs.

Points to remember

  • Property is a bundle of rights; new property includes government largesse protected by Art. 14.
  • Immovable property is defined in s.3 TPA and s.3(26) General Clauses Act.
  • Natural resources are held in public trust.
  • Capitalist (Locke) and socialist (Marx) views; India follows a mixed approach.
  • Possession is good against all but the true owner (Armory v Delamirie); adverse possession can found title (Ravinder Kaur Grewal).

Unit 2

General principles of transfer of property and sale

The TPA deals only with transfers between living persons. Its general rules decide what can be transferred, by whom, with what conditions, and how third parties are protected.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • General principles: transfer of property, what may be transferred, competence, conditions restraining alienation, rule against perpetuity, vested and contingent interests, conditional transfers, election, ostensible owner, feeding the grant by estoppel, lis pendens, fraudulent transfer, part performance
  • Specific transfer: sale

Meaning of transfer: s.5

Statutes.5, TPA

"Transfer of property" means an act by which a living person conveys property, in present or in future, to one or more other living persons, or to himself, or to himself and one or more other living persons; and "to transfer property" is to perform such act. "Living person" includes a company or association or body of individuals, whether incorporated or not.

  • The Act does not apply to transfers by will or by operation of law (inheritance, insolvency, court sale).
  • Transfer of property is an act inter vivos.

What may be transferred: s.6

  • Competence (s.7): a person competent to contract and entitled to transferable property, or authorised to dispose of property not his own, may transfer it.
  • Operation (s.8): unless a different intention appears, a transfer passes all the interest the transferor can pass, including legal incidents.
  • Oral transfer (s.9): permitted wherever writing is not expressly required by law.

Conditions restraining alienation and repugnant interests

  • s.10: where property is transferred subject to a condition absolutely restraining the transferee from parting with or disposing of his interest, the condition is void (except in a lease for the benefit of the lessor, and in property transferred to a woman other than a Hindu, Muslim or Buddhist, during marriage). Partial restraints may be valid.
  • s.11: where an absolute interest is created, a direction restricting its enjoyment is void.
  • s.12: condition making the interest determinable on insolvency or attempted alienation is void (except in a lease for the lessor's benefit).

Rule against perpetuity: ss.13 and 14

Statutes.14, TPA

No transfer of property can operate to create an interest which is to take effect after the lifetime of one or more persons living at the date of such transfer, and the minority of some person who shall be in existence at the expiration of that period, and to whom, if he attains full age, the interest created is to belong.

  • s.13: a transfer for the benefit of an unborn person must be preceded by a prior interest, and the interest to the unborn must be the whole remaining interest of the transferor.
  • Maximum period: life or lives in being plus the minority (18 years) of the ultimate beneficiary (plus gestation if relevant).
  • Exceptions: transfers for the benefit of the public for religion, knowledge, commerce, health, safety or other benefit to mankind (s.18); covenants of redemption in mortgages; personal agreements.
  • Accumulation (s.17): directions to accumulate income beyond the life of the transferor or 18 years are void beyond that period, with exceptions.

Vested and contingent interests

  • Vested (s.19): takes effect on the transfer or on a fixed event certain to happen; not defeated by the death of the transferee before possession; transferable and heritable.
  • Contingent (s.21): depends on an uncertain event; becomes vested when the event happens; if the transferee dies before, the interest fails.
  • Conditional transfers (ss.25 to 34): conditions precedent must be lawful and possible; conditions subsequent that are void do not affect the transfer.

Doctrine of election: s.35

Where a person professes to transfer property that he has no right to transfer, and as part of the same transaction confers a benefit on the owner of that property, the owner must elect either to confirm the transfer or to dissent from it; if he dissents, he must relinquish the benefit.

Transfer by ostensible owner: s.41

Statutes.41, TPA

Where, with the consent, express or implied, of the persons interested in immovable property, a person is the ostensible owner of such property and transfers the same for consideration, the transfer shall not be voidable on the ground that the transferor was not authorised to make it, provided that the transferee, after taking reasonable care to ascertain that the transferor had power to make the transfer, has acted in good faith.

Feeding the grant by estoppel: s.43

Where a person fraudulently or erroneously represents that he is authorised to transfer immovable property and professes to transfer it for consideration, the transfer, at the option of the transferee, operates on any interest the transferor may later acquire in that property, during the subsistence of the contract (without prejudice to purchasers for value without notice).

Lis pendens: s.52

Statutes.52, TPA

During the pendency in any court of a suit or proceeding which is not collusive and in which any right to immovable property is directly and specifically in question, the property cannot be transferred or otherwise dealt with by any party so as to affect the rights of any other party under any decree or order which may be made, except under the authority of the court.

  • The transfer is not void, but the transferee is bound by the result of the litigation.
CaseBellamy v Sabine (1857)

The doctrine of lis pendens is based on the necessity that neither party to litigation may alienate the property in dispute so as to affect his opponent; otherwise no suit could be brought to a successful end.

Fraudulent transfer: s.53

A transfer made with intent to defeat or delay the creditors of the transferor is voidable at the option of any creditor so defeated or delayed (subject to rights of a transferee in good faith for consideration); a gratuitous transfer with intent to defraud a subsequent transferee is voidable by him.

Part performance: s.53A

Statutes.53A, TPA

Where any person contracts to transfer for consideration any immovable property by writing signed by him or on his behalf, from which the terms can be ascertained with reasonable certainty, and the transferee has taken possession in part performance of the contract, or being already in possession continues in possession in part performance and has done some act in furtherance of the contract, and has performed or is willing to perform his part, then, notwithstanding that the contract, though required to be registered, has not been registered, the transferor shall be debarred from enforcing against the transferee any right in respect of the property, other than a right expressly provided by the contract.

  • Indian part performance is a shield, not a sword: it protects the transferee's possession but does not confer title.
  • Since the Registration and Other Related Laws (Amendment) Act, 2001, a contract for s.53A purposes must be registered; unregistered contracts after 2001 cannot claim protection.

Sale: ss.54 to 57

Statutes.54, TPA

"Sale" is a transfer of ownership in exchange for a price paid or promised or part-paid and part-promised. Such transfer, in the case of tangible immovable property of the value of one hundred rupees and upwards, or of a reversion or other intangible thing, can be made only by a registered instrument. For tangible immovable property of less than one hundred rupees, transfer may be by registered instrument or by delivery of the property. A contract for the sale of immovable property is a contract that a sale shall take place on terms settled between the parties; it does not, of itself, create any interest in or charge on such property.

CaseSuraj Lamp and Industries v State of Haryana (2011)

Transfers of immovable property through sale agreements, general powers of attorney and wills (SA/GPA/Will) do not convey title. Immovable property can be legally transferred only by a registered deed of conveyance.

Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseRamesh Chand v Suresh Chand (2025)

An agreement to sell, a GPA, an affidavit and a receipt do not transfer title; only a registered sale deed does (s.54). Section 53A can be used only by a transferee in possession, as a shield and not a sword.

CaseMehnoor Fatima v Vishveshka Infrastructure (2025)

Registration does not create title where the seller had none; land that had vested in the State could not be sold.

Points to remember

  • Only a registered sale deed transfers title; s.53A needs possession (Ramesh Chand, 2025).
  • Transfer is an act inter vivos by a living person (s.5); s.6 lists non-transferable interests.
  • Absolute restraints on alienation are void (s.10); rule against perpetuity limits remote vesting (s.14).
  • Ostensible owner (s.41), feeding the grant (s.43), lis pendens (s.52), fraudulent transfer (s.53), part performance (s.53A).
  • Sale of immovable property worth ₹100 or more requires a registered deed (s.54); SA/GPA/Will transactions do not transfer title (Suraj Lamp).
  • Rights and liabilities of seller and buyer under s.55.

Unit 3

Mortgage

A mortgage is a transfer of an interest in immovable property as security for a loan. The mortgagor's right to redeem is jealously protected: "once a mortgage, always a mortgage".

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Mortgage
  • Kinds: simple, conditional sale (distinguished from sale with condition to repurchase), usufructuary, English, deposit of title deeds, anomalous
  • Registration and formalities
  • Rights of mortgagor: redemption, due and payable, clog on redemption, partial redemption, accession, improvements, power to lease
  • Rights and liabilities of mortgagee: foreclosure or sale, suit for money, accession, mortgagee in possession, substituted security
  • Postponement of prior mortgage
  • Marshalling and contribution
  • Who may sue for redemption
  • Subrogation
  • "Redeem up and foreclose down"

Definition: s.58(a)

Statutes.58(a), TPA

A mortgage is the transfer of an interest in specific immovable property for the purpose of securing the payment of money advanced or to be advanced by way of loan, an existing or future debt, or the performance of an engagement which may give rise to a pecuniary liability. The transferor is the mortgagor, the transferee the mortgagee; the principal money and interest are the mortgage-money; and the instrument is the mortgage-deed.

Mortgage by conditional sale v sale with condition to repurchase

CaseChunchun Jha v Ebadat Ali (1955)

Whether a transaction is a mortgage by conditional sale or an out-and-out sale with a condition of repurchase depends on the intention of the parties as gathered from the document itself. If the condition for reconveyance is in a separate document, it cannot be a mortgage (proviso to s.58(c)). Indicators of a mortgage: a debtor-creditor relationship, price far below value, and continued possession by the transferor.

CaseTamboli Ramanlal Motilal v Ghanchi Chimanlal Keshavlal (1993)

The real intention, not the form, decides; where the consideration was close to market value and there was no debtor-creditor relationship, the transaction was a sale with an option to repurchase.

Registration and formalities: s.59

Where the principal money secured is one hundred rupees or upwards, a mortgage other than a mortgage by deposit of title deeds can be effected only by a registered instrument signed by the mortgagor and attested by at least two witnesses. Below one hundred rupees, it may be by registered instrument or (except a simple mortgage) by delivery.

CaseK.J. Nathan v S.V. Maruthi Rao (1965)

A mortgage by deposit of title deeds does not require writing or registration; if the parties reduce the bargain into a document that is itself the contract, it must be registered; a mere memorandum recording a past deposit need not be.

  • The debt may be proved even if the mortgage is invalid for want of registration, so the creditor can sue on the personal covenant.

Rights of the mortgagor

Right to redeem: s.60

Statutes.60, TPA

At any time after the principal money has become due, the mortgagor has a right, on payment or tender of the mortgage-money, to require the mortgagee to deliver the mortgage-deed and documents, to deliver possession (if in possession), and to retransfer the property or execute an acknowledgement that the right is extinguished. The right is extinguished by act of the parties or by decree of a court.

  • Due and payable: the right arises when the money becomes "due", even if not yet "payable"; the mortgagee cannot refuse merely because the date has not arrived if the contract allows earlier payment.
  • Clog on redemption: any provision that prevents or unreasonably restricts redemption is void. "Once a mortgage, always a mortgage, and nothing but a mortgage."
CaseSeth Ganga Dhar v Shankar Lal (1958)

A stipulation that the mortgagor would not redeem for 85 years was not, by itself, a clog; a long term is not void unless it is unconscionable or the mortgagee obtains an unfair advantage. On the facts it was valid.

CasePomal Kanji Govindji v Vrajlal Karsandas Purohit (1989)

A usufructuary mortgage for 99 years, at an inadequate rate, with an option to redeem only at the end, was a clog on redemption and void as unconscionable; the right of redemption cannot be fettered.

  • Partial redemption: not allowed, except where the mortgagee has acquired part of the mortgagor's interest (s.60, last para).
  • Accession (s.63): accessions to the property during the mortgage go with the property on redemption, subject to payment of their cost where acquired at the mortgagee's expense and separable.
  • Improvements (s.63A): improvements by the mortgagee in possession go to the mortgagor on redemption; necessary improvements are paid for.
  • Power to lease (s.65A): a mortgagor in lawful possession may grant leases binding on the mortgagee, subject to conditions (ordinary course of management, no premium, best rent, not exceeding three years for non-agricultural property).
  • Other rights: inspection and copies of documents (s.60A); transfer to a third party instead of retransfer (s.60B); redemption of separate mortgages (s.61).

Liabilities of a mortgagee in possession: s.76

Manage the property as a person of ordinary prudence; collect rents; pay government revenue; make necessary repairs; not commit destructive acts; keep accounts; apply receipts towards interest and then principal; protect from loss through his gross negligence.

Priorities, postponement, marshalling and contribution

  • Priority follows time (s.48): qui prior est tempore, potior est jure.
  • Postponement of prior mortgagee (s.78): where through the fraud, misrepresentation or gross neglect of a prior mortgagee another person is induced to advance money on the security, the prior mortgagee is postponed to the subsequent one.
  • Marshalling (s.81): if the owner mortgages two properties to one person and then one to another, the second mortgagee may require the first to satisfy his debt from the property not mortgaged to the second, so far as possible.
  • Contribution (s.82): where property subject to one mortgage belongs to different persons, each contributes rateably according to the value of his share.

Who may sue for redemption: s.91

Any person (other than the mortgagor) who has an interest in or charge on the property, or in the right to redeem: a subsequent mortgagee, a surety, a creditor who has obtained a decree for sale, a purchaser of the equity of redemption, a co-mortgagor.

Subrogation: s.92

  • Legal subrogation: any person (other than the mortgagor) who redeems a mortgage and who is entitled to redeem (s.91) acquires the rights of the mortgagee he redeems.
  • Conventional subrogation: a person who advances money to the mortgagor to redeem, under a written agreement that he shall be subrogated, acquires the redeemed mortgagee's rights.

"Redeem up and foreclose down"

An English doctrine (now reflected in Order XXXIV CPC): where there are several mortgages, a puisne mortgagee who wishes to redeem a prior mortgage must also foreclose the subsequent mortgagees (and the mortgagor), so that all parties interested are before the court and their rights settled in one suit. In India, all persons interested must be made parties in a suit for foreclosure, sale or redemption (O. XXXIV, R. 1).

Current law

Secured creditors (banks and financial institutions) enforce security interests without court intervention under the SARFAESI Act, 2002 (s.13), and corporate debts are resolved under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016.

Points to remember

  • Mortgage: transfer of an interest in specific immovable property as security (s.58(a)).
  • Six kinds: simple, conditional sale, usufructuary, English, deposit of title deeds, anomalous.
  • Conditional sale vs sale with repurchase: intention; condition in the same deed (Chunchun Jha).
  • Registration needed for ₹100 or more, except deposit of title deeds (s.59; K.J. Nathan).
  • Right to redeem (s.60); clogs void (Pomal Kanji).
  • Mortgagee's remedies: foreclosure, sale, money suit, power of sale (ss.67 to 69).
  • Postponement (s.78), marshalling (s.81), contribution (s.82), subrogation (s.92).

Unit 4

Actionable claims, security interests, charges, leases, exchange and gifts

Beyond sale and mortgage, the TPA governs the transfer of debts, charges as security without transfer, leases of the right to enjoy, exchanges and gifts.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Actionable claims
  • Security interests in immovable property
  • Charges
  • Leases
  • Exchange
  • Gifts

Actionable claims: ss.3, 130 to 137

  • "Actionable claim" (s.3): a claim to any debt (other than one secured by mortgage of immovable property or by hypothecation or pledge of movables), or to any beneficial interest in movable property not in possession, which the civil courts recognise as affording grounds for relief.
  • Transfer is by an instrument in writing signed by the transferor; it is complete on execution, and the transferee gets all rights and remedies of the transferor (s.130). Notice to the debtor protects the transferee against payment to the transferor.
  • The transferee takes subject to all liabilities and equities to which the transferor was subject (s.132).
  • Negotiable instruments, shares, debentures and policies are governed by their own statutes (s.137).
  • Security interests: the SARFAESI Act, 2002 defines "security interest" to include mortgages, charges, hypothecation and assignments, and allows secured creditors to enforce without court intervention.

Charges: s.100

Statutes.100, TPA

CaseAssociated Hotels of India Ltd v R.N. Kapoor (1959)

The test of whether a document creates a lease or licence is the intention of the parties, as gathered from the substance of the document, not its form; exclusive possession is an important but not conclusive indicator. The arrangement for running a shop in a hotel was held a lease.

  • Duration in absence of contract (s.106): lease for agricultural or manufacturing purposes is from year to year, terminable by six months' notice; lease for other purposes is month to month, terminable by fifteen days' notice (as amended in 2003).
  • Registration (s.107): a lease from year to year, or for a term exceeding one year, or reserving a yearly rent, can be made only by a registered instrument.
  • Rights and liabilities (s.108): lessor must disclose defects, put lessee in possession, covenant for quiet enjoyment; lessee must pay rent, keep property in good condition, not use it for other purposes, not build permanent structures without consent; lessee may avoid the lease if property is destroyed by fire, flood or violence (s.108(B)(e)).
  • Determination (s.111): efflux of time, happening of a specified event, merger, express or implied surrender, forfeiture (breach of express condition, denial of title, insolvency), notice to quit.
  • Relief against forfeiture for non-payment of rent (s.114) on payment of arrears with interest and costs.
  • Holding over (s.116): if the lessee remains in possession after determination and the lessor accepts rent or assents, the lease is renewed from year to year or month to month.
  • State rent control laws (and the Model Tenancy Act, 2021) override many of these provisions for urban premises.

Exchange: s.118

When two persons mutually transfer the ownership of one thing for the ownership of another, neither thing nor both things being money only, the transaction is called an exchange. Each party has the rights and liabilities of a seller as to what he gives and of a buyer as to what he takes (s.120). Formalities are the same as for sale.

Gifts: ss.122 to 129

Statutes.122, TPA

"Gift" is the transfer of certain existing movable or immovable property made voluntarily and without consideration by one person, called the donor, to another, called the donee, and accepted by or on behalf of the donee. Such acceptance must be made during the lifetime of the donor and while he is still capable of giving. If the donee dies before acceptance, the gift is void.

  • Formalities (s.123): a gift of immovable property must be effected by a registered instrument signed by or on behalf of the donor and attested by at least two witnesses; movables by such an instrument or by delivery.
  • Future property: a gift of future property is void (s.124).
  • Suspension or revocation (s.126): by agreement between donor and donee on the happening of an event not dependent on the donor's will, or in cases where it would be rescinded if it were a contract (fraud, coercion), but not merely at the donor's will.
  • Onerous gifts (s.127): a donee who accepts a gift must accept the burdens attached; he cannot take the benefit and reject the burden of a single gift.
  • Universal donee (s.128): a donee of the whole of the donor's property is personally liable for the donor's debts at the time of the gift to the extent of the property.
  • Exceptions (s.129): donationes mortis causa and gifts under Muslim law (hiba, which needs declaration, acceptance and delivery of possession, but not registration) are not governed by this chapter.
CaseRenikuntla Rajamma v K. Sarwanamma (2014)

A gift deed reserving to the donor the right to enjoy the property during her lifetime is valid; delivery of possession is not essential under s.123 where the registered deed transfers ownership.

Exam tip

Points to remember

  • Gift to child can be cancelled for failure to care for senior citizen (Urmila Dixit, 2025).

  • Charge creates security without transfer of interest (s.100).
  • Lease is a transfer of a right to enjoy (s.105); lease vs licence depends on intention (Associated Hotels).
  • Default duration and notice under s.106; registration under s.107; determination under s.111.
  • Exchange is mutual transfer of ownership (s.118).
  • Gift: existing property, voluntary, without consideration, accepted in donor's lifetime; registration for immovables (ss.122, 123); onerous gifts (s.127).

Easements and licences

An easement is a right over another's land for the beneficial enjoyment of one's own; a licence is a mere personal permission that creates no interest.

What the syllabus lists for this unit

  • Creation
  • Nature and characteristics
  • Extinction

  • Licences (Indian Easements Act, 1882)

Definition: s.4

Statutes.4, Indian Easements Act, 1882

An easement is a right which the owner or occupier of certain land possesses, as such, for the beneficial enjoyment of that land, to do and continue to do something, or to prevent and continue to prevent something being done, in or upon, or in respect of, certain other land not his own.

  • Dominant heritage: land for whose benefit the right exists; servient heritage: land on which the burden is imposed.

Nature and characteristics

  1. There must be a dominant and a servient heritage.
  2. They must belong to different owners.
  3. The right must be for the beneficial enjoyment of the dominant heritage.
  4. It must be to do something or prevent something on the servient land.
  5. The right must be certain and capable of being the subject of a grant.

An easement of necessity arises only where the dominant heritage cannot be used at all without it; mere convenience is not enough. Once the necessity ends (for example, an alternative access is acquired), the easement of necessity ends (s.41).

Extinction of easements (ss.37 to 51)

  • Dissolution of the right of the servient owner (s.37).
  • Release by the dominant owner, express or implied (s.38).
  • Revocation under a reserved power (s.39).
  • Expiry of the limited period or happening of the condition (s.40).
  • Termination of necessity (s.41).
  • Uselessness (s.42).
  • Permanent change in the dominant heritage making the burden heavier (s.43).
  • Permanent alteration of the servient heritage by superior force (s.44).
  • Destruction of either heritage (s.45).
  • Unity of ownership of both heritages (s.46).
  • Non-enjoyment for 20 years (s.47).

Riparian rights

Riparian owners (owners of land on the banks of a natural stream) have natural rights (s.7, illustrations (h) to (j)): every owner of land on a natural stream has the right to the continued flow of water without material alteration in quantity, quality or direction, and to make reasonable use of the water for domestic and agricultural purposes. These are not easements but natural rights incidental to ownership; they may be enlarged or restricted by easement. Statutes (irrigation Acts) and the public trust doctrine regulate them.

Licences: ss.52 to 64

Statutes.52, Indian Easements Act

Where one person grants to another, or to a definite number of other persons, a right to do or continue to do, in or upon the immovable property of the grantor, something which would, in the absence of such right, be unlawful, and such right does not amount to an easement or an interest in the property, the right is called a licence.

  • A licence is personal, not transferable (s.56), and generally revocable (s.60).
  • Irrevocable licences (s.60): (a) where the licence is coupled with a transfer of property and such transfer is in force; (b) where the licensee, acting on the licence, has executed a work of a permanent character and incurred expenses in its execution.
  • Licensee's rights on revocation: reasonable time to leave and remove goods (s.63); compensation if revoked without cause where the licence was granted for consideration (s.64).

Unit 6

Recordation of property rights: registration and stamp duty

Registration gives public notice of property transactions; stamp duty taxes them. Defects in either can make a document inadmissible or ineffective.

What the syllabus lists for this unit

  • exemption of leases and mortgages in favour of Land Development Banks
  • Recordation of rights in agricultural land in the States
  • Investigation of title
  • Law relating to stamp duties
  • liability of instruments to duty
  • by whom payable
  • effect of not duly stamping

Registration Act, 1908

Compulsory registration: s.17

Statutes.17(1), Registration Act

The following documents shall be registered: (a) instruments of gift of immovable property; (b) other non-testamentary instruments which purport or operate to create, declare, assign, limit or extinguish, whether in present or in future, any right, title or interest, whether vested or contingent, of the value of one hundred rupees and upwards, to or in immovable property; (c) non-testamentary instruments acknowledging receipt of consideration for such purposes; (d) leases of immovable property from year to year, or for any term exceeding one year, or reserving a yearly rent; (e) non-testamentary instruments transferring or assigning any decree or order of a court affecting such rights.

  • s.17(1A) (2001): documents containing contracts to transfer immovable property for the purpose of s.53A TPA must be registered.
  • s.17(2) lists exemptions (for example, compositions, shares in companies, debentures, decrees and orders of court except compromise decrees involving other property).
  • Optional registration (s.18): wills, instruments of less than ₹100 value, leases of one year or less.

Exemption for Land Development Banks

State Land Development Bank (now cooperative agriculture and rural development bank) Acts, and s.17(2)(xii)-type provisions, exempt mortgages and leases executed in favour of such banks, or certificates of their loans, from the requirement of registration, or provide for registration by filing a copy, to facilitate agricultural credit.

Time and place

  • Documents must be presented within four months of execution (s.23), with up to four more months on payment of a fine (s.25).
  • Presentation at the office of the sub-registrar within whose sub-district the property is situated (s.28).

Effect of non-registration: s.49

No document required by s.17 or by the TPA to be registered shall affect any immovable property comprised in it, confer any power to adopt, or be received as evidence of any transaction affecting such property, unless registered. Proviso: an unregistered document may be received as evidence of a contract in a suit for specific performance, as evidence of part performance under s.53A (pre-2001 position), or as evidence of any collateral transaction not required to be registered.

  • A registered document operates from the date of its execution (s.47).
  • Registered documents prevail over unregistered ones (s.48).

Recordation of rights in agricultural land

  • Land revenue laws provide for record of rights (pattas, adangals, pahanis, khatauni) maintained by revenue officials. In Andhra Pradesh, the AP Rights in Land and Pattadar Pass Books Act, 1971 provides for record of rights and pass books; Telangana enacted the Bhu Bharathi (Record of Rights in Land) Act, 2025, replacing its 2020 Act.
  • Revenue records are not documents of title; entries raise a presumption for fiscal purposes, but title is decided by civil courts.
CaseSuraj Bhan v Financial Commissioner (2007)

Entries in revenue records do not confer or extinguish title; mutation is only for fiscal purposes (collection of land revenue). Title is decided by competent civil courts.

  • The Digital India Land Records Modernization Programme (DILRMP) aims at conclusive titling; the Unique Land Parcel Identification Number (ULPIN) is being rolled out.

Investigation of title

A purchaser or lender should examine: the chain of title deeds for at least 30 years; encumbrance certificates from the sub-registrar; revenue records and mutation; approved plans and conversion orders; tax receipts; litigation and lis pendens; succession and partition documents; powers of attorney; and statutory permissions (ceiling, land reform, scheduled area restrictions). Under s.55(1) TPA, the seller must produce documents of title and answer relevant questions.

Indian Stamp Act, 1899

  • Stamp duty on instruments is a State subject for rates (List II, Entry 63) except instruments listed in the Union List (bills of exchange, cheques, promissory notes, bills of lading, letters of credit, insurance policies, transfers of shares, debentures, proxies and receipts), which are under Entry 91 of List I. Stamp duties other than judicial stamps are in the Concurrent List (Entry 44) as to legislative power.

Liability of instruments to duty: s.3

Every instrument mentioned in Schedule I (or the State schedule) executed in India is chargeable with the duty indicated. Duty is on the instrument, not the transaction. Several instruments in one document are separately chargeable (s.5); an instrument covering several distinct matters is charged for each.

Effect of not duly stamping; examination and impounding

  • Impounding (s.33): every person having authority to receive evidence, and every person in charge of a public office (except police), before whom an unstamped or insufficiently stamped instrument is produced, shall impound it.
  • Inadmissibility (s.35): no instrument chargeable with duty shall be admitted in evidence for any purpose, or acted upon, registered or authenticated, unless duly stamped. Proviso: it can be admitted on payment of the deficit duty and a penalty (ten times the deficit, or as State law provides).
  • Once an instrument is admitted in evidence, its admission cannot be questioned later on the ground of insufficient stamping (s.36).

Answer any FIVE of the following.

1.Property that cannot be transferred
Model answer

Section 6 of the Transfer of Property Act says property of any kind may be transferred, except:

  • a spes successionis (the chance of an heir apparent succeeding) (s.6(a));
  • a mere right of re-entry for breach of condition;
  • an easement apart from the dominant heritage;
  • an interest restricted to personal enjoyment;
  • a right to future maintenance;
  • a mere right to sue;
  • a public office or salary;
  • pensions of military and civil pensioners; and
  • transfers opposed to the nature of the interest, for an unlawful object, or to a legally disqualified transferee.
2.Rule against perpetuity
Model answer

Under s.14 TPA, no transfer can create an interest that is to take effect after the lifetime of one or more persons living at the date of the transfer and the minority of some person who is in existence at the expiry of that period. The interest must vest within that period.

  • The purpose is to prevent property from being tied up indefinitely.
  • Exceptions:
  • transfers for public benefit, such as religion, knowledge, commerce or health (s.18);
  • covenants for redemption in mortgages; and
  • personal agreements.
3.Doctrine of lis pendens
Model answer

Under s.52 TPA, during the pendency of a suit in which a right to immovable property is directly and specifically in question, the property cannot be transferred by any party so as to affect the rights of any other party under the decree, except with the court's authority.

  • It is based on public policy, necessity and finality (Bellamy v Sabine, 1857).
  • The transfer is not void, but it is subject to the result of the litigation (Thomson Press v Nanak Builders, 2013).
  • Pendency runs from the presentation of the plaint until final disposal, including execution.
4.Part performance
Model answer

Under s.53A TPA, where a transferee under a written, signed contract for transfer of immovable property has taken possession in part performance, and is willing to perform his part, the transferor cannot enforce any right against the transferee in respect of the property, other than a right expressly provided by the contract.

  • It is a shield, not a sword.
  • It requires possession (Ramesh Chand v Suresh Chand, 2025).
  • Since 2001, the contract must be registered for protection under s.53A (s.17(1A) of the Registration Act).
5.Fraudulent transfer
Model answer

Under s.53(1) TPA, every transfer of immovable property made with intent to defeat or delay creditors is voidable at the option of any creditor so defeated or delayed. Bona fide transferees for consideration are protected.

Under s.53(2), a gratuitous transfer with intent to defraud a subsequent transferee is voidable by that transferee.

A suit on behalf of all creditors is required (s.53(1)).

6.Doctrine of marshalling
Model answer

Under s.81 TPA, if the owner of two or more properties mortgages them to one person, and then mortgages one or more of them to another person who has no notice of the first mortgage, the second mortgagee may require the first mortgagee to satisfy his debt from the properties not mortgaged to the second, so far as they suffice.

It cannot prejudice the rights of the first mortgagee or of others claiming under him.

7.Actionable claim
Model answer

Section 3 TPA: a claim to any debt (other than one secured by a mortgage of immovable property, or by hypothecation or pledge of movables) or to any beneficial interest in movable property not in possession, which the civil courts recognise as affording grounds for relief.

Transfer (s.130) is by a signed instrument in writing. It takes effect on execution. Notice to the debtor is needed to bind him (s.131). The transferee takes subject to the debtor's equities (s.132).

8.Onerous gift
Model answer

Section 127 TPA: where a gift is of several things in one transfer and one of them is burdened with an obligation, the donee can take nothing under the gift unless he accepts it fully.

  • Where gifts are made in separate transfers, the donee may accept one and refuse the others.
  • A minor donee may repudiate an onerous gift on attaining majority.
9.Easement of necessity
Model answer

Section 13 of the Indian Easements Act, 1882: where a transfer or partition creates a situation in which an easement is absolutely necessary for enjoying the dominant heritage, such as a land-locked plot, an easement of necessity arises in favour of the dominant owner.

  • It lasts only as long as the necessity lasts (s.41).
  • It must be absolute necessity, not mere convenience.

10.Define sale. Explain the rights and liabilities of the seller and the buyer of immovable property.
Model answer

Definition (s.54 TPA)

Sale is a transfer of ownership in exchange for a price paid or promised, or part-paid and part-promised. For tangible immovable property of ₹100 or more, or a reversion or other intangible thing, sale can be made only by a registered instrument. A contract for sale does not itself create any interest in or charge on the property.

Title only by registered deed

  • Suraj Lamp & Industries v State of Haryana (2011): "GPA sales" do not convey title.
  • Ramesh Chand v Suresh Chand (2025): an agreement to sell, GPA, affidavit and receipt convey no title; only a registered sale deed does.
  • Registration does not cure the absence of title in the seller (Mehnoor Fatima v Vishveshka Infrastructure, 2025).

Seller's duties before completion (s.55(1))

  • disclose material defects in the property or title that the buyer could not discover with ordinary care;
  • produce title documents for inspection;
  • answer questions on title;
  • execute a proper conveyance;
  • take care of the property and documents; and
  • pay all outgoings until the transfer.

Seller's duties after completion

  • give possession;
  • deliver title deeds; and
  • an implied covenant for title (s.55(2)).

Seller's rights (s.55(4))

  • rents and profits until ownership passes; and
  • a charge on the property for unpaid purchase money (the seller's lien).

Buyer's duties

  • disclose facts materially increasing the value of the property (s.55(5)(a));
  • pay the price; and
  • after transfer, bear loss from destruction not caused by the seller, and pay outgoings.

Buyer's rights

  • the benefit of any improvement or increase in value, and rents and profits after transfer; and
  • a charge for the purchase money paid in advance, where the buyer properly declines to accept delivery (s.55(6)(b)).

Conclusion

Section 55 implies these rights and duties unless the parties agree otherwise. The courts insist on a registered deed for title, protecting certainty in transactions.

11.Explain the kinds of mortgages and the right of redemption of a mortgagor.
Model answer

Definition (s.58(a))

A mortgage is the transfer of an interest in specific immovable property to secure payment of money advanced, an existing or future debt, or an engagement giving rise to pecuniary liability.

Kinds

  1. Simple mortgage (s.58(b)): a personal covenant to pay; the property is not delivered; the remedy is sale through court.
  2. Mortgage by conditional sale (s.58(c)): an ostensible sale that becomes absolute on default. The condition must be in the same document (Chunchun Jha v Ebadat Ali, 1954).
  3. Usufructuary mortgage (s.58(d)): possession is delivered, and the mortgagee takes rents and profits in lieu of interest or principal. The remedy is not foreclosure or sale.
  4. English mortgage (s.58(e)): an absolute transfer with a covenant to re-transfer on repayment.
  5. Mortgage by deposit of title deeds (s.58(f)): an equitable mortgage, allowed in notified towns, without a registered instrument.
  6. Anomalous mortgage (s.58(g)): any other kind.

Registration is needed for mortgages of ₹100 or more, except deposit of title deeds (s.59).

Right of redemption (s.60)

After the principal money becomes due, the mortgagor may, on paying or tendering it, require the mortgagee to:

  • deliver the mortgage deed and documents;
  • deliver possession; and
  • re-transfer the property, or acknowledge discharge in writing.
  • Equity of redemption: the right survives until extinguished by the act of the parties or a decree of court.
  • "Once a mortgage, always a mortgage"; no clog on redemption: a stipulation that defeats or fetters redemption is void (Seth Ganga Dhar v Shankar Lal, 1958; Noakes v Rice, 1902).
  • Partial redemption is not allowed (s.60, last paragraph), except where the mortgagee acquires part.
  • Who may redeem: the mortgagor and any person interested (s.91).
  • Limitation: 30 years for a suit for redemption (Art. 61, Limitation Act).

Conclusion

The law protects the mortgagor's equity of redemption as a core right, while securing the mortgagee's remedies of sale, foreclosure or possession.

12.Explain the rights and liabilities of a lessor and a lessee, and the modes of determination of a lease.
Model answer

Lease (s.105 TPA)

A transfer of a right to enjoy immovable property for a certain time, express or implied, or in perpetuity, in consideration of a price paid or promised, or of money, a share of crops, service or any other thing of value, given periodically.

  • Leases from year to year, or for more than a year, need a registered instrument (s.107).
  • Default duration (s.106): leases for agricultural or manufacturing purposes are from year to year, terminable by six months' notice. Others are month to month, terminable by 15 days' notice (as amended in 2002).

Lessor's rights and liabilities (s.108)

  • disclose latent defects;
  • put the lessee in possession;
  • covenant for quiet enjoyment; and
  • receive rent, with the right of re-entry on forfeiture.

Lessee's rights and liabilities

  • the benefit of accretions;
  • the right to remove fixtures before the lease ends;
  • the right to sub-let, unless the contract says otherwise;
  • pay rent;
  • keep the property in good condition;
  • not use it for a different purpose, or erect permanent structures without consent (except for agriculture); and
  • deliver possession at the end.

Determination (s.111)

  • efflux of time;
  • the happening of a specified event;
  • the lessor's interest terminating;
  • merger;
  • express or implied surrender;
  • forfeiture, for breach of an express condition with re-entry, denial of title, or the lessee's insolvency where so provided, with relief against forfeiture (s.114); and
  • notice to quit (s.106).

Holding over

A lessee who remains in possession with the lessor's assent (s.116) creates a tenancy from year to year or month to month. A tenant at sufferance is one who stays without assent.

Rent control laws

Rent control statutes override the TPA where they apply. Examples are the AP Buildings (Lease, Rent and Eviction) Control Act, 1960 and the Model Tenancy Act, 2021.

Conclusion

The TPA provides the default rules, which the contract and rent control statutes may modify.

13.Which documents require compulsory registration? What is the effect of non-registration?
Model answer

Compulsory registration (s.17, Registration Act, 1908)

  • instruments of gift of immovable property;
  • non-testamentary instruments creating, declaring, assigning, limiting or extinguishing any right, title or interest of ₹100 or more in immovable property;
  • acknowledgments of payment of consideration for such transactions;
  • leases from year to year, for more than a year, or reserving yearly rent;
  • decrees or orders assigning such interests (except compromise decrees about property in suit);
  • since 2001, contracts for sale for the purpose of s.53A (s.17(1A)); and
  • State amendments, for example requiring registration of agreements of sale with possession, and of powers of attorney to sell.

Optional registration (s.18)

Wills, and instruments of less than ₹100.

Effect of non-registration (s.49)

An unregistered document required to be registered:

  • does not affect any immovable property comprised in it;
  • confers no power to adopt; and
  • is not received as evidence of any transaction affecting such property.

Proviso: it may be received:

  • as evidence of a contract in a suit for specific performance;
  • for a collateral purpose; or
  • as part performance under s.53A, where registration is not required.

Unregistered agreements to sell are admissible in specific performance suits (S. Kaladevi v V.R. Somasundaram, 2010; R. Hemalatha v Kashthuri, 2023).

Refusal to register

Section 22-A, as inserted in some States, bars registration of documents relating to prohibited property, such as government, endowment and inam lands (K. Ramalingam, 2026).

Stamp duty

Inadequately stamped documents can be impounded and validated on payment (In re Interplay between Arbitration and Stamp Acts, 2023).

Conclusion

Registration ensures publicity and certainty of title. Non-registration renders the document ineffective to transfer an interest, subject to limited evidentiary uses.

14.X holds an agreement to sell, a GPA, an affidavit and a receipt from Y for Y's house, and has paid the full price. He is not in possession. After Y's death, Y's sons claim the house. X sues for possession, claiming ownership. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Do these documents convey title, and can X invoke s.53A?

Law:

  • Suraj Lamp (2011) and Ramesh Chand v Suresh Chand (2025): an agreement to sell, GPA, affidavit and receipt do not convey title; only a registered sale deed under s.54 does.
  • A GPA ends on the principal's death, unless coupled with an interest.
  • Section 53A requires possession, and is a shield, not a sword.

Application: X has no registered sale deed and is not in possession, so he cannot claim ownership or use s.53A.

Conclusion: X's suit for possession fails. At most, he may seek specific performance (subject to limitation) or a refund of the price.

15.During the pendency of a partition suit between A and B, A sells his entire house to C, who buys in good faith without knowing of the suit. The court then decrees partition, giving half to B. C resists. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Is C bound by the decree?

Law: Under s.52 TPA (lis pendens), a transfer during the pendency of a suit concerning immovable property cannot affect the rights of other parties under the decree. Good faith and lack of notice are irrelevant. The transfer is not void but subject to the decree (Thomson Press v Nanak Builders, 2013; Rajendra Singh v Santa Singh, 1973).

Application: A's sale to C occurred while the partition suit was pending.

Conclusion: C is bound by the decree and must give up B's half. C may recover from A, for example under the covenant for title.

16.An 80-year-old mother gifts her house to her son by a registered deed reciting that he will look after her. The son later neglects her and asks her to leave. She seeks to cancel the gift. Advise her.
Model answer

Issue: Can the gift be cancelled for the donee's failure to provide care?

Law:

  • Section 126 TPA: a gift may be revoked on a condition agreed between the parties, or where the gift could be rescinded like a contract.
  • Section 23 of the Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007: a transfer made subject to the condition that the transferee provides basic amenities and needs is deemed void at the transferor's option, on a declaration by the Tribunal, if the transferee fails to do so.
  • Urmila Dixit v Sunil Sharan Dixit (2025): the Act is beneficial and to be read purposively. A gift with a condition of care may be cancelled on neglect.

Application: The deed recites the expectation of care, and the son has failed to provide it.

Conclusion: The mother may apply to the Maintenance Tribunal under s.23 for a declaration that the gift is void, and for restoration of possession.

17.A's house stands in the name of his brother B, with A's tacit consent. B sells it to C for value, after C makes reasonable inquiries. A sues to set aside the sale. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Is the transfer by an ostensible owner binding on the real owner?

Law: Section 41 TPA: where, with the express or implied consent of the persons interested in immovable property, a person is the ostensible owner, and he transfers it for consideration, the transfer is not voidable on the ground that he was not authorised. The transferee must have taken reasonable care to ascertain that the transferor had power to make the transfer and acted in good faith.

Application: A allowed B to appear as the owner. C paid value and made reasonable inquiries.

Conclusion: The sale binds A. A's remedy is against B for the price received.
