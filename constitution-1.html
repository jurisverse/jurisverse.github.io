The Preamble: federalism, secularism and social justice

The Preamble states who made the Constitution, what kind of State it creates and what it aims to secure. It is part of the Constitution and a key to its interpretation.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Preamble
  • Federalism
  • Secularism
  • Social justice
  • Interpretation of the Preamble

Text and structure

The Preamble, as amended by the Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976, reads in substance:

"We, the people of India, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic and to secure to all its citizens: Justice, social, economic and political; Liberty of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; Equality of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all Fraternity assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation; in our Constituent Assembly this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do hereby adopt, enact and give to ourselves this Constitution."

It answers four questions:

QuestionAnswer in the Preamble
------
Source of authority"We, the people of India"
Nature of the StateSovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic
ObjectivesJustice, liberty, equality, fraternity
Date of adoption26 November 1949 (Constitution in force 26 January 1950)

The words socialist, secular and integrity were added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976. It is based on the Objectives Resolution moved by Jawaharlal Nehru in the Constituent Assembly on 13 December 1946.

Key words

  • Sovereign: India is independent internally and externally; membership of the Commonwealth or the UN does not limit it.
  • Socialist: a democratic socialism aiming to end inequality of income, status and opportunity, without abolishing private property.
  • Secular: the State has no religion of its own and treats all religions equally (sarva dharma sambhava), rather than a strict wall of separation.
  • Democratic: government by elected representatives, with universal adult suffrage, and democracy in social and economic life too.
  • Republic: the head of State is elected (the President), not hereditary.
  • Justice: social, economic and political; carried into Directive Principles (Arts. 38, 39) and Fundamental Rights (Arts. 15, 16, 17).
  • Fraternity: a sense of common brotherhood, assuring dignity of the individual and unity of the nation.

Is the Preamble part of the Constitution?

CaseIn re Berubari Union and Exchange of Enclaves (1960)

An advisory opinion under Article 143 on transfer of territory to Pakistan. The Court said the Preamble is a key to open the minds of the makers, but is not part of the Constitution and is not a source of power.

CaseKesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973)

A thirteen-judge bench overruled Berubari on this point: the Preamble is part of the Constitution. It can be amended under Article 368, but not so as to destroy the basic structure, and the basic features can be gathered from the Preamble.

CaseLIC of India v Consumer Education and Research Centre (1995)

The Supreme Court reaffirmed that the Preamble is an integral part of the Constitution and that its socio-economic goals guide interpretation of other provisions.

CaseDr Balram Singh v Union of India (2024)

Petitions challenging the insertion of "socialist" and "secular" into the Preamble in 1976 were dismissed. The Court held that Parliament's amending power extends to the Preamble, the fact that the Constitution was adopted in 1949 does not bar later amendment of the Preamble, and the two words reflect values already embedded in the Constitution.

Federalism

India has a federal structure with strong unitary features. K.C. Wheare called it "quasi-federal"; others call it "cooperative federalism" or a federation with a centralising tendency.

Federal features

  • Written Constitution, supreme over both levels of government.
  • Division of powers in the Seventh Schedule: Union, State and Concurrent Lists.
  • Independent judiciary to decide Centre-State disputes (Art. 131).
  • Rigid amendment procedure for federal provisions (Art. 368(2) proviso, ratification by half the States).
  • Bicameral Parliament with a Council of States representing the States.

Unitary features

  • Single Constitution and single citizenship.
  • Parliament can alter State boundaries without their consent (Art. 3).
  • Residuary powers with the Union (Art. 248).
  • Emergency provisions (Arts. 352, 356, 360).
  • Governors appointed by the President; all-India services.
  • Parliament's power to legislate on State List matters in national interest (Art. 249).
CaseState of West Bengal v Union of India (1962)

The Court held that the Constitution is not truly federal; States are not sovereign, and Parliament can acquire property vested in a State.

CaseS.R. Bommai v Union of India (1994)

A nine-judge bench held that federalism and secularism are basic features. Proclamations under Article 356 are subject to judicial review, the floor of the House is the place to test majority, and a State government that acts against secularism may be dismissed.

Secularism

  • The original Constitution was secular in substance: Articles 14, 15, 16, 25 to 30, 44 and 325.
  • Indian secularism means equal respect and equal distance from all religions; the State may regulate secular activities associated with religion (Art. 25(2)) and undertake social reform.
CaseZiyauddin Burhanuddin Bukhari v Brijmohan Ramdass Mehra (1976)

The Court described the secular State as one with no religion of its own, giving equal freedom to all religions.

Social justice

Social justice means removing social and economic inequalities and giving the weaker sections a fair share. It drives reservation (Arts. 15(4), 16(4)), abolition of untouchability (Art. 17), protection against exploitation (Arts. 23, 24) and the Directive Principles.

CaseAir India Statutory Corporation v United Labour Union (1997)

The Court treated social justice as a fundamental right flowing from the Preamble and Articles 14, 21, 38 and 39, and used it to protect contract labour. (The specific direction on absorption was later overruled in SAIL v National Union Waterfront Workers, 2001, but the social justice reasoning remains frequently cited.)

Interpretation of the Preamble

  • The Preamble is not a source of power or of prohibition; it cannot override an express provision.
  • Where the words of an article are ambiguous, the Preamble helps choose the meaning that furthers its objectives.
  • It is the source of many basic features: sovereignty, democracy, republican form, secularism, federalism, justice, liberty, equality, dignity.
Exam tip

In any Preamble answer, mention Berubari (not part), Kesavananda (part, amendable subject to basic structure) and Balram Singh (2024 challenge to "socialist" and "secular" dismissed). Then link each key word to the articles that carry it out.

Points to remember

  • The Preamble declares the source (the people), nature of the State and objectives of the Constitution.
  • 42nd Amendment added socialist, secular and integrity; upheld in Balram Singh (2024).
  • Preamble is part of the Constitution (Kesavananda overruling Berubari) but is not a source of power.
  • India is federal with unitary features; federalism and secularism are basic features (S.R. Bommai).
  • Indian secularism means equal respect for all religions.

Unit 2

The Union and its territory

India is an indestructible Union of destructible States: Parliament can redraw the map of the States by ordinary law, but ceding Indian territory needs a constitutional amendment.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Name and territory of the Union (Art. 1)
  • Admission or establishment of new States (Art. 2)
  • Formation of new States and alteration of areas, boundaries or names (Art. 3)
  • Laws under Arts. 2 and 3 not amendments (Art. 4)
  • Names of the existing States

Article 1: name and territory

StatuteArt. 1

(1) India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States. (2) The States and the territories thereof shall be as specified in the First Schedule. (3) The territory of India shall comprise the territories of the States, the Union territories specified in the First Schedule, and such other territories as may be acquired.

  • "Union of States" rather than "Federation": Dr Ambedkar explained that the Indian federation is not the result of an agreement among States, and no State has the right to secede.
  • Territory of India (States, UTs, acquired territories) is wider than the "Union of States" (States only).
  • At present there are 28 States and 8 Union territories.

Article 2: admission or establishment of new States

StatuteArt. 2

Parliament may by law admit into the Union, or establish, new States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit.

Art. 2 deals with areas that were not part of India, for example Sikkim, which became a State in 1975 (36th Amendment).

Article 3: formation of new States and alteration of existing States

StatuteArt. 3

Parliament may by law (a) form a new State by separation of territory from any State or by uniting two or more States or parts of States, or by uniting any territory to a part of any State; (b) increase the area of any State; (c) diminish the area of any State; (d) alter the boundaries of any State; (e) alter the name of any State. Proviso: no Bill for the purpose shall be introduced except on the recommendation of the President, and where the proposal affects the area, boundaries or name of a State, the Bill must be referred by the President to the legislature of that State for expressing its views within the specified period.

Points:

  • Parliament acts by a simple majority.
  • The State legislature's views are not binding; Parliament need not accept them, and a fresh reference is not needed for every amendment to the Bill.
CaseBabulal Parate v State of Bombay (1960)

The Court held that Parliament is not bound by the views of the State legislature under the proviso to Article 3, and need not make a fresh reference when it amends the Bill after receiving views.

  • "State" in Art. 3 includes Union territories for clauses (a) to (e), by Explanation I.
CaseIn re Article 370 of the Constitution (2023)

A Constitution Bench upheld the 2019 abrogation of the special status of Jammu and Kashmir. On Article 3, it upheld the carving out of the Union territory of Ladakh. On the conversion of the State of Jammu and Kashmir into a Union territory, the Court did not decide the question, recording the Union's statement that statehood would be restored, and directed elections to the Legislative Assembly by 30 September 2024.

Article 4: such laws are not amendments

StatuteArt. 4

Laws made under Article 2 or 3 may contain amendments to the First and Fourth Schedules and supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions; no such law shall be deemed to be an amendment of the Constitution for the purposes of Article 368.

Cession of territory

Arts. 2 to 4 do not empower Parliament to give away Indian territory to a foreign State.

CaseIn re Berubari Union and Exchange of Enclaves (1960)

The Nehru-Noon agreement of 1958 proposed to transfer half of Berubari Union to Pakistan. The Court advised that cession of Indian territory requires an amendment under Article 368; Art. 3(c) ("diminish the area") covers internal reorganisation only. Parliament then passed the 9th Amendment, 1960.

CaseMaganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel v Union of India (1969)

Settling a disputed boundary (the Rann of Kutch award) is not cession of territory, so it does not need a constitutional amendment.

The 100th Amendment, 2015 gave effect to the India-Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement, exchanging enclaves.

Examples: Madras to Tamil Nadu (1969), Mysore to Karnataka (1973), Uttaranchal to Uttarakhand (2007), Orissa to Odisha (2011). Each was done by a law under Art. 3(e).

Points to remember

  • Art. 1: India, that is Bharat, is a Union of States; territory includes States, UTs and acquired territory.
  • Art. 2 admits or establishes new States from outside; Art. 3 reorganises existing States.
  • Under Art. 3 the President recommends and refers the Bill to the State; the State's views do not bind Parliament (Babulal Parate).
  • Art. 4 laws are not constitutional amendments.
  • Cession needs an Art. 368 amendment (Berubari); boundary settlement does not (Maganbhai).
  • In re Article 370 (2023) upheld abrogation and creation of Ladakh UT.

Citizenship

The Constitution settled who were citizens on 26 January 1950; everything after that is governed by Parliament through the Citizenship Act, 1955.

What the syllabus lists for this unit

  • Persons of Indian origin residing outside India
  • Voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship
  • Continuance of rights

Why citizenship matters

Some rights are given only to citizens: Arts. 15, 16, 19, 29 and 30; the right to vote and to hold offices such as President, Vice-President, judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, Attorney-General, Governor. Others are available to all persons: Arts. 14, 20, 21, 21A, 22, 25.

India has single citizenship: no separate State citizenship.

Unit 4

"State" under Article 12

Fundamental Rights mainly bind the State; Article 12 decides who counts as the State, and the courts have widened it to bodies that are instruments or agencies of government.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Meaning and scope of State
  • Local authorities
  • Other authorities
  • State in the light of Fundamental Rights

The definition

StatuteArt. 12

In this Part, unless the context otherwise requires, "the State" includes the Government and Parliament of India and the Government and the Legislature of each of the States and all local or other authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India.

The definition is inclusive. It covers four groups:

  1. Government and Parliament of India.
  2. Government and Legislature of each State.
  3. Local authorities.
  4. Other authorities within India or under the control of the Government of India.

Local authorities

"Local authority" is defined in s.3(31) of the General Clauses Act, 1897: a municipal committee, district board, body of port commissioners or other authority legally entitled to, or entrusted by the Government with, the control or management of a municipal or local fund. The syllabus examples: municipalities, district boards, panchayats, improvement trusts, mining settlement boards and port commissioners all fit this definition.

CaseMohammad Yasin v Town Area Committee, Jalalabad (1952)

A municipal bye-law imposing a licence fee that effectively created a monopoly in vegetable wholesale trade was struck down as violating Art. 19(1)(g); the town area committee was "State".

Other authorities: the expanding meaning

Early narrow view

Initially, courts read "other authorities" ejusdem generis with government and legislature, limiting it to bodies exercising governmental or sovereign functions (University of Madras v Shantha Bai, Madras HC, 1954).

Statutory power test

CaseRajasthan State Electricity Board v Mohan Lal (1967)

The Court rejected the ejusdem generis approach. "Other authorities" include all authorities created by the Constitution or a statute on which powers are conferred by law, including power to make rules or orders having the force of law. The Electricity Board was "State".

CaseSukhdev Singh v Bhagatram Sardar Singh Raghuvanshi (1975)

ONGC, LIC and the Industrial Finance Corporation, statutory corporations, were held to be "State". Their regulations had the force of law, and they were agencies of the government.

Instrumentality or agency test

CaseRamana Dayaram Shetty v International Airport Authority of India (1979)

Bhagwati J held that a corporation that is an instrumentality or agency of the government is "State". It must act fairly and cannot act arbitrarily in awarding contracts (here, a restaurant licence at Bombay airport).

CaseAjay Hasia v Khalid Mujib Sehravardi (1981)

A society registered under the Societies Registration Act running a regional engineering college was held to be "State". The Court listed tests: (1) entire share capital held by government; (2) financial assistance meeting almost all expenditure; (3) monopoly status conferred or protected by the State; (4) deep and pervasive State control; (5) functions of public importance closely related to governmental functions; (6) a government department transferred to a corporation. The manner of creation (statute or registration) is irrelevant.

CasePradeep Kumar Biswas v Indian Institute of Chemical Biology (2002)

A seven-judge bench held CSIR to be "State" and overruled Sabhajit Tewary (1975). The question is whether, in light of cumulative facts, the body is financially, functionally and administratively dominated by or under the control of the government. Control must be particular to the body and pervasive; mere regulatory control is not enough.

Limits of the test

CaseZee Telefilms Ltd v Union of India (2005)

The Board of Control for Cricket in India (BCCI) was held not to be "State" under Article 12 (3:2), as it was not financially, functionally or administratively dominated by the government. But because it performs public functions, it is amenable to writ jurisdiction under Article 226.

CaseBoard of Control for Cricket in India v Cricket Association of Bihar (2015)

Reaffirming that BCCI performs public functions, the Court held it subject to judicial review under Article 226 for its public duties.

Is the judiciary "State"?

  • When acting on the administrative side (appointments, rule-making), courts are "State".
  • Judicial decisions are not open to challenge as violations of Fundamental Rights by a writ under Art. 32.
CaseNaresh Shridhar Mirajkar v State of Maharashtra (1967)

A High Court order prohibiting publication of a witness's evidence could not be challenged under Art. 32 as violating Art. 19(1)(a); judicial orders of a superior court are not "State action" for that purpose.

State in the light of Fundamental Rights

  • Some rights are expressly enforceable against private persons too: Art. 15(2) (access to shops, wells, tanks), Art. 17 (untouchability), Art. 23 (traffic and forced labour), Art. 24 (child labour).
  • Others apply only against the State, but the State may also be required to protect individuals from private violation (positive duties under Art. 21).
CaseKaushal Kishor v State of Uttar Pradesh (2023)

A Constitution Bench held (4:1) that a fundamental right under Article 19 or 21 can be enforced even against persons other than the State or its instrumentalities. This is a significant step towards horizontal application of Fundamental Rights.

Exam tip

Shetty and Ajay Hasia (instrumentality, six tests) to Pradeep Kumar Biswas (deep and pervasive control) to Zee Telefilms (BCCI not State, but Art. 226 available) to Kaushal Kishor (horizontal rights).

Points to remember

  • Art. 12 is an inclusive definition: government, legislatures, local and other authorities.
  • "Other authorities" are not limited ejusdem generis (Rajasthan SEB).
  • Instrumentality or agency of government is "State" (R.D. Shetty; Ajay Hasia's six tests).
  • Pradeep Kumar Biswas: financial, functional and administrative domination by government.
  • BCCI is not "State" but subject to Art. 226 (Zee Telefilms).
  • Judicial orders are not State action under Art. 32 (Naresh Mirajkar).
  • Arts. 19 and 21 can be enforced against private persons (Kaushal Kishor, 2023).

Unit 5

Fundamental Rights: definition, inconsistent laws, and the right to equality

Equality in India means treating equals equally and permitting reasonable classification, then going further: the State must not act arbitrarily and may make special provisions to achieve real equality.

What are Fundamental Rights?

Part III (Arts. 12 to 35) guarantees rights that are justiciable (enforceable through courts), limit State power, and form part of the basic structure. Dr Ambedkar called Article 32 "the very soul" and "heart" of the Constitution.

Article 13: laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights

StatuteArt. 13

(1) All laws in force immediately before the commencement of this Constitution, in so far as they are inconsistent with this Part, shall, to the extent of such inconsistency, be void. (2) The State shall not make any law which takes away or abridges the rights conferred by this Part, and any law made in contravention of this clause shall, to the extent of the contravention, be void. (3) "Law" includes any ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification, custom or usage having the force of law. (4) Nothing in this article shall apply to any amendment of this Constitution made under article 368.

Doctrines under Article 13

Doctrine of eclipse: a pre-Constitution law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right is not dead but eclipsed. It becomes operative again if the right is amended to remove the inconsistency.

CaseBhikaji Narain Dhakras v State of Madhya Pradesh (1955)

A 1948 law giving the State a monopoly over motor transport conflicted with Art. 19(1)(g). After the 1st Amendment (1951) allowed State monopoly, the law revived. The law was "overshadowed" by the Fundamental Right, not wiped out.

  • The doctrine applies to pre-Constitution laws. Post-Constitution laws made in breach of Art. 13(2) are void from inception as against citizens (Deep Chand v State of UP, 1959), though they may still apply to non-citizens for rights available only to citizens (State of Gujarat v Shri Ambica Mills, 1974).

Doctrine of severability: only the inconsistent part is void, if it can be separated from the rest without defeating the legislative intent.

CaseR.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla v Union of India (1957)

The Prize Competitions Act was held valid as applied to gambling competitions; the parts that could cover competitions of skill were severable.

Doctrine of waiver: a citizen cannot waive a Fundamental Right.

CaseBasheshar Nath v Commissioner of Income Tax (1959)

A taxpayer who had agreed to a settlement under a provision later held discriminatory was not bound; Fundamental Rights rest on public policy and cannot be waived.

Amendments and Art. 13(4): in Golaknath (1967), the Court held a constitutional amendment is "law" under Art. 13(2). The 24th Amendment (1971) added Art. 13(4) to reverse this, and Kesavananda (1973) upheld it, subject to the basic structure limit.

Article 14: equality before law and equal protection of laws

StatuteArt. 14

The State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India.

  • Equality before law (from English law, Dicey's rule of law): no one is above the law; a negative concept.
  • Equal protection of laws (from the 14th Amendment of the US Constitution): equal treatment in equal circumstances; a positive concept.
  • Available to all persons, including non-citizens and corporations.

Reasonable classification

Art. 14 forbids class legislation, not reasonable classification. Classification is valid if:

  1. it is based on an intelligible differentia distinguishing those grouped together from those left out; and
  2. the differentia has a rational nexus with the object of the law.
CaseState of West Bengal v Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952)

A law allowing the State government to send any case to special courts with a shorter procedure, without laying down any criteria, was struck down. Unguided discretion to pick cases violated Art. 14.

CaseRam Krishna Dalmia v Justice S.R. Tendolkar (1958)

The Court restated the classification test and principles: a law may be constitutional even if it applies to one person, if there are special circumstances; there is a presumption of constitutionality.

The new doctrine: arbitrariness

CaseE.P. Royappa v State of Tamil Nadu (1974)

Bhagwati J said equality is a dynamic concept that cannot be confined within traditional limits. Equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies; an arbitrary act is unequal and violates Art. 14.

CaseManeka Gandhi v Union of India (1978)

Art. 14 strikes at arbitrariness in State action and ensures fairness and equality of treatment. Arts. 14, 19 and 21 are inter-linked.

CaseShayara Bano v Union of India (2017)

Triple talaq (talaq-e-biddat) was held unconstitutional. Nariman J, for the majority on this ground, held that even legislation can be struck down for manifest arbitrariness, meaning something done capriciously, irrationally or without adequate determining principle.

Article 15: prohibition of discrimination

StatuteArt. 15(1), (2)

The State shall not discriminate against any citizen on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth or any of them. No citizen shall, on those grounds only, be subject to any disability, liability, restriction or condition with regard to access to shops, public restaurants, hotels and places of public entertainment, or the use of wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads and places of public resort maintained wholly or partly out of State funds or dedicated to the use of the general public.

  • "Only" means discrimination on these grounds alone is forbidden; a classification based on other factors too may be valid.
  • Art. 15(2) binds private persons too.

The 103rd Amendment providing EWS reservation was upheld (3:2). Economic criteria alone can be a basis for reservation, and the exclusion of SCs, STs and OBCs from the EWS quota does not violate the basic structure. The 50% ceiling is not inflexible for EWS.

Section 377 IPC was read down to decriminalise consensual same-sex relations between adults. The Court held that "sex" in Art. 15 includes sexual orientation.

Exceptions:

  • 16(3): Parliament may prescribe residence requirements for employment in a State.
  • 16(4): reservation for backward classes not adequately represented in State services.
  • 16(4A) (77th Amendment, 1995; consequential seniority by 85th Amendment, 2001): reservation in promotion for SCs and STs.
  • 16(4B) (81st Amendment, 2000): carry forward of unfilled reserved vacancies outside the 50% ceiling for that year.
  • 16(5): offices of religious institutions may be reserved for members of that religion.
  • 16(6) (103rd Amendment): up to 10% for EWS.
CaseIndra Sawhney v Union of India (1992)

A nine-judge bench upheld 27% reservation for OBCs (Mandal Commission). It held: (1) caste can be a criterion of backwardness; (2) the "creamy layer" must be excluded from OBCs; (3) reservation should not ordinarily exceed 50%; (4) no reservation in promotions under Art. 16(4) (later overcome by Art. 16(4A)); (5) Art. 16(4) is not an exception to 16(1) but a facet of it.

CaseM. Nagaraj v Union of India (2006)

Upheld Arts. 16(4A) and (4B), but required the State to show backwardness, inadequacy of representation and overall administrative efficiency before providing reservation in promotions.

CaseJarnail Singh v Lachhmi Narain Gupta (2018)

Modified Nagaraj: the State need not collect data on backwardness of SCs and STs for promotion reservation, but the creamy layer principle applies to them in promotions.

CaseState of Punjab v Davinder Singh (2024)

A seven-judge bench (6:1) held that sub-classification within Scheduled Castes for reservation is permissible, overruling E.V. Chinnaiah (2004). The majority also suggested a creamy layer for SCs and STs, though that was not a binding direction.

Articles 17 and 18

  • Art. 17: untouchability is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden; enforced through the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 and the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989.
  • Art. 18: abolition of titles, except military and academic distinctions. National awards such as Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are not titles, provided they are not used as prefixes or suffixes: Balaji Raghavan v Union of India (1996).
Exam tip

Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseChinthada Anand v State of Andhra Pradesh (2026)

A person who has converted to Christianity cannot claim Scheduled Caste status or invoke the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, because the Constitution (Scheduled Castes) Order, 1950 confines SC status to Hindus, Sikhs and Buddhists.

Whether a candidate belongs to the OBC 'creamy layer' cannot be decided on parental income alone; the status and post of the parents must also be considered.

CaseJane Kaushik v Union of India (2025)

Upholding the rights of a transgender teacher who was discharged from two schools, the Court ordered compensation and directed steps to implement the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019.

Points to remember

  • SC status not available to converts to Christianity (Chinthada Anand, 2026, an AP case); creamy layer not by income alone (Rohith Nathan, 2026).
  • Art. 13 voids inconsistent laws; doctrines of eclipse (Bhikaji), severability (R.M.D.C.) and no waiver (Basheshar Nath).
  • Art. 14: reasonable classification needs intelligible differentia and rational nexus; arbitrariness also violates Art. 14 (Royappa; Shayara Bano).
  • Art. 15 forbids discrimination on grounds "only" of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth; 15(3) to 15(6) permit special provisions.
  • Indra Sawhney: OBC reservation upheld, creamy layer, 50% rule, no promotion reservation (later Art. 16(4A)).
  • EWS upheld (Janhit Abhiyan, 2022); SC sub-classification allowed (Davinder Singh, 2024).

Right to freedom: Article 19 and property

Article 19 gives citizens six freedoms, each matched by a clause listing the only grounds on which the State may impose reasonable restrictions.

  • Freedom of the press

  • Freedom of movement
  • Freedom to reside and settle

  • Property and social control after 1978

  • Restrictions must be imposed by law, on a listed ground, and must be reasonable, both substantively and procedurally.
CaseState of Madras v V.G. Row (1952)

The test of reasonableness must be applied to each statute; the court considers the nature of the right, the purpose of the restriction, the extent and urgency of the evil, the disproportion of the imposition and prevailing conditions. There is no abstract standard.

Since Modern Dental College v State of MP (2016) and K.S. Puttaswamy (2017), Indian courts also apply proportionality: a legitimate aim, a rational connection, the least restrictive means and a fair balance.

6.1 Freedom of speech and expression

It includes the right to express one's views by words, writing, printing, pictures, films, symbols and any other medium; the right to receive information; the right to silence; the right to fly the national flag (Naveen Jindal, 2004); commercial speech (Tata Press v MTNL, 1995); and expression over the internet.

CaseRomesh Thappar v State of Madras (1950)

A ban on entry and circulation of the journal Cross Roads in Madras for "public safety" was struck down. Freedom of speech includes freedom of circulation. "Public order" was not then a ground in 19(2); the 1st Amendment (1951) added public order, friendly relations with foreign States and incitement to an offence, and inserted the word "reasonable".

CaseShreya Singhal v Union of India (2015)

Section 66A of the IT Act (punishing "grossly offensive" or "annoying" online messages) was struck down as vague and overbroad. The Court distinguished discussion, advocacy and incitement: only incitement can be curbed on public order grounds.

CaseAnuradha Bhasin v Union of India (2020)

Freedom of speech and the freedom to carry on trade through the internet are protected under Arts. 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(g). Internet shutdowns must be temporary, proportionate, published and subject to review.

CaseS. Rangarajan v P. Jagjivan Ram (1989)

Freedom of expression cannot be suppressed on account of threats of demonstrations; the anticipated danger must be proximate and directly connected to the expression, like a "spark in a powder keg".

6.2 Freedom of the press

The Constitution does not mention the press separately; it is part of Art. 19(1)(a), as Dr Ambedkar explained in the Constituent Assembly.

CaseBrij Bhushan v State of Delhi (1950)

Pre-censorship of a journal was held to be a restriction on freedom of the press.

CaseSakal Papers v Union of India (1962)

A law regulating the number of pages according to price was struck down; it would reduce circulation and so curtail freedom of the press. The State cannot curtail one freedom to promote the general public interest in a business matter.

CaseBennett Coleman and Co v Union of India (1973)

Newsprint control that restricted the number of pages was struck down; the press has freedom of circulation and of volume.

CaseIndian Express Newspapers v Union of India (1985)

Excessive import duty on newsprint could be challenged under Art. 19(1)(a); a tax that stifles the press is not reasonable.

6.3 Freedom of speech and contempt of court

"Contempt of court" is a ground of restriction in 19(2). The Supreme Court and High Courts are courts of record with power to punish for contempt (Arts. 129 and 215). The Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 defines civil contempt (wilful disobedience of orders) and criminal contempt (scandalising the court, prejudicing proceedings, obstructing justice). Since 2006, s.13 allows truth as a defence if in public interest and bona fide.

CaseE.M.S. Namboodiripad v T. Narayanan Nambiar (1970)

The Chief Minister of Kerala described judges as instruments of class oppression. The Court held this was contempt; freedom of speech does not extend to bringing the administration of justice into disrepute.

CaseIn re Arundhati Roy (2002)

The author was sentenced to a symbolic one day's imprisonment and fine for statements attributing improper motives to the Court.

CaseIn re Prashant Bhushan (2020)

The advocate was held guilty of criminal contempt for tweets on the Chief Justice and the Court, and fined one rupee. The case renewed debate on whether "scandalising the court" is consistent with free speech.

6.4 Freedom of assembly

The right to hold meetings, processions and demonstrations, peaceably and without arms.

CaseHimat Lal K. Shah v Commissioner of Police (1973)

Rules giving the police unguided power to refuse permission for public meetings on streets were struck down. The State may regulate but not prohibit the use of public streets for meetings.

CaseRamlila Maidan Incident, In re (2012)

A midnight police action against sleeping protesters at Ramlila Maidan was held to violate Arts. 19(1)(a), 19(1)(b) and 21; restrictions must be reasonable and not excessive.

CaseAmit Sahni v Commissioner of Police (2020)

Concerning the Shaheen Bagh protest, the Court held that public ways cannot be occupied indefinitely; the right to protest must be balanced with the rights of commuters, and demonstrations must be in designated areas.

6.5 Freedom of association

The right to form associations, unions and co-operative societies (the last added by the 97th Amendment, 2011). It includes the right not to join an association.

CaseAll India Bank Employees' Association v National Industrial Tribunal (1962)

The right to form unions does not include a fundamental right to strike or to collective bargaining.

CaseDamyanti Naranga v Union of India (1971)

A law that forced new members into a private society (the Hindi Sahitya Sammelan) without the consent of existing members violated Art. 19(1)(c). The right includes the right to associate only with those one chooses.

CaseT.K. Rangarajan v Government of Tamil Nadu (2003)

Government employees have no fundamental, legal, moral or equitable right to strike.

6.6 and 6.7 Freedom of movement and to reside and settle

Art. 19(1)(d) protects movement within India; movement outside India is part of Art. 21 (Satwant Singh Sawhney, 1967; Maneka Gandhi, 1978). Art. 19(1)(e) protects residence and settlement anywhere in India; restrictions protect Scheduled Tribes (Fifth and Sixth Schedule areas) and public interests.

CaseN.B. Khare v State of Delhi (1950)

An externment order under the East Punjab Public Safety Act was upheld as a reasonable restriction, with procedural safeguards.

CaseKharak Singh v State of Uttar Pradesh (1963)

Police regulations authorising domiciliary visits at night violated personal liberty under Art. 21; surveillance did not by itself restrict movement under Art. 19(1)(d). The minority (Subba Rao J) recognised a right to privacy, later adopted in Puttaswamy.

CaseState of Uttar Pradesh v Kaushalya (1964)

The externment of prostitutes under the Suppression of Immoral Traffic Act was upheld as a reasonable restriction in the interests of the general public.

6.8 Freedom of profession, occupation, trade or business

CaseChintaman Rao v State of Madhya Pradesh (1950)

A total ban on manufacturing bidis during the agricultural season in certain villages was struck down as excessive. A restriction must not be arbitrary or beyond what the interests of the public require.

CaseSodan Singh v New Delhi Municipal Committee (1989)

Street trading by hawkers is a fundamental right under Art. 19(1)(g), subject to reasonable regulation of time and place. (Now regulated by the Street Vendors Act, 2014.)

CaseKhoday Distilleries v State of Karnataka (1995)

There is no fundamental right to trade in intoxicating liquor; the State may prohibit it or grant privileges on conditions.

  • Art. 19(6) allows the State to require professional or technical qualifications and to create State monopolies (added by the 1st Amendment), validating nationalisation of transport and similar businesses.

6.9 Property and social control: 1950 to 1978

Originally, property was protected by Art. 19(1)(f) (right to acquire, hold and dispose of property) and Art. 31 (no deprivation except by authority of law; compulsory acquisition only for public purpose with compensation). This led to a long contest between Parliament and the courts over land reform and nationalisation.

6.10 Property after 1978

The 44th Amendment (1978) deleted Art. 19(1)(f) and Art. 31, and inserted Art. 300A: "No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law." The right to property became a constitutional and legal right, not a Fundamental Right.

Consequences:

  • A person cannot approach the Supreme Court directly under Art. 32 for violation of the right to property; he must go to the High Court under Art. 226 or a civil court.
  • Art. 31A(1) second proviso: when the State acquires land under personal cultivation within the ceiling limit, it must pay compensation at not less than market value.
  • Art. 30(1A): when acquiring property of a minority educational institution, the amount fixed must not restrict or abrogate the Art. 30 right.
CaseJilubhai Nanbhai Khachar v State of Gujarat (1995)

Art. 300A does not itself require payment of compensation; the right is subject to law, and the law need not provide compensation.

CaseK.T. Plantation v State of Karnataka (2011)

A Constitution Bench held that a law depriving a person of property under Art. 300A must be just, fair and reasonable and serve a public purpose; the obligation to pay compensation, though not expressly in Art. 300A, can be inferred, and its absence could make a law vulnerable.

CaseVidya Devi v State of Himachal Pradesh (2020)

The State took the appellant's land for a road in 1967 without acquisition proceedings. The Court held that the right to property is a constitutional right and a human right; the State cannot take private property without due process, and ordered compensation.

CaseKolkata Municipal Corporation v Bimal Kumar Shah (2024)

The Court identified seven sub-rights within Art. 300A: the right to notice, to be heard, to a reasoned decision, to acquisition only for public purpose, to restitution or fair compensation, to an efficient and expeditious procedure, and to conclusion of proceedings.

Exam tip

For any Art. 19 question, use a three-step structure: (1) Is the activity within the freedom? (2) Is the restriction on a ground listed in clauses (2) to (6)? (3) Is it reasonable and proportionate? Then cite one leading case for each step.

6.11 Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseImran Pratapgarhi v State of Gujarat (2025)

An FIR over a poem recited in a social media video was quashed. Speech must be judged by the standards of reasonable, strong-minded persons, and the police must respect Art. 19(1)(a) before registering cases over speech.

CaseJust Rights for Children Alliance v S. Harish (2026)

The Court recognised access to the internet as part of the rights under Arts. 19(1)(a) and 21, while calling for strong measures (including a national database) against child sexual abuse material.

Points to remember

  • Speech offences judged by reasonable persons' standards (Imran Pratapgarhi, 2025); internet access as a fundamental right (2026).
  • Art. 19 gives six freedoms to citizens; restrictions must be by law, on listed grounds, and reasonable (V.G. Row) and proportionate.
  • Speech includes press (Sakal Papers, Bennett Coleman), circulation (Romesh Thappar) and internet (Shreya Singhal, Anuradha Bhasin).
  • Contempt of court is a valid restriction; truth is a defence since 2006.
  • Assembly can be regulated but not prohibited outright (Himat Lal Shah); public ways cannot be occupied indefinitely (Amit Sahni).
  • No fundamental right to strike (All India Bank Employees; T.K. Rangarajan).
  • Property: from Art. 19(1)(f) and 31 to Art. 300A (44th Amendment, 1978); now a constitutional and human right (Vidya Devi; Bimal Kumar Shah).

Unit 7

Right against exploitation and rights of minorities

Articles 23 and 24 protect people from exploitation by anyone, State or private; Articles 29 and 30 protect the cultural and educational life of minorities.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Prohibition of traffic in human beings and forced labour (Art. 23)
  • Prohibition of employment of children in factories etc. (Art. 24)
  • Protection of interests of minorities (Art. 29)
  • Right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions (Art. 30)

Article 23: traffic in human beings and forced labour

StatuteArt. 23

(1) Traffic in human beings and begar and other similar forms of forced labour are prohibited, and any contravention shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law. (2) Nothing in this article prevents the State from imposing compulsory service for public purposes, without discrimination on grounds only of religion, race, caste or class.

  • "Traffic in human beings" includes sale and purchase of persons, trafficking of women and children for immoral purposes, and slavery.
  • "Begar" is compulsory work without remuneration, once common under zamindars.
  • "Other similar forms of forced labour" include bonded labour and payment below minimum wages.
  • Art. 23 is enforceable against private persons.
  • Laws: Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956; Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976; trafficking is now punishable under s.143 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.
CasePeople's Union for Democratic Rights v Union of India (1982)

Workers on the Asiad construction projects in Delhi were paid less than the minimum wage. Bhagwati J held that labour or service provided for remuneration less than the minimum wage is "forced labour" under Art. 23; force includes economic compulsion that leaves no choice.

CaseBandhua Mukti Morcha v Union of India (1984)

On a letter petition about bonded labourers in stone quarries near Faridabad, the Court issued directions for identification, release and rehabilitation of bonded labourers, using Arts. 21 and 23 and the Directive Principles.

Article 24: prohibition of child labour

StatuteArt. 24

No child below the age of fourteen years shall be employed to work in any factory or mine or engaged in any other hazardous employment.

CaseM.C. Mehta v State of Tamil Nadu (1996)

Concerning child workers in the Sivakasi match and fireworks industry, the Court directed that offending employers pay ₹20,000 per child into a Child Labour Rehabilitation-cum-Welfare Fund, that an adult member of the child's family be given employment, and that the children be educated.

  • The Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986 (as amended in 2016) prohibits employment of children below 14 in all occupations (except helping family enterprises after school hours) and adolescents (14 to 18) in hazardous occupations.
  • Art. 21A (86th Amendment, 2002) makes free and compulsory education for children of 6 to 14 a Fundamental Right; Art. 39(e) and (f) direct protection of children.

7.3 Protection of interests of minorities: Article 29

StatuteArt. 29

(1) Any section of the citizens residing in India having a distinct language, script or culture of its own shall have the right to conserve the same. (2) No citizen shall be denied admission into any educational institution maintained by the State or receiving aid out of State funds on grounds only of religion, race, caste, language or any of them.

  • Art. 29(1) protects any section of citizens, not only minorities.
  • Art. 29(2) is an individual right of every citizen, not limited to minorities.

7.4 Right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions: Article 30

StatuteArt. 30

(1) All minorities, whether based on religion or language, shall have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. (1A) In making any law for compulsory acquisition of any property of an educational institution established and administered by a minority, the State shall ensure that the amount fixed is such as would not restrict or abrogate the right. (2) The State shall not, in granting aid, discriminate against any educational institution on the ground that it is under the management of a minority, whether based on religion or language.

Who is a minority?

The Constitution does not define it. Minority status is to be determined State-wise, by reference to the population of the State, for both religious and linguistic minorities (T.M.A. Pai Foundation, 2002). The Union notifies national religious minorities under the National Commission for Minorities Act, 1992 (Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Parsis and, since 2014, Jains).

Leading cases

CaseIn re Kerala Education Bill (1958)

The right under Art. 30(1) is not absolute. The State may impose regulations in the interest of educational standards and efficiency (qualifications of teachers, curriculum), but not regulations that destroy the minority character of the institution. Institutions of their "choice" includes general education, not only religious or cultural instruction.

CaseRev. Sidhajbhai Sabhai v State of Bombay (1963)

An order requiring a minority training college to reserve 80% seats for government nominees was struck down. Regulations must be reasonable and must serve the institution's own interest as a minority educational institution.

CaseAhmedabad St Xavier's College Society v State of Gujarat (1974)

A nine-judge bench held that minority institutions are entitled to affiliation, but must follow regulatory standards; provisions vesting control of appointments and discipline in the university were struck down as they interfered with administration.

CaseT.M.A. Pai Foundation v State of Karnataka (2002)

An eleven-judge bench held: (1) minority status is to be decided State-wise; (2) the right to establish and administer includes the right to admit students, set up a reasonable fee structure, constitute a governing body, appoint staff and take disciplinary action; (3) aided minority institutions may be required to admit a reasonable number of non-minority students; (4) the State may regulate for maintaining standards but cannot nationalise seats.

CaseP.A. Inamdar v State of Maharashtra (2005)

A seven-judge bench held that the State cannot impose its reservation policy or quota on unaided minority and non-minority professional institutions. (Parliament responded with Art. 15(5), which excludes minority institutions.)

CasePramati Educational and Cultural Trust v Union of India (2014)

Art. 15(5) was upheld; but the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 was held not applicable to minority schools, aided or unaided, since applying it would infringe Art. 30(1).

CaseAligarh Muslim University v Naresh Agarwal (2024)

A seven-judge bench (4:3) overruled S. Azeez Basha (1967), which had held that AMU was not a minority institution because it was established by a central statute. The Court held that an institution does not lose minority character merely because it was incorporated by statute; the test is who established it, looking at the genesis, purpose and implementation. The question whether AMU itself is a minority institution was left to a regular bench to decide on these tests.

Exam tip

In Art. 30 answers, stress the dual principle from Kerala Education Bill onward: minorities have the right to establish and administer, but the State may make regulations for standards and efficiency that do not destroy minority character.

Unit 8

Personal liberty and rights of the accused

Article 21 began as a narrow guarantee against executive action without law and has become the most expansive right in the Constitution, protecting a life of dignity against unfair law.

What the syllabus lists for this unit

  • Right against self-incrimination (Art. 20(3))
  • Protection against retroactive punishment (Art. 20(1))
  • Double jeopardy (Art. 20(2))
  • Protection against arrest and detention (Art. 22)

Article 21: protection of life and personal liberty

StatuteArt. 21

No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.

From Gopalan to Maneka

CaseA.K. Gopalan v State of Madras (1950)

A communist leader detained under the Preventive Detention Act, 1950 challenged it. The majority held that "procedure established by law" means procedure laid down in any enacted law; it does not import American "due process". Articles 19, 21 and 22 were treated as separate compartments. Fazl Ali J dissented, arguing that the rights overlap.

CaseManeka Gandhi v Union of India (1978)

The petitioner's passport was impounded "in public interest" without reasons or a hearing. A seven-judge bench held: (1) the procedure prescribed by law must be fair, just and reasonable, not fanciful, oppressive or arbitrary; (2) Arts. 14, 19 and 21 are not mutually exclusive; a law affecting personal liberty must satisfy all three (the "golden triangle"); (3) the right to travel abroad is part of personal liberty; (4) natural justice is part of fair procedure. In effect, substantive and procedural due process entered Indian law.

8.2 Right against self-incrimination: Article 20(3)

StatuteArt. 20(3)

No person accused of any offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself.

Three elements: (1) the person must be accused of an offence (a formal accusation such as an FIR or complaint); (2) the protection is against compulsion; (3) it is against being a witness, that is, against testimonial compulsion.

CaseM.P. Sharma v Satish Chandra (1954)

"To be a witness" was read broadly to include producing documents. Search and seizure was held not to violate Art. 20(3) because it does not compel the accused to do anything.

CaseState of Bombay v Kathi Kalu Oghad (1961)

An eleven-judge bench held that "to be a witness" means imparting personal knowledge. Taking specimen signatures, handwriting, fingerprints or thumb impressions is not testimonial and does not violate Art. 20(3).

CaseNandini Satpathy v P.L. Dani (1978)

The protection extends to police interrogation and to a suspect, not only at trial; the accused may refuse to answer questions that tend to incriminate him, and has the right to consult a lawyer during interrogation.

CaseSelvi v State of Karnataka (2010)

Involuntary administration of narco-analysis, polygraph and brain electrical activation profile tests violates Art. 20(3) and Art. 21. Voluntary tests are allowed with safeguards, and information discovered may be used as under the provisions on discovery.

Under s.349 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (earlier s.311A CrPC), a Magistrate may order any person to give specimen signatures, handwriting, finger impressions or voice samples; this is consistent with Kathi Kalu Oghad (see also Ritesh Sinha v State of UP, 2019, on voice samples).

8.3 Protection against retroactive punishment: Article 20(1)

StatuteArt. 20(1)

No person shall be convicted of any offence except for violation of a law in force at the time of the commission of the act charged as an offence, nor be subjected to a penalty greater than that which might have been inflicted under the law in force at the time of the commission of the offence.

  • Bars ex post facto criminal laws: creation of new offences retrospectively and enhancement of punishment.
  • Applies only to conviction and penalty, not to trial or procedure; a change in procedure or forum may be retrospective (Rao Shiv Bahadur Singh v State of Vindhya Pradesh, 1953).
  • Does not prevent a retrospective law that reduces punishment; the accused may get the benefit.
CaseKedar Nath Bajoria v State of West Bengal (1953)

A later law increased the penalty for an offence by adding a fine equal to the amount of money procured. Applying the enhanced penalty to an offence committed earlier violated Art. 20(1).

CaseRattan Lal v State of Punjab (1965)

A boy convicted of an offence was given the benefit of the Probation of Offenders Act, which came into force after the offence. A beneficial retrospective law is not barred by Art. 20(1).

8.4 Double jeopardy: Article 20(2)

StatuteArt. 20(2)

No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once.

  • There must have been a previous prosecution and punishment, before a court or judicial tribunal, for the same offence.
  • The protection is narrower than the common law rule of autrefois acquit and autrefois convict, which is in s.337 of the BNSS (earlier s.300 CrPC).
CaseMaqbool Hussain v State of Bombay (1953)

Confiscation of gold by customs authorities was not a "prosecution and punishment", because the customs authority is not a court; a later criminal prosecution was not barred.

CaseS.A. Venkataraman v Union of India (1954)

A departmental inquiry and dismissal did not bar a later criminal prosecution; the inquiry was not a prosecution.

8.5 Protection against arrest and detention: Article 22

StatuteArt. 22(1), (2)

No person who is arrested shall be detained in custody without being informed, as soon as may be, of the grounds for such arrest, nor shall he be denied the right to consult, and to be defended by, a legal practitioner of his choice. Every person arrested and detained in custody shall be produced before the nearest magistrate within twenty-four hours of such arrest, excluding the time necessary for the journey, and shall not be detained beyond that period without the authority of a magistrate.

  • Art. 22(3): these safeguards do not apply to an enemy alien or to a person arrested or detained under a law providing for preventive detention.
  • Art. 22(4) to (7): safeguards for preventive detention: detention beyond three months requires an Advisory Board report; grounds must be communicated as soon as may be; the detenu must be given the earliest opportunity to make a representation; facts against public interest need not be disclosed.
CasePankaj Bansal v Union of India (2023)

Under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, the grounds of arrest must be furnished to the arrested person in writing.

CasePrabir Purkayastha v State (NCT of Delhi) (2024)

The requirement of furnishing written grounds of arrest was extended to arrests under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act; arrest and remand without informing grounds in writing were held invalid. The Court treated the right to be informed of the grounds as flowing from Art. 22(1).

8.6 Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseHarish Rana v Union of India (2026)

For the first time, the Supreme Court permitted withdrawal of life support, including clinically assisted nutrition and hydration, for a patient in a persistent vegetative state for about 13 years. It applied the Common Cause (2018) guidelines and linked dying with dignity to palliative care.

The grounds of arrest must be given to the arrested person in writing, in a language they understand, for every offence. If that is not possible at the time of arrest, they must be supplied at least two hours before production before the Magistrate; otherwise the arrest and remand are illegal.

CaseDr Jaya Thakur v Union of India (2026)

Menstrual health is part of the right to life and dignity under Art. 21. The Court directed provision of sanitary pads and separate toilets for girls in schools.

CaseManiyar Iliyaz v P. Ayyappan (2026)

Safe, usable footpaths for pedestrians are part of Art. 21.

Points to remember

  • Passive euthanasia allowed for the first time (Harish Rana, 2026); written grounds of arrest in every case (Mihir Rajesh Shah, 2025).
  • Gopalan read Art. 21 narrowly; Maneka required fair, just and reasonable procedure and linked Arts. 14, 19 and 21.
  • Art. 21 now covers dignity, livelihood, privacy (Puttaswamy), speedy trial, legal aid, environment, health and dignified death.
  • Art. 20(3): no testimonial compulsion of an accused; specimen signatures and fingerprints are not testimony (Kathi Kalu Oghad); involuntary narco tests violate it (Selvi).
  • Art. 20(1) bars retrospective criminal laws and enhanced penalties but not beneficial changes (Rattan Lal).
  • Art. 20(2) needs prosecution and punishment before a court (Maqbool Hussain).
  • Art. 22: grounds of arrest, counsel, magistrate within 24 hours; written grounds required (Pankaj Bansal; Prabir Purkayastha).

Unit 9

Constitutional remedies

A right is only as strong as its remedy. Article 32 makes the Supreme Court the protector of Fundamental Rights and is itself a Fundamental Right.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Enforcement of Fundamental Rights (Art. 32)
  • Power of Parliament to modify the rights in their application to the armed forces and others (Art. 33)

Article 32

StatuteArt. 32

(1) The right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for the enforcement of the rights conferred by this Part is guaranteed. (2) The Supreme Court shall have power to issue directions or orders or writs, including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari, whichever may be appropriate, for the enforcement of any of the rights conferred by this Part. (3) Parliament may by law empower any other court to exercise within its local limits all or any of these powers. (4) The right guaranteed by this article shall not be suspended except as otherwise provided for by this Constitution.

  • Dr Ambedkar called Art. 32 "the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it".
  • Art. 32 itself is a Fundamental Right; judicial review is part of the basic structure: L. Chandra Kumar v Union of India (1997).
  • It can be suspended only during an emergency under Art. 359.

Procedural rules

  • Res judicata: a petition under Art. 226 decided on merits bars a fresh petition under Art. 32 on the same facts: Daryao v State of Uttar Pradesh (1961). A dismissal in limine without a speaking order does not.
  • Locus standi: traditionally only the aggrieved person could apply. Public interest litigation relaxed this.

Public interest litigation and new remedies

CaseS.P. Gupta v Union of India (1981)

In the Judges' Transfer case, the Court held that any member of the public acting bona fide can maintain an action for redress of a public wrong or injury, especially for persons unable to approach the court due to poverty or disability.

CaseBandhua Mukti Morcha v Union of India (1984)

A letter could be treated as a writ petition; the Court can appoint commissions to gather facts, and the proceedings are not adversarial.

Compensation as a remedy

CaseRudul Sah v State of Bihar (1983)

The petitioner was kept in jail for 14 years after his acquittal. The Court ordered compensation under Art. 32 for violation of Art. 21, holding that the right to compensation is a part of the remedy for deprivation of fundamental rights.

CaseNilabati Behera v State of Orissa (1993)

A young man died in police custody. The Court held that compensation for violation of Fundamental Rights is a public law remedy based on strict liability; the defence of sovereign immunity does not apply.

Article 33: modification of rights for armed forces and others

StatuteArt. 33

Parliament may by law determine to what extent any of the rights conferred by this Part shall, in their application to (a) the members of the armed forces; (b) members of forces charged with the maintenance of public order; (c) persons employed in any bureau or organisation established by the State for purposes of intelligence or counter-intelligence; or (d) persons employed in or in connection with telecommunication systems set up for the purposes of any of these forces, be restricted or abrogated so as to ensure the proper discharge of their duties and the maintenance of discipline among them.

  • Only Parliament, not State legislatures, can make such laws.
  • Laws include the Army Act, 1950, the Navy Act, 1957, the Air Force Act, 1950, the Police Forces (Restriction of Rights) Act, 1966.
CaseRam Sarup v Union of India (1965)

s.125 of the Army Act, allowing military authorities to choose whether to try an offender by court-martial or criminal court, was upheld as a law under Art. 33.

CaseR. Viswan v Union of India (1983)

Restrictions on members of the General Reserve Engineering Force (Border Roads Organisation), integral to the armed forces, were upheld under Art. 33.

CaseLt Col Prithi Pal Singh Bedi v Union of India (1982)

The Court held that members of the armed forces are citizens in uniform; Art. 33 permits restriction only to the extent necessary for discipline, and fundamental rights are not wholly abrogated without a law under Art. 33.

  • Art. 34: Parliament may indemnify acts done in an area where martial law was in force.
  • Art. 35: Parliament alone has power to make laws for certain matters in Part III (for example Arts. 16(3), 32(3), 33, 34) and to prescribe punishment for acts declared offences under Part III (such as untouchability).

Points to remember

  • Art. 32 is a Fundamental Right and part of the basic structure; it can be suspended only under Art. 359.
  • Five writs: habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto.
  • Art. 226 is wider than Art. 32 (any other purpose).
  • Res judicata applies to writ petitions decided on merits (Daryao).
  • PIL relaxed standing (S.P. Gupta); compensation is a public law remedy (Rudul Sah; Nilabati Behera).
  • Art. 33 lets Parliament restrict rights of the armed forces, police and intelligence personnel for discipline.

Unit 10

Directive Principles and Fundamental Duties

Directive Principles are not enforceable in courts but are fundamental in governance; the courts have moved from treating them as subordinate to Fundamental Rights to reading the two together as a harmonious whole.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Directive Principles: reasons for incorporation
  • Directions of social change and a new social order
  • Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles: inter-relationship and judicial balancing
  • Constitutional amendments: Arts. 31A, 31B and 31C
  • Judicial policy towards Directive Principles
  • Fundamental Duties (Art. 51A)

10.1 Why Directive Principles?

  • Drawn from the Irish Constitution (1937), which had "Directive Principles of Social Policy", and from the Government of India Act, 1935's "Instruments of Instructions".
  • They set out the goal of a welfare State and economic democracy. Dr Ambedkar described them as a novel feature, instructions to future governments.
  • Resources in 1950 were too scarce to make socio-economic rights enforceable immediately, so they were made non-justiciable.
StatuteArt. 37

The provisions contained in this Part shall not be enforceable by any court, but the principles therein laid down are nevertheless fundamental in the governance of the country and it shall be the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws.

Phase 1: primacy of Fundamental Rights

CaseState of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan (1951)

The Directive Principles have to conform to and run subsidiary to the chapter on Fundamental Rights. A G.O. justified by Art. 46 could not override Art. 29(2).

Phase 2: harmonious construction

CaseIn re Kerala Education Bill (1958)

While Fundamental Rights cannot be overridden, courts should adopt the principle of harmonious construction and give effect to both as far as possible.

Phase 3: balance as basic structure

CaseKesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973)

The Court upheld the first part of Art. 31C (laws giving effect to Art. 39(b) and (c) are not void for violating Arts. 14 and 19), but struck down the second part, which excluded judicial review of whether a law actually gives effect to those principles.

CaseMinerva Mills v Union of India (1980)

The 42nd Amendment had extended Art. 31C to all Directive Principles. The Court struck down that extension. Chandrachud CJ said that the Indian Constitution is founded on the bedrock of the balance between Parts III and IV; to give absolute primacy to one over the other is to disturb the harmony of the Constitution. That harmony and balance is a basic feature.

Phase 4: Directive Principles to interpret Fundamental Rights

The courts now read Directive Principles into Fundamental Rights, especially Art. 21: livelihood (Olga Tellis, Art. 39(a)), legal aid (Hoskot, Art. 39A), education (Unni Krishnan, Arts. 41, 45), environment (Art. 48A), and child labour (M.C. Mehta, Art. 39(e), (f)).

10.5 Judicial policy towards Directive Principles

  • Courts cannot direct the legislature to enact a law to implement a Directive Principle, but they use the Principles to determine reasonableness of restrictions under Art. 19 and the content of Art. 21.
CaseState of Gujarat v Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kassab Jamat (2005)

A seven-judge bench upheld a total ban on slaughter of cows and progeny, reading Arts. 48 and 51A(g) together with Art. 19(6) to find the restriction reasonable.

CaseUnni Krishnan v State of Andhra Pradesh (1993)

Reading Arts. 41 and 45 into Art. 21, the Court held that children up to 14 have a fundamental right to free education. This led to Art. 21A (86th Amendment, 2002).

10.6 Fundamental Duties: Article 51A

Added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976 on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee, inspired by the Constitution of the former USSR. Ten duties were added; an eleventh, 51A(k), was added by the 86th Amendment, 2002.

It shall be the duty of every citizen of India:

  1. to abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag and the National Anthem;
  2. to cherish and follow the noble ideals which inspired our national struggle for freedom;
  3. to uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India;
  4. to defend the country and render national service when called upon;
  5. to promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood, and renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women;
  6. to value and preserve the rich heritage of our composite culture;
  7. to protect and improve the natural environment, and have compassion for living creatures;
  8. to develop the scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform;
  9. to safeguard public property and abjure violence;
  10. to strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity;
  11. (k) who is a parent or guardian, to provide opportunities for education to his child or ward between six and fourteen years.

Enforcement and use

  • Fundamental Duties are not directly enforceable, but courts use them to interpret statutes and to judge reasonableness of restrictions.
  • Several laws give effect to them: Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971; Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972; Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.
CaseAIIMS Students' Union v AIIMS (2001)

Fundamental Duties, though not enforceable by writ, provide a valuable guide and aid to interpretation of constitutional and legal issues; an institutional reservation that diluted merit was struck down, with reference to Art. 51A(j).

CaseBijoe Emmanuel v State of Kerala (1986)

Students who stood respectfully but did not sing the National Anthem because of their faith (Jehovah's Witnesses) could not be expelled. There is no law compelling singing, and the duty under Art. 51A(a) is to show respect.

Use the four-phase history (Champakam, Kerala Education Bill, Kesavananda and Minerva Mills, then Art. 21 expansion) for any question on the relationship between Parts III and IV. End with Property Owners Association (2024) to show you are current.

Points to remember

  • Directive Principles are non-justiciable but fundamental in governance (Art. 37).
  • They are grouped as socialist, Gandhian and liberal-intellectual principles.
  • Relationship moved from primacy of rights (Champakam) to harmony (Kerala Education Bill) to balance as basic structure (Minerva Mills).
  • Arts. 31A, 31B and 31C protect laws implementing Directive Principles; post-1973 Ninth Schedule laws are open to basic structure review (I.R. Coelho).
  • Art. 39(b) does not cover every private resource (Property Owners Association, 2024).
  • Fundamental Duties (Art. 51A) were added in 1976; 51A(k) in 2002; they aid interpretation.

Part A Short answer questions5 × 4 = 20 marks

Answer any FIVE of the following.

1.Is the Preamble a part of the Constitution?
Model answer
  • In In re Berubari Union (1960), the Court said the Preamble is not part of the Constitution.
  • Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973) held that it is part of the Constitution and can be amended under Art. 368, provided its basic features are not destroyed. LIC v Consumer Education and Research Centre (1995) agreed.
  • The Preamble is not a source of power or of limitations, but it is a key to interpretation where the text is ambiguous.
  • The 42nd Amendment (1976) added "socialist", "secular" and "integrity". In Dr Balram Singh v Union of India (2024), the Court rejected a challenge to these additions.
2.Formation of new States under Article 3
Model answer

Parliament may by law:

  • form a new State by separating territory from a State or uniting States;
  • increase or diminish the area of a State; and
  • alter its boundaries or name.

The procedure:

  • The Bill needs the President's recommendation, and the President must refer it to the State legislature for its views within a set period.
  • Those views are not binding.
  • Such a law is not an amendment under Art. 368 (Art. 4(2)).

Examples: Andhra State (1953); Telangana (2014); J&K reorganisation (2019), upheld in In re Article 370 (2023).

3.Overseas Citizenship of India
Model answer
  • Art. 11 lets Parliament regulate citizenship. Sections 7A to 7D of the Citizenship Act, 1955 provide for registration of persons of Indian origin as Overseas Citizens of India (OCI).
  • OCI is not dual citizenship. Holders get multiple-entry lifelong visas and parity with NRIs in some economic and educational matters.
  • OCI holders have no voting rights, cannot hold constitutional offices, and cannot normally acquire agricultural land.
  • Registration may be cancelled on grounds such as fraud or disaffection (s.7D).
4."Other authorities" under Article 12
Model answer

Art. 12 defines "State" to include the Government and Parliament of India, State Governments and legislatures, local authorities, and "other authorities".

  • Rajasthan SEB v Mohan Lal (1967): ejusdem generis does not apply; any statutory authority is included.
  • Ajay Hasia v Khalid Mujib (1981): the tests are deep and pervasive State financial, functional and administrative control.
  • Pradeep Kumar Biswas v IICB (2002): the body must be financially, functionally and administratively dominated by the Government.
  • Zee Telefilms v Union of India (2005): the BCCI is not "State", though a writ under Art. 226 lies against it for its public functions.
5.Doctrine of eclipse
Model answer

A pre-Constitution law inconsistent with fundamental rights is not void ab initio. It is overshadowed (eclipsed) by the fundamental right and is dormant. If the inconsistency is removed by a constitutional amendment, the law revives (Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v State of MP, 1955). Points to remember:

  • The doctrine applies to pre-Constitution laws (Art. 13(1)).
  • A post-Constitution law that violates fundamental rights is void from inception and cannot revive (Deep Chand v State of UP, 1959).
  • A post-Constitution law may still be valid against non-citizens where the right is available only to citizens (State of Gujarat v Ambica Mills, 1974).
6.Creamy layer
Model answer

The creamy layer is the advanced section of a backward class, excluded from reservation benefits so that benefits reach the truly backward.

  • Indra Sawhney v Union of India (1992) required its exclusion for OBCs.
  • Jarnail Singh v Lachhmi Narain Gupta (2018) applied the creamy-layer principle to SC/ST promotions.
  • State of Punjab v Davinder Singh (2024) permitted sub-classification within SCs.
  • Union of India v Rohith Nathan (2026): creamy-layer status cannot be decided on parental income alone; the parents' posts and status must also be considered.
7.Right to education under Article 21A
Model answer
  • The 86th Amendment (2002) inserted Art. 21A: the State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children aged 6 to 14 in such manner as the State may by law determine. It also amended Art. 45 (early childhood care) and added Art. 51A(k).
  • The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 implements Art. 21A. It includes the 25% quota for disadvantaged children in private unaided schools, upheld in Society for Unaided Private Schools v Union of India (2012).
  • Unaided minority schools are exempt (Pramati Educational Trust, 2014). The correctness of that exemption was referred to a larger bench in 2025.
8.Writ of quo warranto
Model answer

"By what authority?" The writ questions a person's right to hold a public office. Conditions:

  1. the office must be public, created by the Constitution or a statute;
  2. it must be substantive, not held at pleasure;
  3. the person must be in actual possession of it; and
  4. the appointment must be contrary to law.

Any person may apply; there is no need to be personally aggrieved. Example: University of Mysore v C.D. Govinda Rao (1964).

9.Fundamental Duties
Model answer
  • Part IVA (Art. 51A) was added by the 42nd Amendment (1976) on the Swaran Singh Committee's recommendation. The 86th Amendment (2002) added clause (k). There are eleven duties, among them:
  • to respect the Constitution, the National Flag and Anthem;
  • to uphold sovereignty;
  • to protect the environment;
  • to develop scientific temper;
  • for parents, to provide education.
  • The duties are not directly enforceable, but courts use them to interpret laws and test reasonableness (AIIMS Students' Union v AIIMS, 2002).

Part B Essay questions2 × 15 = 30 marks

Answer any TWO of the following.

10."Equality is antithetic to arbitrariness." Discuss the scope of Article 14.
Model answer

Text

The State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India.

  • Equality before the law is a negative concept taken from Dicey's rule of law: no one is above the law.
  • Equal protection is positive, taken from the American 14th Amendment: like should be treated alike.

Reasonable classification

Art. 14 forbids class legislation but permits reasonable classification. Two tests (State of WB v Anwar Ali Sarkar, 1952; Ram Krishna Dalmia v Justice Tendolkar, 1958):

  1. an intelligible differentia distinguishing those grouped together from those left out; and
  2. a rational nexus between the differentia and the object of the law.

A law may apply to a single person if that person forms a class by himself (Dalmia).

New doctrine: arbitrariness

  • E.P. Royappa v State of Tamil Nadu (1974): equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies.
  • Maneka Gandhi v Union of India (1978): Arts. 14, 19 and 21 form a golden triangle; procedure must be fair, just and reasonable.
  • Ajay Hasia (1981) applied arbitrariness to State action.
  • Shayara Bano v Union of India (2017) and Joseph Shine v Union of India (2018) struck down even legislation for manifest arbitrariness: triple talaq and adultery respectively.

Equality and protective discrimination

  • Arts. 15(4), 15(5), 16(4) and 16(4A) are facets of equality, not exceptions to it (State of Kerala v N.M. Thomas, 1976; Indra Sawhney, 1992).
  • The 50% ceiling and creamy-layer exclusion apply.
  • The 10% EWS reservation was upheld in Janhit Abhiyan v Union of India (2022).

Other applications

  • Equal pay for equal work (Randhir Singh, 1982).
  • Transgender persons as a third gender (NALSA, 2014).
  • Recent cases on converts and SC status (Chinthada Anand, 2026) and on the creamy layer (Rohith Nathan, 2026).

Conclusion

Art. 14 has grown from a rule against unreasonable classification into a general guarantee against arbitrariness, and it supports substantive equality.

11.Discuss the freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a) and the reasonable restrictions on it.
Model answer

Scope

Art. 19(1)(a) guarantees to citizens freedom of speech and expression. The courts have read it to include:

  • freedom of the press (Romesh Thappar v State of Madras, 1950; Indian Express Newspapers, 1985);
  • the right to information and to know (State of UP v Raj Narain, 1975);
  • the right to broadcast; airwaves are public property (Cricket Association of Bengal, 1995);
  • commercial speech (Tata Press, 1995);
  • the right to fly the national flag (Naveen Jindal, 2004);
  • expression of gender identity (NALSA, 2014);
  • speech and business over the internet (Anuradha Bhasin v Union of India, 2020). The Court has since spoken of access to the internet as a fundamental right (Just Rights for Children Alliance v S. Harish, 2026).

Restrictions under Art. 19(2)

These are exhaustive. Restrictions must be reasonable and in the interests of:

  • sovereignty and integrity of India;
  • security of the State;
  • friendly relations with foreign States;
  • public order;
  • decency or morality;
  • contempt of court;
  • defamation;
  • incitement to an offence.

Tests

  • Proximate nexus: the speech must be like a "spark in a powder keg" (S. Rangarajan v P. Jagjivan Ram, 1989).
  • Distinction between discussion, advocacy and incitement (Shreya Singhal v Union of India, 2015, which struck down s.66A of the IT Act).
  • Proportionality (Anuradha Bhasin, 2020).
  • Criminal defamation was upheld (Subramanian Swamy, 2016).
  • Kaushal Kishor v State of UP (2023): no additional restrictions beyond Art. 19(2), and the rights operate horizontally in some cases.
  • Imran Pratapgarhi v State of Gujarat (2025): speech is judged by the standards of reasonable, strong-minded persons, and police must respect free speech before registering FIRs.

Conclusion

Free speech is the foundation of democracy. Restrictions must fall within the eight heads of Art. 19(2) and must be proportionate.

12.Trace the expansion of the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21.
Model answer

From Gopalan to Maneka

  • A.K. Gopalan v State of Madras (1950) read "procedure established by law" as any procedure enacted by law, and read the rights as separate compartments.
  • Maneka Gandhi v Union of India (1978) held that the procedure must be fair, just and reasonable. Arts. 14, 19 and 21 are inter-linked, which in effect brought in due process.

Life means more than animal existence

The Court has read many rights into "life":

  • livelihood (Olga Tellis, 1985);
  • a healthy environment (Subhash Kumar, 1991; M.C. Mehta);
  • free legal aid and speedy trial (Hussainara Khatoon, 1979);
  • dignity against custodial violence (D.K. Basu, 1997);
  • privacy (K.S. Puttaswamy, 2017);
  • dying with dignity and living wills (Common Cause, 2018), first actually applied to allow withdrawal of life support in Harish Rana v Union of India (2026);
  • sexual autonomy (Navtej Singh Johar, 2018);
  • shelter, health and education;
  • menstrual health in schools (Dr Jaya Thakur, 2026);
  • safe footpaths (Maniyar Iliyaz, 2026);
  • protection against the adverse effects of climate change (M.K. Ranjitsinh v Union of India, 2024).

Personal liberty and the accused

  • Written grounds of arrest are required (Pankaj Bansal, 2023; Prabir Purkayastha, 2024). Mihir Rajesh Shah (2025) extended this to every offence, at least two hours before production.
  • Bail is the rule (Satender Kumar Antil, 2022).

Conclusion

Art. 21 has become the most dynamic of all fundamental rights. It is the source of a wide range of unenumerated rights, interpreted in harmony with the Directive Principles and international law.

13.Examine the relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of State Policy.
Model answer

Nature

  • Fundamental Rights (Part III) are justiciable; Directive Principles (Part IV) are not enforceable in courts but are fundamental in governance (Art. 37).
  • Rights are mainly negative obligations on the State; the Directives are positive goals of social and economic democracy.

Phases of the relationship

  1. Primacy of rights. State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan (1951): Directives must run subsidiary to rights. This led to the 1st Amendment (Art. 15(4)).
  2. Harmonious construction. In re Kerala Education Bill (1958): avoid conflict, giving effect to both as far as possible.
  3. Constitutional amendments. The 25th Amendment inserted Art. 31C, protecting laws for Arts. 39(b) and (c). Kesavananda (1973) upheld its first part. The 42nd Amendment extended it to all Directives, which Minerva Mills (1980) struck down as destroying the balance.
  4. Balance as basic structure. Minerva Mills: harmony and balance between Parts III and IV is a basic feature.
  5. Directives as content of rights. Free legal aid (Art. 39A), equal pay (Art. 39(d)), education (Art. 45, later Art. 21A), environment (Art. 48A), and workers' participation were read into Arts. 14 and 21 (Unni Krishnan, 1993; Bandhua Mukti Morcha, 1984).
  6. Recent position. Property Owners Association v State of Maharashtra (2024): not all private resources are "material resources of the community" under Art. 39(b).

Conclusion

The two parts are complementary. The Directives give content and direction to the rights, and courts read them together to achieve the Preamble's goals.

14.The government of a State suspends all mobile internet services in a district indefinitely to prevent "rumours", without publishing the order. Traders challenge it. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Does an indefinite, unpublished internet shutdown violate Arts. 19(1)(a), 19(1)(g) and 21?

Law:

  • Freedom of speech and the freedom to carry on trade through the internet are protected (Anuradha Bhasin v Union of India, 2020).
  • Restrictions must:
  • be published;
  • be necessary and proportionate;
  • be temporary and reviewed periodically, under the Telecom Suspension Rules (now framed under the Telecommunications Act, 2023); and
  • be open to judicial review.
  • Access to the internet has been recognised as part of fundamental rights (Just Rights for Children Alliance v S. Harish, 2026).

Application: An indefinite suspension, unpublished and unreviewed, based on a vague fear of rumours, fails the tests of necessity and proportionality. It is not tied to an imminent threat to public order.

Conclusion: The order is unconstitutional. The court will quash it or direct a reasoned, published, time-bound order with review.

15.X is arrested for cheating. He is told orally why he is arrested but is given no written grounds. He is produced before the Magistrate and remanded. Advise X.
Model answer

Issue: Is the arrest valid without written grounds?

Law:

  • Art. 22(1) and s.47 BNSS require that the arrested person be informed of the grounds of arrest.
  • Pankaj Bansal (2023, PMLA) and Prabir Purkayastha (2024, UAPA) required written grounds.
  • Vihaan Kumar v State of Haryana (2025): non-compliance renders the arrest illegal.
  • Mihir Rajesh Shah v State of Maharashtra (2025): written grounds, in a language the arrested person understands, are mandatory for all offences. If not given at arrest, they must be supplied at least two hours before production for remand; otherwise the arrest and remand are illegal.

Application: Only oral grounds were given, and nothing in writing was supplied before the remand hearing.

Conclusion: The arrest and remand are illegal. X is entitled to be released, though the investigation may continue in accordance with law. X may also seek compensation for illegal detention.

16.A State law reserves 60% of seats in State medical colleges for backward classes, in addition to the 10% EWS quota. Its validity is challenged. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Can reservation exceed the 50% ceiling?

Law:

  • Indra Sawhney (1992): reservation under Arts. 15(4) and 16(4) should not ordinarily exceed 50%, except in extraordinary situations, such as far-flung areas, which must be shown by quantifiable data.
  • Dr Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil v Chief Minister (2021): the Maratha quota beyond 50% was struck down.
  • Janhit Abhiyan (2022): the 10% EWS quota is outside the 50% ceiling, which applies only to backward-class reservation.

Application: A 60% backward-class reservation breaches the 50% ceiling. Unless the State shows exceptional circumstances with quantifiable data, it is invalid. The separate EWS quota is not counted towards the ceiling.

Conclusion: The 60% quota is struck down to the extent it exceeds 50%, unless extraordinary circumstances are established.

17.A cricket player challenges his ban by a national sports body registered as a society, which receives no State funding but regulates the sport. He files a writ petition under Article 32. Is it maintainable?
Model answer

Issue: Is the sports body "State" under Art. 12?

Law:

  • Under the Ajay Hasia and Pradeep Kumar Biswas tests, a body is "State" if it is financially, functionally and administratively dominated by the Government.
  • Zee Telefilms v Union of India (2005): the BCCI is not "State" under Art. 12, so an Art. 32 petition does not lie. However, because it performs public functions, a writ under Art. 226 lies against it (also BCCI v Cricket Association of Bihar, 2015).

Application: The body receives no State funding and is not under deep and pervasive State control, so it is not "State".

Conclusion: The Art. 32 petition is not maintainable. The player may file a writ under Art. 226 because the body performs public functions, or may sue in a civil court.
