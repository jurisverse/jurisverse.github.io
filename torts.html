Unit 1

Evolution, nature and scope of the law of torts

A tort is a civil wrong, other than a mere breach of contract or trust, for which the remedy is an action for unliquidated damages. The law grew case by case in England and came to India as justice, equity and good conscience.

Development in England

  • "Tort" comes from the Latin tortum, twisted or crooked; in French it means simply "wrong".
  • English law developed through forms of action: a plaintiff had to obtain the correct royal writ.
  • Trespass (vi et armis, with force and arms) covered direct and forcible injury to person, land or goods.
  • Trespass on the case (action on the case) covered indirect or consequential harm. Out of it grew negligence, nuisance, defamation, deceit and conversion.
  • The forms of action were abolished by the Common Law Procedure Act, 1852 and the Judicature Acts, 1873 to 1875, but, as Maitland said, "the forms of action we have buried, but they still rule us from their graves": the categories of tort still follow their old outlines.

Reception in India

  • There is no general statute of torts in India. The law is largely uncodified and judge-made.
  • In the Presidency towns, English common law applied. Elsewhere, courts were directed by Regulations (from 1781) and later statutes to decide according to justice, equity and good conscience where no rule existed.
CaseWaghela Rahsanji v Sheikh Masludin (1887)

The Privy Council said that justice, equity and good conscience generally means the rules of English law, if found applicable to Indian society and circumstances.

  • Indian courts therefore apply English principles but adapt them: for example, absolute liability in M.C. Mehta (1987) went beyond English law.
  • Several statutes deal with particular torts: the Fatal Accidents Act, 1855; the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988; the Railways Act, 1989; the Consumer Protection Act, 2019; the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.

Definitions

  • Salmond: a tort is a civil wrong for which the remedy is a common law action for unliquidated damages, and which is not exclusively the breach of a contract or the breach of a trust or other merely equitable obligation.
  • Winfield: tortious liability arises from the breach of a duty primarily fixed by law; this duty is towards persons generally and its breach is redressible by an action for unliquidated damages.
  • Limitation Act, 1963, s.2(m): "tort" means a civil wrong which is not exclusively a breach of contract or breach of trust.

Essentials

  1. A wrongful act or omission by the defendant (an act which violates a legal right of the plaintiff).
  2. Legal damage, meaning violation of a legal right, whether or not actual loss results.
  3. A legal remedy, usually unliquidated damages (an amount not fixed in advance, assessed by the court).

Law of tort or law of torts?

  • Winfield (general principle theory): all harm is actionable unless there is a just cause; so "law of tort".
  • Salmond (pigeonhole theory): there are only specific, recognised torts; a plaintiff must bring his case within one; so "law of torts".
  • Courts have created new torts (negligence after Donoghue v Stevenson, absolute liability in India), which supports Winfield, but they still proceed by recognised heads.

CaseGloucester Grammar School Case (1410)

A rival schoolmaster set up a school nearby, causing the plaintiff to reduce fees. Held: no action. Competition causes loss, but infringes no legal right.

CaseMogul Steamship Co v McGregor, Gow and Co (1892)

Shipowners combined to offer low freight rates to drive a rival out of the China tea trade. Held: no tort. Loss caused by lawful competition is damnum sine injuria.

CaseAshby v White (1703)

A returning officer wrongly refused to register the plaintiff's vote; his candidate won anyway. Held: the plaintiff could sue. Holt CJ said every injury imports a damage; the violation of a right is actionable without proof of loss.

CaseBhim Singh v State of Jammu and Kashmir (1985)

A member of the State Legislative Assembly was arrested and detained to prevent him from attending the Assembly session. The Supreme Court awarded ₹50,000 as exemplary compensation for violation of his personal liberty, an injuria for which compensation was given though no pecuniary loss was proved.

Changing scope and objects of the law of torts

Tort law is expanding because of:

  • complex modern society, scientific and technological progress, industrialisation and occupational hazards;
  • mass torts (Bhopal), environmental harm, product liability, medical negligence, data and privacy injuries;
  • public law compensation for violation of Fundamental Rights (Rudul Sah, Nilabati Behera).

Objects:

  • prescribing standards of conduct (deterrence);
  • compensating victims by money damages;
  • preventing wrongs by injunction;
  • spreading loss (through insurance and vicarious liability).
Exam tip

Points to remember

  • Tort grew from the writs of trespass and case; forms of action still shape it.
  • In India it came as justice, equity and good conscience (Waghela Rahsanji).
  • Tort = civil wrong, not exclusively breach of contract or trust, remedied by unliquidated damages (Salmond; s.2(m) Limitation Act).
  • Damnum sine injuria is not actionable (Gloucester Grammar School; Mogul Steamship); injuria sine damno is (Ashby v White; Bhim Singh).
  • Tort differs from crime (public wrong) and contract (duty fixed by parties).

Principles of liability in torts

Most tort liability rests on fault (intention or negligence), but modern law also imposes liability without fault where activities are hazardous or where statutes demand it.

2.1 to 2.3 Fault-based liability

  • Fault means blameworthy conduct: intention, recklessness or negligence.
  • Wrongful intent: the defendant desires or foresees the harmful consequence. Torts of intention: assault, battery, false imprisonment, deceit, malicious prosecution, conspiracy, trespass.
  • Negligence: failure to take reasonable care where there is a duty to do so (Unit 7). It is both a state of mind (careless) and an independent tort.

2.4 Liability without fault

  • Strict liability (Rylands v Fletcher, 1868): for escape of dangerous things brought on land for non-natural use; some defences allowed.
  • Absolute liability (M.C. Mehta v Union of India, 1987): for enterprises engaged in hazardous or inherently dangerous activities; no exceptions.
  • Vicarious liability: a master is liable for torts of a servant committed in the course of employment though the master is not at fault (Unit 5).
  • Statutory no-fault schemes: motor accidents, railway accidents, employees' compensation.

2.5 Violation of ethical codes

Professional codes (medical council regulations, Bar Council rules, advertising codes) are not themselves torts, but breach of them is evidence of failure to meet the professional standard of care and may support liability in negligence or before consumer commissions.

2.8 Place of motive in torts

Motive (the ulterior reason for an act) is generally irrelevant. A lawful act does not become unlawful because of a bad motive, and an unlawful act is not excused by a good motive.

CaseBradford Corporation v Pickles (1895)

Pickles drained water from his own land so as to reduce the water flowing to the Corporation's reservoir, intending to force the Corporation to buy his land at a high price. Held: no tort. He had a legal right to do what he did, and his malicious motive was irrelevant.

Exceptions where motive (malice) matters:

  • malicious prosecution (malice is an essential);
  • conspiracy (predominant purpose to injure);
  • defamation, where malice defeats the defences of qualified privilege and fair comment;
  • nuisance, where malice may make otherwise reasonable conduct unreasonable (Christie v Davey, 1893).

2.9 to 2.11 Extinguishment of liability

Death: actio personalis moritur cum persona

"A personal action dies with the person." At common law, an action in tort ended on the death of either party.

  • England: the Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, 1934 provides that causes of action survive for and against the estate, except defamation.
  • India: s.306 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 provides that all demands and rights to prosecute or defend any action survive to and against executors and administrators, except causes of action for defamation, assault (as defined in the penal law), or other personal injuries not causing death of the party. The Legal Representatives' Suits Act, 1855 allows actions for wrongs to property within one year before death.
  • Where the wrongful act causes death, the dependants have a separate cause of action under the Fatal Accidents Act, 1855.

Waiver and acquiescence

A person who, knowing of the wrong, elects one remedy (for example, sues in contract or for money had and received) may be taken to have waived the tort. Acquiescence means the plaintiff stands by and allows the wrong to continue, which can bar equitable relief such as an injunction.

Release

The injured person may release the wrongdoer from liability, by deed or for consideration. A release obtained by fraud or without full knowledge is not binding.

Accord and satisfaction

An agreement (accord) to accept something in place of the claim, followed by its performance (satisfaction), discharges the tort. A mere accord without satisfaction does not.

Points to remember

  • Liability rests on fault (intention, negligence) or on no-fault principles (strict, absolute, vicarious, statutory).
  • Statutes impose no-fault liability for railway and motor accidents and employment injuries.
  • Motive is generally irrelevant (Bradford v Pickles), except in malicious prosecution, conspiracy, defamation privilege and nuisance.
  • Actio personalis moritur cum persona is largely abolished by s.306 Indian Succession Act, except for defamation, assault and personal injuries not causing death.
  • Liability may be extinguished by waiver, release, accord and satisfaction and limitation.

Justification in tort: general defences

Even where all the elements of a tort are present, the defendant escapes liability if he can bring himself within a recognised justification.

3.1 Volenti non fit injuria

"To one who consents, no injury is done." A person who voluntarily accepts the risk of harm cannot complain when it happens.

Consent may be express or implied: a spectator at a cricket or motor-racing event, a player in a contact sport, a patient consenting to surgery.

CaseHall v Brooklands Auto Racing Club (1933)

A spectator at a car race was injured when two cars collided and one flew into the crowd. Held: the risk was inherent in the sport and accepted by spectators; the defendants were not liable.

CasePadmavati v Dugganaika (1975)

Two strangers took a lift in a jeep; a wheel came off due to a loose nut, and they were injured. Held: the driver was not liable; the accident was due to a sudden mechanical failure (inevitable accident), and the passengers had voluntarily taken the lift.

Mere knowledge is not consent

Scienti non fit injuria is not the law: knowing of a risk is not the same as agreeing to bear it.

CaseSmith v Baker and Sons (1891)

A workman drilling rocks knew that stones were being swung by crane over his head without warning. A stone fell and injured him. Held: knowledge of the risk did not mean he consented to it; volenti did not apply, especially as an employee does not freely accept risks created by the employer's negligence.

Limits of volenti

  • Consent must be free, not obtained by fraud or under compulsion.
  • The harm must be the same as that consented to.
  • Consent to an illegal act is no defence in many cases.
  • Rescue cases: a person who is injured while trying to rescue others from danger created by the defendant's negligence is not volens.
CaseHaynes v Harwood (1935)

A policeman was injured stopping runaway horses left unattended in a crowded street. Held: the owner was liable; the rescuer acted under a moral duty and was not volens.

  • Unfair Contract Terms: in consumer contracts, exclusion of liability for negligence causing death or personal injury is treated as an unfair term (s.2(46), CPA 2019).

Informed consent in medical treatment

CaseSamira Kohli v Dr Prabha Manchanda (2008)

During a diagnostic laparoscopy to which the patient had consented, the surgeon removed her uterus and ovaries, taking consent from her mother while she was unconscious. The Supreme Court held that consent for diagnosis is not consent for therapeutic surgery; the patient must be given adequate information about the nature, purpose, risks and alternatives. The doctor was liable for the unauthorised surgery.

3.2 Necessity

An act done to prevent greater harm to the public or to the defendant's own interests is justified if reasonable.

  • Public necessity: destroying property to stop a fire spreading or pulling down houses to prevent the spread of a conflagration.
  • Private necessity: protecting one's own person or property.
CaseLeigh v Gladstone (1909)

Force-feeding a suffragette prisoner on hunger strike to save her life was held justified by necessity; no battery.

CaseCope v Sharpe (1912)

The defendant entered the plaintiff's land to prevent a fire from spreading to adjoining land where his master had shooting rights. Held: a reasonable act done in necessity, even if it turned out unnecessary, was justified.

3.3 Plaintiff's default

Where the harm is due to the plaintiff's own wrongful act or fault, he cannot recover, or his damages are reduced.

  • Contributory negligence (Unit 7): reduces damages in proportion to fault.
  • Ex turpi causa non oritur actio: a plaintiff injured while engaged in a criminal enterprise may be barred.

3.4 Act of God and inevitable accident

Act of God (vis major)

An extraordinary natural event, without human intervention, that could not have been foreseen or prevented by reasonable care: exceptionally heavy rainfall, earthquake, lightning, storm.

CaseNichols v Marsland (1876)

Artificial lakes on the defendant's land burst their banks after extraordinary rainfall, "greater and more violent than any within the memory of witnesses". Held: the defendant was not liable under Rylands v Fletcher; the escape was due to an act of God.

Inevitable accident

An accident that could not have been avoided by the exercise of ordinary care, caution and skill. Unlike act of God, it need not involve natural forces.

CaseStanley v Powell (1891)

During a shooting party, a pellet from the defendant's gun glanced off a tree and hit the plaintiff. Held: an inevitable accident; no liability, as the defendant was not negligent.

CaseHolmes v Mather (1875)

Horses driven carefully on a highway bolted on being startled by a barking dog and knocked down the plaintiff. Held: inevitable accident.

3.5 Private defence

A person may use reasonable force to protect his person or property against unlawful force. The force must be proportionate to the threat and used only while the danger lasts.

CaseBird v Holbrook (1828)

The defendant set a spring-gun in his garden without notice. A trespasser who entered to retrieve a peahen was injured. Held: liable; using such a dangerous device without warning was more than reasonable defence of property.

3.6 Statutory authority

An act authorised by statute is not actionable, even if it causes harm, provided it is done without negligence. Where the statute gives absolute authority, there is no liability even for unavoidable nuisance; where it is conditional, the act must be done without causing nuisance.

CaseVaughan v Taff Vale Railway Co (1860)

Sparks from a railway engine, authorised by statute to run on the line, set fire to the plaintiff's wood. Held: the railway was not liable, as it had used all reasonable care.

CaseHammersmith and City Railway Co v Brand (1869)

Vibration from trains running under statutory authority reduced the value of the plaintiff's house. Held: no action, as the damage was the inevitable result of the authorised activity.

3.7 Judicial and quasi-judicial acts

Judges are not liable for acts done in the discharge of their judicial duties, even if in excess of jurisdiction, provided they acted in good faith believing they had jurisdiction. Protection in India is given by the Judicial Officers' Protection Act, 1850 and the Judges (Protection) Act, 1985. Similar protection extends to quasi-judicial authorities and to executive officers carrying out judicial orders.

3.8 Parental and quasi-parental authority

Parents and those in loco parentis (guardians, teachers) traditionally had authority to impose reasonable discipline. In India, s.17 of the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 prohibits physical punishment and mental harassment of children in schools, so corporal punishment by teachers is no longer a defence.

Exam tip

Distinguish carefully: act of God involves natural forces of an extraordinary nature; inevitable accident is any accident not preventable by reasonable care. Both negate negligence, but only act of God is a defence to strict liability under Rylands v Fletcher.

Points to remember

  • Volenti non fit injuria requires free and full consent to the risk; mere knowledge is not consent (Smith v Baker).
  • Rescuers are not volens (Haynes v Harwood); informed consent is required for medical treatment (Samira Kohli).
  • Necessity justifies reasonable acts to prevent greater harm (Leigh v Gladstone; Cope v Sharpe).
  • Act of God (Nichols v Marsland) and inevitable accident (Stanley v Powell) excuse in the absence of negligence.
  • Private defence must be proportionate (Bird v Holbrook).
  • Statutory authority protects acts done without negligence (Vaughan v Taff Vale; Hammersmith v Brand).

Unit 4

Doctrine of sovereign immunity and State liability

The State in India is liable in tort like any employer for acts of its servants, except in a shrinking area of "sovereign functions"; in public law, courts now award compensation for violation of Fundamental Rights without regard to sovereign immunity.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Liability of the State: sovereign and non-sovereign functions
  • Crown Proceedings Act of UK and Federal Tort Claims Act of USA
  • Constitution of India, Articles 294 and 300
  • Act of State

The English and American position

  • At common law "the King can do no wrong", so the Crown was not liable in tort.
  • Crown Proceedings Act, 1947 (UK): the Crown is liable in tort as if it were a private person of full age and capacity, for torts of its servants and agents, for breach of duties owed by employers and for breach of duties attaching to ownership of property.
  • Federal Tort Claims Act, 1946 (USA): the United States is liable for negligent or wrongful acts of federal employees acting within the scope of employment, as a private person would be, subject to exceptions such as discretionary functions.

The constitutional provisions

StatuteArt. 300(1)

The Government of India may sue or be sued by the name of the Union of India and the Government of a State may sue or be sued by the name of the State, and may, subject to any provisions which may be made by Act of Parliament or the State legislature, sue or be sued in relation to their respective affairs in the like cases as the Dominion of India and the corresponding Provinces might have sued or been sued if this Constitution had not been enacted.

  • Art. 300 does not itself define liability; it carries forward the position under s.176 of the Government of India Act, 1935, which went back through the earlier Acts to the liability of the East India Company.
  • Art. 294 provides for succession of the Union and States to the property, assets, rights, liabilities and obligations of the former Dominion and Provinces. This means the State inherits the liabilities of the East India Company and the Secretary of State.

Judicial development

CasePeninsular and Oriental Steam Navigation Co v Secretary of State for India (1861)

A servant of the plaintiff company was injured when government workmen in the Kidderpore dockyard negligently dropped an iron funnel on a road, frightening the horses. Peacock CJ held the government liable, distinguishing between sovereign functions (for which the East India Company was not liable) and non-sovereign or commercial functions which a private person could perform (for which it was liable). Maintaining a dockyard was non-sovereign.

CaseState of Rajasthan v Vidyawati (1962)

A government jeep, kept for the Collector's use, was being driven back from repair by a government driver who negligently knocked down and killed a pedestrian. The Supreme Court held the State liable like any other employer. The State has no immunity in a modern welfare State for such acts; the activity was not sovereign.

CaseKasturilal Ralia Ram Jain v State of Uttar Pradesh (1965)

Police seized gold from a jeweller on suspicion and kept it in the malkhana; a constable absconded with it. The Supreme Court held the State not liable: the power to arrest, search and seize is a sovereign power, so the State was immune, though the act was negligent. The Court urged Parliament to legislate.

CaseN. Nagendra Rao and Co v State of Andhra Pradesh (1994)

Goods seized under the Essential Commodities Act deteriorated through negligence of officers. The Supreme Court held the State liable. It said the doctrine of sovereign immunity has no place in a welfare State, except for functions such as defence, foreign affairs, administration of justice and legislation; Kasturilal should be confined to its own facts.

CaseState of Andhra Pradesh v Challa Ramakrishna Reddy (2000)

A prisoner killed in jail by a bomb attack, despite requests for protection, was held entitled to compensation. The Court held that the right to life is available even to prisoners, and the doctrine of sovereign immunity is not available where Fundamental Rights are violated.

Public law compensation

In Rudul Sah (1983), Bhim Singh (1985) and Nilabati Behera (1993), the Supreme Court awarded compensation under Arts. 32 and 226 for violation of Art. 21. This is a public law remedy based on strict liability, and the defence of sovereign immunity does not apply to it. The private law action in tort remains available separately.

Need for legislation

The First Law Commission (1956, its First Report) recommended a statute defining State liability. Government (Liability in Tort) Bills introduced in 1965 and 1967 lapsed. The law is still judge-made.

Act of State

An act of the sovereign power of a State against a foreign State or its subjects, done in exercise of sovereign power, not cognisable by municipal courts.

CaseBuron v Denman (1848)

A British naval commander destroyed a Spanish slave trader's property and released slaves on the West African coast. The British government ratified the act. Held: it became an act of State, and the Spaniard could not sue in English courts.

CaseSecretary of State in Council of India v Kamachee Boye Sahaba (1859)

The East India Company seized the property of the deceased Raja of Tanjore, claiming the Raj had lapsed. The Privy Council held it an act of State not cognisable by municipal courts.

CaseState of Saurashtra v Memon Haji Ismail (1959)

After Junagadh's accession, the State took possession of property; the Supreme Court held that acts during the transition from one sovereign to another were acts of State.

Points:

  • An act of State cannot be pleaded against a citizen of the State itself, nor against a friendly alien resident within the territory (Johnstone v Pedlar, 1921).
  • After the Constitution, the government cannot plead act of State against its own citizens in respect of their Fundamental Rights.

Unit 5

Vicarious liability

A person may be liable for a tort he did not commit, because of his relationship with the wrongdoer. The master is liable for torts of his servant committed in the course of employment.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Basis, scope and justification
  • Express authorisation
  • Ratification
  • Abetment
  • Special relationships
  • Master and servant: course of employment, who is master, control test, who is servant, borrowed servant, independent contractor
  • Principal and agent
  • Corporation and principal officer

Basis and justification

  • Qui facit per alium facit per se: he who acts through another acts himself.
  • Respondeat superior: let the principal answer.
  • Justifications: the master has the "deep pocket" and insurance; he benefits from the servant's work so should bear its risks; he selects and controls the servant; liability encourages care in selection and supervision; loss is spread across the enterprise.

Ways in which vicarious liability arises

  • Express authorisation: liability for torts one authorises another to commit.
  • Ratification: an act done on one's behalf without authority, later adopted with knowledge, is treated as authorised (the act must have been done on behalf of the ratifier).
  • Abetment: one who instigates, aids or procures a tort is a joint tortfeasor.
  • Special relationships: master and servant, principal and agent, partners (s.26, Indian Partnership Act, 1932), company and its officers, employer and independent contractor (limited).

5.6 Master and servant

Who is a servant?

TestContentCase
---------

The control test is unsuited to skilled professionals (surgeons, pilots), so courts look at the whole relationship. Hospitals are liable for negligence of their staff doctors (Cassidy v Ministry of Health, 1951).

Borrowed servant

Where a servant is lent by his general employer to another, the general employer usually remains liable unless he clearly proves that control over the manner of work passed to the hirer.

CaseMersey Docks and Harbour Board v Coggins and Griffith (1947)

The Board lent a crane with its driver to stevedores, who directed what to load but not how to work the crane. The driver negligently injured a person. Held: the Board, as general employer, remained liable; the burden of shifting liability is heavy.

Independent contractor

An employer is generally not liable for torts of an independent contractor, because he does not control how the work is done. Exceptions:

  • the employer authorised or ratified the tort, or was himself negligent in selecting the contractor;
  • the work is extra-hazardous or inherently dangerous (Honeywill and Stein v Larkin Bros, 1934, where photographers using magnesium flares set fire to a cinema);
  • non-delegable duties imposed by statute or common law (for example, operations on a highway, withdrawal of support from neighbouring land);
  • cases of strict liability under Rylands v Fletcher.

Course of employment

The most important question. Salmond's test: a master is liable for a wrongful act of his servant if it is (a) a wrongful act authorised by the master, or (b) a wrongful and unauthorised mode of doing some act authorised by the master.

CaseCentury Insurance Co v Northern Ireland Road Transport Board (1942)

A driver delivering petrol lit a cigarette and threw away a lighted match while petrol was being transferred, causing an explosion. Held: the master was liable; the driver was doing his authorised job of delivering petrol, though in a negligent way.

CaseLimpus v London General Omnibus Co (1862)

A bus driver, against express instructions, raced with a rival bus and caused an accident. Held: the master was liable; the prohibited act was only a wrongful mode of doing his job of driving.

CaseBeard v London General Omnibus Co (1900)

A bus conductor, in the absence of the driver, drove the bus himself and caused an accident. Held: the employer was not liable, because driving was not within the conductor's employment.

CaseRose v Plenty (1976)

A milkman, against orders, took a boy on his float to help deliver milk; the boy was injured. Held: the employer was liable, as the boy was being used to further the employer's business.

CaseLloyd v Grace, Smith and Co (1912)

A solicitor's managing clerk fraudulently induced a client to transfer her properties to him. Held: the firm was liable, even though the fraud was for the clerk's own benefit, because it was committed within the apparent scope of his authority.

CaseState Bank of India v Shyama Devi (1978)

A bank employee, who was a friend of the plaintiff's husband, received money from the plaintiff to deposit and misappropriated it without following bank procedure. Held: the bank was not liable, because he received the money as a friend, not as part of his employment.

CasePushpabai Purshottam Udeshi v Ranjit Ginning and Pressing Co (1977)

A company's manager, driving the company car on company business, gave a lift to a person, and an accident killed him. The Supreme Court held the company liable, applying the modern trend of treating such acts as within the course of employment.

The close connection test

English courts now ask whether the tort was so closely connected with the employment that it would be fair and just to hold the employer liable: Lister v Hesley Hall (2001) (warden's sexual abuse of boys in a boarding house) and Mohamud v Wm Morrison Supermarkets (2016) (petrol station attendant's assault on a customer).

5.7 Principal and agent

A principal is liable for torts committed by his agent within the scope of the agent's actual or apparent authority. Under s.238 of the Indian Contract Act, misrepresentation or fraud by an agent acting in the course of business affects the principal. Partners are liable for torts of a co-partner in the ordinary course of business of the firm (s.26, Partnership Act).

5.8 Corporation and principal officer

A corporation can act only through human agents, so it is liable for the torts of its servants and agents done in the course of employment, including torts requiring malice. Torts may be attributed to the company itself through its directing mind and will. A corporation is liable for torts even if the act was ultra vires the company, if done within the course of employment.

Exam tip

In a problem, ask three questions in order: (1) Is the wrongdoer a servant or an independent contractor? (2) Was the tort in the course of employment (authorised act in an unauthorised mode)? (3) Does any exception apply (frolic of his own, express prohibition limiting the scope of work)?

Points to remember

  • Vicarious liability rests on qui facit per alium facit per se and respondeat superior.
  • Servant status is decided by control, integration and multiple tests.
  • Borrowed servant: general employer stays liable unless control clearly passes (Mersey Docks).
  • No liability for independent contractors, except for hazardous work, non-delegable duties and own negligence.
  • Course of employment: wrongful mode of an authorised act (Century Insurance, Limpus); not acts outside employment (Beard; Shyama Devi).
  • Liability for fraud within apparent authority (Lloyd v Grace, Smith).

Torts against persons, personal relations, property and business

6.1 Assault, battery and mayhem

  • Assault: an act which causes the plaintiff reasonable apprehension of immediate unlawful force. No contact is needed. Mere words are generally not assault, but words can negative an apparent threat.
  • Battery: intentional application of unlawful force to another without lawful justification. The least touching in anger is a battery (Cole v Turner, 1704).
  • Mayhem: maiming, depriving a person of a member of the body useful for fighting; now covered by battery and grievous hurt.
CaseTuberville v Savage (1669)

The defendant laid his hand on his sword and said, "If it were not assize-time, I would not take such language from you." Held: no assault. The words showed that he did not intend to strike.

CaseStephens v Myers (1830)

At a parish meeting, the defendant advanced with a clenched fist towards the chairman but was stopped before reaching him. Held: assault, since he was close enough and intended to strike.

6.2 False imprisonment

Total restraint of a person's liberty without lawful justification, however short. Physical force is not needed; a show of authority is enough.

CaseBird v Jones (1845)

Part of a public footway was fenced off for spectators; the plaintiff was prevented from going forward but was free to go back. Held: no false imprisonment, as the restraint was partial, not total.

CaseMeering v Grahame-White Aviation Co (1919)

The plaintiff was kept in a room by works police, without knowing he was being watched. Held: false imprisonment; knowledge of the restraint by the plaintiff is not necessary, though it may affect damages.

  • In India, unlawful arrest or detention by the police gives rise to both a private law claim and public law compensation (Rudul Sah, Bhim Singh).

6.3 Defamation

Publication of a false statement that tends to lower a person in the estimation of right-thinking members of society generally, or makes them shun or avoid him.

Essentials

  1. The statement must be defamatory: judged by right-thinking members of society. Innuendo (a hidden meaning understood by people with special knowledge) can make innocent words defamatory.
  2. It must refer to the plaintiff: the test is whether reasonable people would understand it as referring to him, even if the defendant did not intend it.
  3. It must be published: communicated to at least one person other than the plaintiff.
CaseCassidy v Daily Mirror Newspapers (1929)

A newspaper published a photo of Mr Cassidy with a woman, saying their engagement had been announced. His wife's acquaintances thought she had been living with him unmarried. Held: defamatory of the wife by innuendo, though the paper did not know he was married.

CaseNewstead v London Express Newspapers (1940)

A report that "Harold Newstead, a thirty-year-old Camberwell man" had been convicted of bigamy was true of one Newstead but read as referring to another of the same name and age. Held: the paper was liable to the plaintiff; lack of intention is no defence.

CaseMahendra Ram v Harnandan Prasad (1958)

A defamatory letter in Urdu was sent to the plaintiff, who could not read Urdu and had it read by a third person. Held: no publication by the defendant, since he did not know or intend that a third person would read it.

Defences

  • Justification (truth): the statement is substantially true. Truth is a complete defence in civil cases.
  • Fair comment: honest expression of opinion on a matter of public interest, based on true facts, without malice.
  • Absolute privilege: statements in Parliament and State legislatures (Arts. 105, 194), in judicial proceedings by judges, advocates, parties and witnesses, and communications between high officers of State. No action lies even if made maliciously.
  • Qualified privilege: statements made in discharge of a legal, social or moral duty to a person with a corresponding interest (references for employees, reports to police, fair reports of public proceedings). Lost if the statement was made with malice.
CaseR. Rajagopal v State of Tamil Nadu (1994)

The Court held that, in matters of public record and conduct of public officials in discharge of official duties, no action for damages lies for publication of the truth, and even for false statements, the official must show that the publication was made with reckless disregard for truth (adopting the actual-malice principle of New York Times v Sullivan).

6.4 Torts against domestic and personal relations

  • Marital relations: historically, a husband could sue for loss of consortium caused by injury to his wife, for enticement or harbouring of the wife, and (formerly) for adultery. Modern law treats spouses equally; actions for enticement and adultery have largely disappeared. In India, after Joseph Shine v Union of India (2018), adultery is no longer a crime.
  • Parental relations: a parent could sue for loss of the services of a child injured by the defendant (per quod servitium amisit), and for seduction of a daughter.
  • Master and servant: a master could sue for loss of services of a servant injured by the defendant. These relational actions are now rare; dependants rely instead on the Fatal Accidents Act and on claims for loss of consortium in motor accident cases.

6.5 Malicious prosecution

Essentials (the plaintiff must prove all):

  1. Prosecution of the plaintiff by the defendant (setting the criminal law in motion, not merely giving information).
  2. Absence of reasonable and probable cause.
  3. Malice: a purpose other than bringing an offender to justice.
  4. Termination of the prosecution in favour of the plaintiff.
  5. Damage to reputation, person or property.
CaseWest Bengal State Electricity Board v Dilip Kumar Ray (2007)

The Supreme Court restated the elements: the defendant must have initiated proceedings without reasonable and probable cause, with malice, and the proceedings must have ended in the plaintiff's favour. Reasonable and probable cause means an honest belief, based on reasonable grounds, in the guilt of the accused.

6.6 Shortened expectation of life

Damages may be awarded for loss of expectation of life caused by injury. Courts award a moderate, conventional sum rather than attempt to value life.

CaseBenham v Gambling (1941)

The House of Lords held that damages for loss of expectation of life should be moderate, since the court is not valuing a life but compensating the loss of a prospect of happiness; a small conventional sum was awarded for a child's death.

In Indian motor accident cases, courts award conventional amounts for loss of estate, loss of consortium and funeral expenses, following National Insurance Co v Pranay Sethi (2017).

6.7 Nervous shock

Psychiatric injury (a recognised mental illness, not mere grief or distress) caused by the defendant's negligence.

CaseDulieu v White and Sons (1901)

A pregnant woman suffered shock and a premature birth when a horse-drawn van crashed into the bar where she was serving. Held: she could recover, as she feared for her own safety.

CaseHambrook v Stokes Bros (1925)

A mother suffered shock and died after seeing a runaway lorry heading towards where she had left her children. Held: recoverable though she feared for her children, not herself.

CaseBourhill v Young (1943)

A pregnant fishwife heard a motorcycle accident and later saw blood on the road; she suffered shock and a stillbirth. Held: no duty owed to her, as she was outside the area of foreseeable danger.

CaseAlcock v Chief Constable of South Yorkshire Police (1992)

Arising from the Hillsborough stadium disaster, the House of Lords distinguished primary victims (in danger themselves) from secondary victims (witnesses), who must show close ties of love and affection with the victim, proximity in time and space to the event or its immediate aftermath, and perception by their own unaided senses.

6.8 Defences to these torts

Consent, private defence, necessity, lawful authority (arrest by police under law), parental authority, statutory authority, and for defamation, truth, fair comment and privilege.

6.9 Trespass to land

Unjustified intrusion on land in the possession of another: entering, remaining, or placing objects on it. Actionable per se. Mistake is no defence (Basely v Clarkson, 1682, where the defendant mowed the plaintiff's grass believing it to be his own).

  • Trespass ab initio: where a person enters land under authority of law and then abuses that authority by a positive wrongful act, he is treated as a trespasser from the beginning (The Six Carpenters' Case, 1610: mere non-payment for wine was a non-feasance and did not make the entry a trespass ab initio).
  • Dispossession: the person dispossessed may recover possession by suit (s.6 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 gives a summary remedy within six months of dispossession, without regard to title), and claim mesne profits.

6.11 Torts against business interests

  • Injurious falsehood: false and malicious statement about the plaintiff's goods or business causing special damage (Ratcliffe v Evans, 1892, where a false report that the plaintiff's business had ceased caused loss of trade).
  • Negligent misstatement: where a person with special skill gives advice knowing another will rely on it, he may owe a duty of care for economic loss: Hedley Byrne and Co v Heller and Partners (1964).
  • Deceit: a false statement made knowingly or recklessly, intended to be acted on, causing damage (Derry v Peek, 1889).
  • Passing off: misrepresenting one's goods or services as those of another, damaging his goodwill. The elements (from Erven Warnink v Townend, 1979) are a misrepresentation, in the course of trade, to prospective customers, calculated to injure the business or goodwill of another, causing actual damage.
CaseCadila Health Care Ltd v Cadila Pharmaceuticals Ltd (2001)

Concerning drugs sold as "Falcigo" and "Falcitab", the Supreme Court held that in passing off actions for medicinal products a stricter approach is needed to prevent confusion, and set out factors for deciding deceptive similarity.

  • Nervous shock: primary and secondary victims (Alcock).

7.1 and 7.2 Negligence: meaning and theories

  • Subjective theory: negligence is a state of mind, carelessness or indifference (Salmond).
  • Objective theory: negligence is conduct falling below the standard of a reasonable person, whatever the state of mind (Winfield, and the modern law).
  • Negligence as a tort has three elements: (1) a duty of care owed to the plaintiff; (2) breach of that duty; (3) damage caused by the breach that is not too remote.

Duty of care

CaseDonoghue v Stevenson (1932)

A woman drank ginger beer, bought by a friend, from an opaque bottle containing a decomposed snail and fell ill. Held: the manufacturer owed her a duty of care despite the absence of a contract. Lord Atkin's "neighbour principle": you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour, meaning persons so closely and directly affected by your act that you ought reasonably to have them in contemplation.

CaseCaparo Industries v Dickman (1990)

The House of Lords set a three-stage test for new duty situations: foreseeability of damage, proximity of relationship, and whether it is fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty.

7.3 Standard of care and breach

The standard is that of the reasonable man: "Negligence is the omission to do something which a reasonable man would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do" (Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co, 1856).

Factors in deciding breach:

  • Likelihood of harm: Bolton v Stone (1951), a cricket ball hit out of the ground only about six times in thirty years; no breach.
  • Seriousness of harm: Paris v Stepney Borough Council (1951), a one-eyed workman not given goggles; higher care owed because loss of his remaining eye would be catastrophic.
  • Cost and practicality of precautions.
  • Social value of the activity.
  • Special skill: professionals are judged by the standard of a reasonably competent member of the profession.

7.4 Contributory negligence

Where the plaintiff's own lack of care contributes to his harm.

  • Old common law: complete defence (Butterfield v Forrester, 1809).
  • The last opportunity rule softened this: the party who had the last opportunity to avoid the accident is liable (Davies v Mann, 1842, a donkey tethered on the highway).
  • England: Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act, 1945 provides for apportionment: damages reduced to the extent the court thinks just.
  • India has no general statute, but courts apply apportionment as a rule of justice, equity and good conscience.
CaseMunicipal Corporation of Greater Bombay v Laxman Iyer (2003)

The Supreme Court explained that contributory negligence means failure by a person to use reasonable care for his own safety; where both parties are negligent, damages are apportioned according to the degree of fault.

7.5 Res ipsa loquitur

"The thing speaks for itself." Where the cause of the accident is unknown, the plaintiff may raise an inference of negligence if: (1) the thing causing harm was under the management and control of the defendant; (2) the accident is such as in the ordinary course does not happen if proper care is used; (3) the cause is not known.

CaseScott v London and St Katherine Docks Co (1865)

Bags of sugar fell from a crane onto a customs officer. Erle CJ laid down the doctrine: where the thing is under the defendant's management and the accident is one that does not ordinarily happen with care, it is reasonable evidence of negligence in the absence of explanation.

CaseMunicipal Corporation of Delhi v Subhagwanti (1966)

The Chandni Chowk clock tower collapsed, killing several people. The Supreme Court applied res ipsa loquitur: the tower was under the Corporation's control and such collapse does not happen without negligence in maintenance.

  • The effect is to shift the evidential burden to the defendant to explain the accident consistently with no negligence.

7.6 Professional negligence, especially under consumer law

Medical negligence

CaseJacob Mathew v State of Punjab (2005)

A patient died when an oxygen cylinder was found empty. The Supreme Court adopted the Bolam test (Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee, 1957): a doctor is not negligent if he acts in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion. For criminal liability, negligence must be gross or reckless. It also laid down guidelines that a private complaint against a doctor should be supported by credible medical opinion.

CaseKusum Sharma v Batra Hospital (2010)

The Court summarised principles of medical negligence: a doctor must exercise reasonable skill; a mere error of judgment or choice of one reasonable course over another is not negligence; doctors must not be harassed by frivolous complaints.

CaseV. Kishan Rao v Nikhil Super Speciality Hospital (2010)

A patient treated for typhoid when she had malaria died. The Court held that expert evidence is not needed in every case before a consumer forum; where negligence is obvious, res ipsa loquitur may apply.

Professionals under the Consumer Protection Act

CaseIndian Medical Association v V.P. Shantha (1995)

Medical services rendered for payment (including services paid for through insurance or by an employer) are "services" under consumer law; only services rendered entirely free of charge, or under a contract of personal service, are excluded.

CaseBar of Indian Lawyers v D.K. Gandhi PS National Institute of Communicable Diseases (2024)

The Supreme Court held that services of advocates are not within the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, because the advocate-client relationship involves a high degree of direct control by the client and is a contract of personal service, and the profession is sui generis. It also referred the correctness of V.P. Shantha to a larger bench.

7.7 to 7.9 Strict liability: the rule in Rylands v Fletcher

CaseRylands v Fletcher (1868)

The defendants had a reservoir built on their land by independent contractors, who negligently failed to block old mine shafts. Water flooded the plaintiff's mine. Blackburn J held: a person who for his own purposes brings on his land and collects and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes must keep it in at his peril; if he does not, he is prima facie answerable for all damage which is the natural consequence of its escape. The House of Lords added that the use of land must be "non-natural".

Essentials:

  1. A dangerous thing (likely to do mischief if it escapes): water, gas, electricity, explosives, fire, noxious fumes, vibrations.
  2. Escape from the defendant's land to a place outside his control (Read v J. Lyons and Co, 1947: no escape when a shell exploded inside a munitions factory, so no liability).
  3. Non-natural use of land (ordinary domestic water supply is natural use: Rickards v Lothian, 1913).
  4. Damage to the plaintiff.

Exceptions:

  • plaintiff's own default;
  • act of God (Nichols v Marsland, 1876);
  • consent of the plaintiff or common benefit;
  • act of a stranger (Box v Jubb, 1879, where a third party emptied his reservoir into the drain);
  • statutory authority (Green v Chelsea Waterworks, 1894).

In Cambridge Water Co v Eastern Counties Leather (1994) the House of Lords added a requirement that the type of damage be foreseeable.

Absolute liability: the Indian development

CaseM.C. Mehta v Union of India (1987)

Oleum gas leaked from a unit of Shriram Foods and Fertilisers in Delhi in December 1985, affecting many people. The Supreme Court (Bhagwati CJ) held that an enterprise engaged in a hazardous or inherently dangerous industry which poses a potential threat to the health and safety of persons working in the factory and residing in the surrounding areas owes an absolute and non-delegable duty to the community. If harm results, the enterprise is absolutely liable, and none of the exceptions to Rylands v Fletcher apply. The measure of compensation must be correlated to the magnitude and capacity of the enterprise, so that it has a deterrent effect.

Bhopal gas disaster

  • On the night of 2 to 3 December 1984, methyl isocyanate gas leaked from the Union Carbide India Ltd plant in Bhopal, killing thousands and injuring hundreds of thousands.
  • The Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act, 1985 made the Union of India the sole representative of the victims (parens patriae); upheld in Charan Lal Sahu v Union of India (1990).
  • In Union Carbide Corporation v Union of India (1989), the Supreme Court recorded a settlement of US $470 million in full and final settlement. A curative petition by the Union seeking enhanced compensation was dismissed in 2023.
  • The disaster led to the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and the Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991, which requires owners handling hazardous substances to take insurance and provides no-fault relief.

Other disasters named in the syllabus

  • Machhu dam failure (Morbi, Gujarat, 1979): the dam burst after heavy rain, causing massive loss of life; it raised questions of State liability, act of God and design negligence.
  • Nuclear installations: the Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010 channelled liability for nuclear incidents to the operator on a no-fault basis, with a cap and a right of recourse against suppliers. It was repealed, together with the Atomic Energy Act, 1962, by the Sustainable Harnessing and Advancement of Nuclear Energy for Transforming India (SHANTI) Act, 2025, which keeps strict liability of the operator but replaces the flat cap with a tiered structure and removes the open-ended supplier liability.
CaseIndian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v Union of India (1996)

Chemical industries at Bichhri village in Rajasthan polluted groundwater with sludge. The Court applied absolute liability and the "polluter pays" principle, ordering the polluters to pay for remediation.

Unit 8

Nuisance

Nuisance protects the use and enjoyment of land against unreasonable interference, and protects the public against acts that endanger or inconvenience the community.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Definition, essentials and types
  • Acts which constitute nuisance: obstruction of highways, pollution of air and water, noise, interference with light and air

8.1 Definition and types

Nuisance is an unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, or of some right over or in connection with it (Winfield).

Public nuisance

  • An act or omission that causes common injury, danger or annoyance to the public or to people in general who live or occupy property in the vicinity. It is a crime (s.270, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023).
  • A private individual can sue for public nuisance only if he suffers special damage beyond that suffered by the public in general.
  • Remedies: suit by the Advocate-General or two or more persons with leave of court under s.91 of the CPC (no special damage needed); magistrate's conditional order to remove a public nuisance under s.152 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (earlier s.133 CrPC).
CaseMunicipal Council, Ratlam v Vardhichand (1980)

Residents complained of open drains and filth. The magistrate ordered the municipality to construct drains under s.133 CrPC. The Supreme Court upheld the order: a municipality cannot plead lack of funds to escape its statutory duty to remove a public nuisance.

Private nuisance

An unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land or some right over it. Kinds:

  • encroachment on the neighbour's land (overhanging branches, roots);
  • physical damage to land (vibrations, flooding);
  • interference with comfort and enjoyment (smoke, smells, noise, dust).

Essentials of private nuisance

  1. Unreasonable interference: judged by the standards of an ordinary person in that locality. Factors: locality, duration, intensity, sensitivity of the plaintiff, malice of the defendant.
  2. With use or enjoyment of land or a right over it.
  3. Damage: actual damage, or, in cases of interference with comfort, substantial discomfort.
CaseSt Helen's Smelting Co v Tipping (1865)

Fumes from a copper smelter damaged trees and shrubs on the plaintiff's estate. Held: nuisance. Where there is material physical damage to property, the character of the locality is irrelevant.

CaseRobinson v Kilvert (1889)

Heat from the defendant's cellar damaged the plaintiff's unusually sensitive brown paper stored above. Held: no nuisance, because ordinary paper would not have been damaged; a person carrying on an exceptionally delicate trade cannot complain.

CaseChristie v Davey (1893)

The defendant, annoyed by music lessons next door, banged trays and shouted to disrupt them. Held: nuisance; his malicious intent made the noise unreasonable.

CaseSturges v Bridgman (1879)

A doctor built a consulting room at the end of his garden and then complained of noise from a confectioner's machines which had been used for years. Held: nuisance; it is no defence that the plaintiff came to the nuisance.

9.3 Remoteness of damage

Directness test

CaseRe Polemis and Furness, Withy and Co (1921)

Stevedores negligently dropped a plank into a ship's hold; it caused a spark that ignited petrol vapour and the ship was destroyed. Held: the defendants were liable for all direct consequences of their negligence, even if unforeseeable.

Foreseeability test

CaseOverseas Tankship (UK) v Morts Dock and Engineering Co, The Wagon Mound (No. 1) (1961)

Oil negligently spilled from a ship in Sydney harbour spread to a wharf where welding was going on. The oil, which was not thought capable of catching fire on water, ignited and destroyed the wharf. The Privy Council rejected Re Polemis: the defendant is liable only for damage of a kind that was reasonably foreseeable.

Refinements:

  • If the type of harm is foreseeable, the exact manner or extent need not be: Hughes v Lord Advocate (1963), where a boy knocked a paraffin lamp into a manhole and it exploded.
  • Egg-shell skull rule: the defendant takes his victim as he finds him, so is liable for the full extent of injury aggravated by the victim's pre-existing condition: Smith v Leech Brain and Co (1962), where a burn on the lip triggered a latent cancer.
  • Novus actus interveniens: an independent intervening act of a third party or the plaintiff may break the chain of causation.

Indian courts generally follow the foreseeability test, combined with the egg-shell skull rule.

9.4 Injunction

  • Temporary (interim): under Order XXXIX, Rules 1 and 2 CPC and s.37(1) of the Specific Relief Act; granted on a prima facie case, balance of convenience and irreparable injury.
  • Perpetual: by decree after trial (ss.37(2), 38 SRA).
  • Mandatory: to compel the defendant to undo a wrongful act, such as pulling down an obstruction (s.39 SRA).
  • Quia timet: to restrain an apprehended wrong which has not yet happened, on proof of imminent danger of substantial damage (Kuldip Singh v Subhash Chander Jain, 2000).

9.5 Specific restitution of property

  • Immovable property: suit for recovery of possession by a person entitled (s.5 SRA), and a summary suit under s.6 within six months by a person dispossessed without his consent otherwise than in due course of law.
  • Movable property: recovery of specific movable property (s.7 SRA); a person in possession of a specific movable not as owner may be compelled to deliver it to the person entitled (s.8 SRA), for example where compensation in money would not be adequate.

9.7 The judicial process in tort: problems in India

  • Delay: civil suits take many years; this deters claims, except in motor accident and consumer cases with dedicated tribunals.
  • Complicated procedure and evidence: strict proof of damage, need for expert evidence (doctors, engineers, scientists), and reports of testing laboratories.
  • Court fees: ad valorem court fees on the amount claimed in damages suits make litigation expensive. Motor accident claims and consumer complaints have low or nominal fees, which is why most tort claims go there.
  • Access to justice: legal aid under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 and PIL have improved access, but private tort litigation remains rare in India.

9.8 Parties: who may sue and who may not be sued

Who may sue

  • Aggrieved individuals.
  • Representative suits: under Order I Rule 8 CPC, one or more persons may sue or be sued on behalf of all with the same interest, with permission of the court and notice to all interested. This is the Indian class action.
  • Social action groups: public interest litigation lets public-spirited persons and organisations seek redress for those who cannot approach courts (S.P. Gupta, 1981).
  • Statutory standing: statutes allow specified persons or groups to sue: s.91 CPC (public nuisance), consumer associations and the Central Consumer Protection Authority (class actions) under the CPA, 2019.

Common law, the consumer and the Consumer Protection Act, 2019

The common law protected consumers through negligence and deceit; the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 adds a fast, low-cost forum, statutory product liability and a regulator against unfair trade practices.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Duty to take care and liability for negligence of manufacturers, traders and service providers including professionals
  • Caveat emptor and caveat venditor
  • Deceit and false advertisement
  • Liability for hazardous industrial activity
  • Product liability and the EEC directive
  • Right to common property resources and pathways
  • Consumer Protection Act, 2019

10.1 Common law duty of care

  • Manufacturers: owe a duty of care to ultimate consumers (Donoghue v Stevenson, 1932), independent of contract.
  • Traders and sellers: liable for negligence in handling or selling goods, and in contract for breach of implied conditions.
  • Service providers and professionals: doctors, engineers, architects, accountants owe a duty to use reasonable skill (Bolam; Jacob Mathew). Advocates are liable in negligence under general law but, after D.K. Gandhi (2024), not before consumer commissions.

10.2 Caveat emptor and caveat venditor

  • Caveat emptor ("let the buyer beware"): s.16 of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 provides that there is no implied condition as to quality or fitness, except where the buyer relies on the seller's skill and the goods are of a description the seller deals in (fitness for purpose), or where goods are bought by description from a dealer (merchantable quality), or as implied by trade usage.
  • Caveat venditor ("let the seller beware"): modern consumer law shifts the burden to sellers and manufacturers through implied conditions, mandatory disclosures, product liability and penalties for misleading advertisements.

10.3 Deceit and false advertisement

  • Common law: deceit (Derry v Peek) requires a knowingly false statement intended to be relied upon.
  • Statute: the CPA, 2019 defines a misleading advertisement (s.2(28)) as one that falsely describes a product or service, gives a false guarantee, conveys an express or implied representation which would amount to an unfair trade practice, or deliberately conceals important information.
  • The Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) may direct discontinuance or modification of a misleading advertisement and impose a penalty of up to ₹10 lakh on a manufacturer or endorser, and up to ₹50 lakh for subsequent contraventions; it may prohibit an endorser from endorsing for up to one year (three years for subsequent contraventions) (s.21). An endorser is not liable if he exercised due diligence to verify the claims.
  • s.89 makes a false or misleading advertisement by a manufacturer or service provider an offence punishable with imprisonment up to two years and fine up to ₹10 lakh (higher for subsequent offences).

10.4 Liability for hazardous and inherently dangerous industrial activity

Absolute liability (M.C. Mehta, 1987) and the polluter pays principle (Enviro-Legal Action, 1996) protect people living near hazardous industries, supported by the Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991 and the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010 (which applies the principle of no-fault liability in accidents).

10.5 Product liability

The EEC Directive

The European Economic Community Directive 85/374/EEC (1985) introduced strict liability of producers for damage caused by defective products: the injured person need prove only the defect, the damage and the causal link, not negligence. A product is defective when it does not provide the safety a person is entitled to expect. Defences include the "development risks" defence (state of scientific knowledge did not allow the defect to be discovered). It influenced the UK Consumer Protection Act, 1987 and many other laws.

Product liability under the CPA, 2019 (Chapter VI, ss.82 to 87)

  • "Product liability" (s.2(34)) means the responsibility of a product manufacturer or product seller to compensate for any harm caused to a consumer by a defective product or deficiency in services.
  • An action can be brought against a product manufacturer, product service provider or product seller (s.83).
Statutes.84, CPA, 2019

A product manufacturer is liable in a product liability action if the product contains a manufacturing defect; is defective in design; deviates from manufacturing specifications; does not conform to the express warranty; or fails to contain adequate instructions of correct usage to prevent harm or warnings regarding improper or incorrect usage. A manufacturer is liable even if he proves that he was not negligent or fraudulent in making the express warranty.

  • Product service providers (s.85): liable where the service was faulty, imperfect or deficient, where there was an act of omission or commission or negligence, where adequate instructions or warnings were not given, or where the service did not conform to the express warranty.
  • Product sellers (s.86): liable where they exercised substantial control over design, testing, manufacture, packaging or labelling; altered or modified the product; made an independent express warranty; sold a product whose manufacturer is not identifiable or not subject to Indian law; or failed to exercise reasonable care.
  • Exceptions (s.87): the product was misused, altered or modified; adequate warnings were provided; the product was sold to an employer or as a component for a sophisticated user; the danger is obvious or commonly known; or the consumer used it under the influence of alcohol or a non-prescribed drug.

10.6 Right to common property resources

Members of the public and communities have rights over common resources: rights of way along pathways (public rights of way, and customary easements under s.18 of the Indian Easements Act, 1882), village grazing lands, tanks and ponds. Courts protect these through the public trust doctrine and Art. 21.

CaseJagpal Singh v State of Punjab (2011)

The Supreme Court directed State governments to remove illegal encroachments on village common lands (gram sabha, gram panchayat, poramboke, shamlat lands) and restore them to the common use of villagers.

Leading consumer cases

CaseLucknow Development Authority v M.K. Gupta (1994)

A child became permanently brain-damaged after a wrong injection by a nurse. The Court held that both the child and the parents are consumers and awarded compensation to each; the hospital was vicariously liable.

CaseDr Balram Prasad v Dr Kunal Saha (2013)

In a medical negligence claim arising from the death of Dr Kunal Saha's wife, the Supreme Court enhanced compensation to nearly ₹6 crore plus interest, one of the largest awards for medical negligence in India, and apportioned liability among the hospital and doctors.

Part A Short answer questions5 × 4 = 20 marks

Answer any FIVE of the following.

1.Damnum sine injuria and injuria sine damno
Model answer
  • Injuria sine damno: violation of a legal right without actual loss is actionable. Example: Ashby v White (1703), where a voter was wrongfully refused his vote. In Bhim Singh v State of J&K (1985), illegal detention attracted compensation.
  • Damnum sine injuria: actual loss without violation of a legal right is not actionable. Examples: Gloucester Grammar School (1410), a rival school drawing away pupils; Mogul Steamship v McGregor (1892), lawful trade competition.

The test is the infringement of a legal right, not the existence of loss.

2.Volenti non fit injuria
Model answer

A person who freely and voluntarily consents, with knowledge of the risk, cannot sue for harm resulting from it. Examples: spectators at games (Hall v Brooklands Auto Racing, 1933), and surgical operations with consent. Limits:

  • knowledge alone is not consent (Smith v Baker, 1891);
  • the rescue cases (Haynes v Harwood, 1935);
  • consent obtained by fraud is invalid;
  • the defence cannot cover the defendant's negligence beyond the risk consented to;
  • statutory limits apply, such as unfair contract terms in consumer contracts.
3.Res ipsa loquitur
Model answer

"The thing speaks for itself." This is a rule of evidence that raises an inference of negligence where:

  1. the thing was under the defendant's management;
  2. the accident would not ordinarily happen without negligence; and
  3. the cause is unexplained.

The burden then shifts to the defendant to explain. Examples:

  • Scott v London and St Katherine Docks (1865);
  • Municipal Corporation of Delhi v Subhagwanti (1966), the collapse of a clock tower;
  • medical cases such as a swab left inside a patient (Achutrao Khodwa v State of Maharashtra, 1996).
4.Nervous shock
Model answer

Psychiatric injury caused by shock is actionable if it is a recognised illness and was reasonably foreseeable.

  • Dulieu v White (1901): fear for one's own safety.
  • Hambrook v Stokes (1925): fear for one's children.
  • McLoughlin v O'Brian (1983): proximity in time and space.
  • Alcock v Chief Constable (1992): secondary victims need close ties of love, proximity, and perception by their own senses.

Indian courts allow compensation for mental agony in negligence and consumer cases.

5.Malicious prosecution
Model answer

Essentials:

  1. a prosecution initiated by the defendant;
  2. without reasonable and probable cause;
  3. with malice;
  4. terminated in the plaintiff's favour; and
  5. damage to reputation, person or property.

Leading cases:

  • Bhogilal Chunilal Pandya v State of Bombay (1959): a mere complaint can be prosecution.
  • West Bengal State Electricity Board v Dilip Kumar Ray (2007): all elements must be proved.

Limitation: one year from acquittal (Art. 74, Limitation Act).

6.Private nuisance
Model answer

Private nuisance is unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land or rights over it. Elements:

  • unreasonable interference, such as noise, smell, smoke or encroachment;
  • damage; and
  • the plaintiff's interest in the land.

Points to note:

  • Sensitivity of the plaintiff is ignored (Robinson v Kilvert, 1889).
  • Malice may make otherwise lawful conduct a nuisance (Christie v Davey, 1893).
  • "Coming to the nuisance" is no defence.
  • Remedies are damages, injunction and abatement.

Public nuisance is a crime (s.270 BNS). An individual may sue for it only on proof of special damage.

7.Consumer under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019
Model answer

Section 2(7): a person who buys goods or hires or avails services for consideration, including online and teleshopping transactions.

It excludes persons who obtain goods for resale or a commercial purpose. Use for earning a livelihood by self-employment is not a commercial purpose.

Points to note:

  • Users with the buyer's approval and beneficiaries of services are consumers.
  • Free services are excluded (IMA v V.P. Shantha, 1995).
  • Advocates are outside the Act (Bar of Indian Lawyers v D.K. Gandhi, 2024).
  • Landowners under a joint development agreement are not consumers of the builder (Habib Alladin, 2026).
8.Product liability under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019
Model answer

Chapter VI (ss.82 to 87) makes manufacturers, service providers and sellers liable for harm caused by a defective product or deficient service.

  • A manufacturer is liable for a manufacturing or design defect, deviation from specifications, non-conformity with an express warranty, or inadequate instructions, even without proof of negligence or fraud (s.84).
  • Sellers are liable in specified cases (s.86), such as exercising substantial control or altering the product.

Exceptions (s.87): misuse or alteration of the product, adequate warnings given, and obvious dangers.

9.Vicarious liability of the State
Model answer
  • Under Art. 300, the Union and States may be sued like the East India Company.
  • Kasturi Lal v State of UP (1965): no liability for torts committed in the exercise of sovereign functions (police custody of seized gold).
  • State of Rajasthan v Vidyawati (1962): liability for a negligent government jeep driver.
  • N. Nagendra Rao v State of AP (1994): the sovereign immunity concept is outdated; the State is liable unless the act is a truly sovereign function.
  • Public-law compensation is available for violations of Art. 21 (Nilabati Behera, 1993). For example, the Court ordered ₹25 lakh for a custodial death in Lahra Bai Tamre (2026).

10.Distinguish between strict liability and absolute liability. Explain the Indian position.
Model answer

Strict liability (Rylands v Fletcher, 1868)

A person who brings onto his land and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes is liable for the damage caused by its escape, even without negligence. Essentials:

  1. a dangerous thing;
  2. its escape; and
  3. non-natural use of land.

Exceptions:

  • act of God (Nichols v Marsland, 1876);
  • act of a stranger (Box v Jubb, 1879);
  • the plaintiff's own default;
  • consent or common benefit;
  • statutory authority (Green v Chelsea Waterworks, 1878).

Absolute liability (M.C. Mehta v Union of India, 1987, the Oleum gas leak)

An enterprise engaged in a hazardous or inherently dangerous activity owes an absolute and non-delegable duty to the community. It is liable for all harm from an accident in that activity, and none of the Rylands exceptions apply. Compensation is related to the enterprise's magnitude and capacity, which gives it a deterrent effect.

Differences

Later developments

  • The Bhopal gas tragedy led to the Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991 (no-fault relief) and the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010 (s.17(3): no-fault principle).
  • Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action (1996) applied absolute liability with "polluter pays".
  • Klaus Mittelbachert v East India Hotels (1997) applied the idea to a five-star hotel's non-delegable duty.

Conclusion

India has moved beyond Rylands to a stricter rule suited to industrial hazards. Absolute liability is now the governing principle for hazardous enterprises.

11.What is negligence? Explain its essentials with the help of decided cases.
Model answer

Meaning

Negligence is the breach of a legal duty to take care, which results in damage to the plaintiff (Winfield). Negligence can be a mode of committing other torts, but it is also an independent tort.

Essentials

  1. Duty of care. Under the neighbour principle (Donoghue v Stevenson, 1932), a duty is owed to persons so closely and directly affected that the defendant ought to have had them in mind. Caparo Industries v Dickman (1990) sets a three-stage test: foreseeability, proximity, and whether it is fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty.
  2. Breach of duty. The standard is the reasonable man. Courts weigh probability, gravity of harm, the cost of precautions and social utility (Bolton v Stone, 1951; Paris v Stepney BC, 1951). Professionals are judged by the Bolam test, adopted in India for doctors (Jacob Mathew v State of Punjab, 2005, which also requires gross negligence for criminal liability).
  3. Damage. There must be causation and the damage must not be too remote. Remoteness is tested by reasonable foreseeability (The Wagon Mound, 1961), replacing the directness test of Re Polemis (1921).

Defences

  • Contributory negligence: damages are apportioned.
  • Volenti non fit injuria.
  • Inevitable accident.
  • Act of God.

Proof

Res ipsa loquitur raises an inference of negligence.

Indian applications

  • Medical negligence (V.P. Shantha, 1995; Kunal Saha v AMRI, 2013).
  • Motor accidents under the Motor Vehicles Act. Recent heads of compensation include loss of domestic care for homemakers (Shishu Pal v Surjeet, 2026). Mediclaim receipts are not deducted (Dolly Gandhi, 2026).

Conclusion

Negligence is the most important modern tort. It balances freedom of action against the security of others through the test of reasonable care.

12.Explain the doctrine of vicarious liability. Discuss the liability of the master for the torts of his servant, and the liability of the State.
Model answer

Basis

Qui facit per alium facit per se (he who acts through another acts himself). Respondeat superior (let the principal answer). The rationale is that the master benefits from the servant's work, has deeper pockets, and controls the enterprise.

Master and servant

  1. A relationship of service. Tests: control (Yewens v Noakes, 1880), integration, and the multiple or economic-reality test. There is generally no vicarious liability for independent contractors, except for non-delegable duties and hazardous work.
  2. Course of employment. The master is liable for wrongful acts authorised by him, or for wrongful and unauthorised modes of doing authorised acts:
  • Century Insurance v Northern Ireland Road Transport Board (1942): a driver lighting a cigarette while unloading petrol.
  • Lloyd v Grace Smith & Co (1912): fraud by a managing clerk.
  • Lister v Hesley Hall (2001): the "close connection" test.
  • Limpus v London General Omnibus (1862): the master is liable even for prohibited modes of doing authorised work.
  • No liability where the servant is on a "frolic of his own".

Other relationships

Principal and agent, partners (s.26 of the Partnership Act), and the owner and driver of a vehicle (Motor Vehicles Act).

State liability

  • Art. 300.
  • Peninsular and Oriental Steam Navigation Co v Secretary of State (1861): sovereign versus non-sovereign functions.
  • Vidyawati (1962): liability for a negligent government jeep driver.
  • Kasturi Lal (1965): immunity for sovereign functions.
  • N. Nagendra Rao (1994) and Common Cause v Union of India (1999) narrowed the immunity.
  • Constitutional torts under Arts. 32 and 226 attract compensation for violation of fundamental rights (Rudul Sah, 1983; Nilabati Behera, 1993; D.K. Basu, 1997). Example: Lahra Bai Tamre (2026), custodial death, ₹25 lakh.

Conclusion

Vicarious liability ensures compensation to victims. In India, the sovereign immunity exception has shrunk under the influence of Art. 21.

14.Chlorine gas leaks from a chemical factory in a city because a valve failed, despite all reasonable care. Residents nearby suffer injuries. The factory pleads that it was not negligent and that the failure was an unforeseen accident. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Is a hazardous enterprise liable without negligence?

Law: Under the rule of absolute liability (M.C. Mehta v Union of India, 1987), an enterprise carrying on a hazardous or inherently dangerous activity is absolutely liable for harm caused by an accident in that activity. The exceptions to Rylands v Fletcher, including inevitable accident, do not apply. Compensation is related to the enterprise's size and capacity. No-fault relief is also available under the Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991, and the NGT applies no-fault liability (s.17(3), NGT Act).

Application: Handling chlorine is a hazardous activity. The plea of due care or inevitable accident is not available.

Conclusion: The factory is absolutely liable to compensate all injured residents, with deterrent damages.

15.A police constable, driving a police van to take a sick colleague to hospital, negligently knocks down P. P sues the State. The State pleads sovereign immunity. Decide.
Model answer

Issue: Is the State vicariously liable for the negligence of its servant?

Law:

  • Art. 300.
  • State of Rajasthan v Vidyawati (1962): the State is liable for a government driver's negligence in non-sovereign work.
  • Kasturi Lal (1965) granted immunity for sovereign functions, but N. Nagendra Rao (1994) and later cases limit immunity to truly sovereign acts such as defence and the administration of justice.

Application: Driving a colleague to hospital is not an exercise of sovereign power that only the State can perform. Any private driver could do it.

Conclusion: The State is vicariously liable and must pay P compensation. P may also claim under the Motor Vehicles Act before the Motor Accident Claims Tribunal.

16.After an abdominal surgery, R continues to suffer pain. A scan shows a surgical mop left inside. The hospital says R cannot prove which doctor was negligent. Advise R.
Model answer

Issue: Can R prove negligence without identifying the individual doctor?

Law:

  • Res ipsa loquitur applies where the thing is under the defendant's control and the accident would not occur without negligence (Achutrao Khodwa v State of Maharashtra, 1996, a mop left in the abdomen).
  • Hospitals are vicariously liable for their staff.
  • Medical services for payment are "services" under the Consumer Protection Act (V.P. Shantha).
  • Jacob Mathew (2005) governs criminal prosecution only. Civil liability needs only a breach of the reasonable standard.

Application: A mop left inside is a lapse that speaks for itself, so the burden shifts to the hospital.

Conclusion: The hospital is liable. R may file a consumer complaint or a civil suit for compensation, including for pain and suffering.

17.S paid ₹45 lakh for a flat to be delivered in 2022. The builder has not delivered it and now offers a refund without interest. S wants to file a complaint. Advise S on the forum and the relief.
Model answer

Issue: Which forum has jurisdiction, and what relief can S get?

Law:

  • Delay in delivering a flat is a deficiency in service. A buyer cannot be made to wait indefinitely and may seek a refund with interest (Fortune Infrastructure v Trevor D'Lima, 2018; Pioneer Urban Land v Govindan Raghavan, 2019).
  • Under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 and the 2021 Rules, the District Commission has jurisdiction where the consideration paid is up to ₹50 lakh. The complaint may be filed where S resides.
  • Limitation is two years, but a continuing default keeps the cause alive.
  • The RERA remedy is concurrent (Imperia Structures v Anil Patni, 2020).

Application: The consideration paid is ₹45 lakh.

Conclusion: S should file before the District Commission, or alternatively before the RERA authority. S may seek possession with compensation for delay, or a refund with reasonable interest, plus litigation costs.
