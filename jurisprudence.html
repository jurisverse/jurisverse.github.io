Unit 1

Why study jurisprudence?

Jurisprudence is the study of the nature of law. It asks what law is, why it binds, and what it ought to be, and so trains lawyers to see the assumptions beneath rules.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Need to study jurisprudence
  • Its relationship with politics, power structures and a just society

Meaning

  • Latin juris prudentia: knowledge or skill in law.
  • Austin: the philosophy of positive law, studying general principles common to mature legal systems.
  • Salmond: the science of the first principles of the civil law.
  • Holland: the formal science of positive law.
  • Julius Stone: the lawyer's extraversion, the examination of law in the light of other disciplines.
  • Modern jurisprudence includes analytical (concepts), sociological (law in society), normative or ethical (what law ought to be) and critical approaches.

Why study it?

  • It explains basic concepts (rights, duties, persons, ownership, possession, liability) used in every branch of law.
  • It sharpens reasoning and argument: how to interpret statutes, use precedents and handle gaps.
  • It reveals the values behind law and the link between law and justice.
  • It helps reform: understanding why a law exists makes it possible to criticise and improve it.
  • In India, constitutional cases constantly turn on jurisprudential questions: the limits of the amending power (Kesavananda), natural justice (Maneka Gandhi), the meaning of dignity and privacy (Puttaswamy).

Law, politics, power structures and a just society

  • Law and politics: legislation is the output of political processes; courts decide politically charged disputes (federalism, elections, emergency). Positivists separate law from politics and morality; realists and critical scholars argue law reflects power.
  • Power structures: Marxists see law as a tool of the ruling class; feminist jurisprudence shows how law has reflected patriarchy; Dr B.R. Ambedkar saw the Constitution as an instrument to break caste hierarchy.
  • Just society: theories of justice (Aristotle's distributive and corrective justice, utilitarianism, Rawls's justice as fairness, the Indian idea of dharma, Amartya Sen's nyaya and niti) guide law-making and judging.
CaseIndira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975)

An amendment placing a Prime Minister's election beyond judicial scrutiny was struck down; the case shows how jurisprudential concepts (rule of law, democracy, basic structure) restrain political power.

Points to remember

  • Jurisprudence studies the nature, sources, concepts and purposes of law.
  • It underlies reasoning in every branch of law and in constitutional adjudication.
  • Law is closely tied to politics and power; theories of justice evaluate it.

Unit 2

What is a concept? What is a theory?

Concepts are the building blocks of legal thought; theories organise them to explain or justify the law.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Concept
  • Ideas and notions
  • What is a theory
  • Difference between theory, hypothesis, conjecture and opinion

Concepts, ideas and notions

  • A concept is a general idea formed by abstracting common features from particular instances (for example, "contract", "possession", "person"). Legal concepts give shape and predictability to rules.
  • An idea is any content of thought; a notion is a vague or provisional idea not yet sharpened into a concept.
  • Legal concepts can be: descriptive (describing facts, such as possession), normative (prescribing, such as duty), or institutional (existing only because of rules, such as marriage or a company).
  • Hart warned against defining legal terms in isolation; their meaning is understood through the rules in which they operate (his essay "Definition and Theory in Jurisprudence", 1953).

What is a theory?

A theory is a systematic, reasoned explanation of a phenomenon that connects concepts, can be tested against experience or argument, and aims to explain or justify. In law, theories explain what law is (positivism, natural law, realism) or what it ought to be (theories of justice).

TermMeaningExample
---------
TheoryA coherent, reasoned, tested system of explanationHart's theory of law as a union of primary and secondary rules
HypothesisA tentative proposition to be tested"Higher penalties reduce pollution offences"
ConjectureAn unproved guess, with limited evidence"The framers meant to exclude this case" without support
OpinionA personal belief or judgment, not necessarily reasoned or testedA view that a law is unjust

Points to remember

  • Concepts abstract common features; ideas and notions are less precise.
  • Legal concepts are descriptive, normative or institutional.
  • A theory is systematic and tested; hypothesis, conjecture and opinion are weaker forms.

Unit 3

What is a norm?

A norm is a standard that guides conduct by saying what ought to be done. Law is a system of norms, structured, as Hart showed, by rules about rules.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Difference between maxims, rules, principles and customary rules
  • Primary and secondary rules
  • What is a normative system

Norms

A norm is a statement of what ought to be done or not done, as opposed to what is. Kelsen: the law is a system of norms whose validity derives from a higher norm.

3.1 Maxims, rules, principles and customary rules

TypeNatureExample
---------
MaximA short traditional statement of a general legal truth, used as an aidAudi alteram partem; nemo judex in causa sua; ignorantia juris non excusat
RuleSpecific; applies in an all-or-nothing fashion (Dworkin)A will must be signed by two witnesses
PrincipleGeneral standard with a dimension of weight, balanced against othersNo one shall profit from his own wrong (Riggs v Palmer, 1889); natural justice; proportionality
Customary ruleA norm arising from long, continuous, reasonable and certain practice accepted as bindingCustoms of marriage and succession (Collector of Madura v Moottoo Ramalinga, 1868)

Dworkin's distinction between rules and principles

Ronald Dworkin (Taking Rights Seriously, 1977) argued against Hart that law consists not only of rules but also of principles, which judges must apply in hard cases. In Riggs v Palmer (1889), a grandson who murdered his grandfather could not inherit under the will, though the statute of wills had no exception, because of the principle that no one may profit from his own wrong.

3.2 Primary and secondary rules (Hart)

  • Primary rules impose duties: they require people to do or abstain from actions (criminal law, tort).
  • Secondary rules are rules about primary rules:
  1. Rule of recognition: identifies which rules are valid law (in India, roughly: what the Constitution, statutes and binding precedents lay down).
  2. Rules of change: empower persons to introduce, alter or repeal rules (legislative power, Art. 368).
  3. Rules of adjudication: empower persons to decide whether rules have been broken (courts' jurisdiction).
  • Hart: a legal system is the union of primary and secondary rules. A pre-legal society with only primary rules suffers from uncertainty, static character and inefficiency.

3.3 What is a normative system?

A set of norms related to one another so that the validity of each can be traced to a common source.

  • Kelsen's Pure Theory: a hierarchy of norms (Stufenbau); each norm derives validity from a higher norm, ending in the Grundnorm (basic norm), which is presupposed, not posited. In India, one might say the Grundnorm is that the Constitution ought to be obeyed.
  • Hart's system: validity by reference to the rule of recognition, whose existence is a social fact (accepted by officials from the internal point of view).
  • Other normative systems: morality, religion, etiquette; law differs in having secondary rules and institutionalised sanctions.
Exam tip

Combine Hart (primary and secondary rules), Kelsen (hierarchy of norms) and Dworkin (rules and principles) in any answer on norms. Give an Indian example for each.

Points to remember

  • A norm says what ought to be done.
  • Maxims, rules, principles and customary rules differ in generality and operation.
  • Rules apply all-or-nothing; principles have weight (Dworkin; Riggs v Palmer).
  • Hart's primary rules impose duties; secondary rules recognise, change and adjudicate.
  • Kelsen's hierarchy of norms ends in the Grundnorm.

Unit 4

The concept of law

Legal laws prescribe what ought to happen and can be broken; scientific laws describe what does happen and are disproved by exceptions.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Concept of law
  • Its difference from laws of natural science, social sciences, statistics and history
  • Laws of obligation

Meaning of "law" in different senses

SenseNatureBreach
---------
Laws of natural science (Newton's law of gravitation)Descriptive: state uniformities in natureAn exception disproves or revises the law
Laws of social sciences (law of demand)Descriptive generalisations about human behaviour, often probabilisticExceptions are explained, not punished
Statistical lawsStatements of probability and regularity in large numbersIndividual deviations are expected
Laws of history (Marx's historical materialism)Claims about patterns or direction of historical changeContested; not normative
Legal laws (Penal Code, Contract Act)Prescriptive: state what ought to be done, backed by sanctions or institutionsBreach does not invalidate the law; it leads to consequences

Definitions of law

  • Austin (positivist): law is a command of the sovereign, backed by a sanction, addressed to persons habitually obedient.
  • Hart: the union of primary and secondary rules.
  • Kelsen: a coercive order of norms.
  • Salmond: the body of principles recognised and applied by the State in the administration of justice.
  • Savigny (historical): law is found, not made; it grows from the spirit of the people (Volksgeist).
  • Roscoe Pound (sociological): law is social engineering, balancing competing interests.
  • Holmes (realist): the prophecies of what the courts will do in fact.
  • Natural law: law must accord with reason and morality; an unjust law is not law (lex injusta non est lex, Augustine and Aquinas).
  • Indian tradition: law as dharma, which binds the king as well as the subject.

4.1 Laws of obligation

Laws that impose obligations (duty-imposing rules) differ from power-conferring rules (contracts, wills, legislation). Hart criticised Austin for reducing all law to commands, ignoring power-conferring rules and the continuity of legal authority. Laws of obligation are characterised by serious social pressure, importance to social life, and potential conflict with self-interest.

Points to remember

  • Scientific laws describe; legal laws prescribe.
  • Definitions vary by school: command (Austin), rules (Hart), norms (Kelsen), Volksgeist (Savigny), social engineering (Pound), prediction (Holmes), morality (natural law), dharma.
  • Laws of obligation impose duties; power-conferring rules enable action.

Unit 5

Why are laws obligatory?

Laws bind because of sanction, social acceptance, consent, morality or justice, depending on which theory one accepts. Indian constitutional law draws on all of them.

What the syllabus lists for this unit

  • Contractarian theories: general will, free will and autonomous theories
  • Positivist theories from Austin onwards
  • Dworkin, Rawls
  • Marxian doctrine of withering away of the State
  • Transcendental theories

5.2 Theories of obligation

Contractarian theories

  • Hobbes: in the state of nature life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short"; people surrender rights to an absolute sovereign for security; obligation rests on that covenant.
  • Locke: people consent to government to protect natural rights of life, liberty and property; government that violates them may be resisted.
  • Rousseau (general will): law binds because it expresses the general will of the community, of which each citizen is a part; obeying it is obeying oneself.
  • Free-will and autonomy theories (Kant): a person is bound by laws that a rational, autonomous will could give to itself; the categorical imperative.

Positivist theories

  • Austin: law is binding because it is a command of the sovereign backed by sanction.
  • Kelsen: validity derives from a higher norm, ultimately the presupposed Grundnorm.
  • Hart: obligation exists when rules are accepted from the internal point of view and backed by serious social pressure; officials' acceptance of the rule of recognition grounds the system.

Dworkin

Law is integrity: judges must interpret legal practice in its best moral light, so that the community speaks with one voice of principle. Obligation arises from "associative obligations" of a community of principle.

Rawls

In A Theory of Justice (1971), principles of justice are those that free and equal persons would choose behind a veil of ignorance in the original position: (1) equal basic liberties for all; (2) social and economic inequalities arranged to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged (the difference principle) and attached to offices open to all under fair equality of opportunity. There is a natural duty to support just institutions.

Marx and the withering away of the State

Law is part of the superstructure built on the economic base; it serves the interests of the ruling class. After the proletarian revolution and a transitional dictatorship of the proletariat, class antagonisms disappear and the State (and law as coercion) will "wither away" (Engels). Critics point out that socialist States grew stronger, not weaker.

Transcendental theories

Obligation derives from a source beyond human will: divine law, natural law (Aquinas), or a moral order such as dharma; positive law binds because and in so far as it conforms to that higher order.

5.3 Whom does the law obligate?

  • Legal personality: natural persons and legal persons (companies, the State, idols, trusts). A Hindu idol is a juristic person capable of holding property: Pramatha Nath Mullick v Pradyumna Kumar Mullick (1925). The Guru Granth Sahib was held a juristic person in Shiromani Gurdwara Prabandhak Committee v Som Nath Dass (2000).
  • The people: the Constitution is made by "We, the people"; citizens owe Fundamental Duties (Art. 51A).
  • The State: bound by Fundamental Rights and directed by Directive Principles (Art. 37: fundamental in governance).
  • Locus standi: who can enforce obligations; relaxed by PIL (S.P. Gupta, 1981).
CaseRandhir Singh v Union of India (1982)

Drivers of the Delhi Police were paid less than drivers in other government departments doing the same work. The Court held that "equal pay for equal work", though a Directive Principle (Art. 39(d)), is a constitutional goal enforceable through Arts. 14 and 16. The State is obligated by Directive Principles when read with Fundamental Rights.

CaseI.C. Golak Nath v State of Punjab (1967)

The majority held that Parliament itself is obligated by Fundamental Rights even when amending the Constitution, as an amendment is "law" under Art. 13. Though overruled on this point in Kesavananda, it raised the question whether the sovereign legislature can be bound.

Exam tip

When asked "why is law obligatory?", compare at least four theories (sanction, social acceptance, consent, justice) and end with an Indian synthesis: constitutional supremacy grounded in popular sovereignty and justice, enforced by courts.

Points to remember

  • Key concepts: duty, obligation, liability, sanction, coercion, estoppel, promise, dharma.
  • Contractarians (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Kant) base obligation on consent or rational will.
  • Positivists (Austin, Kelsen, Hart) on command, validity or acceptance.
  • Dworkin on integrity; Rawls on fairness behind the veil of ignorance; Marx predicted withering away of the State.
  • Law obligates persons (natural and juristic), people and the State (Randhir Singh; Golak Nath).

Unit 6

Theories of authority and precedent

Legislation, precedent and custom are the main sources of legal authority. A precedent binds through its ratio decidendi, and jurists have offered several methods to find it.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Types of authority: legislative, judicial and customary, and their binding nature
  • Bindingness of precedent
  • Determination of ratio: methods of Wambaugh, Salmond, Goodhart, Simpson's approach in Jacobs' case
  • Llewellyn's factors
  • no distinction between making and declaring law
  • Gestalt psychology (Peter Brett)

6.1 Types of authority

SourceNatureBinding force in India
---------
LegislativeDeliberate law-making by a competent legislatureBinding if within competence and consistent with the Constitution
JudicialLaw declared in judgmentsArt. 141: law declared by the Supreme Court binds all courts; High Court decisions bind subordinate courts in the State
CustomaryLong-standing practice accepted as lawBinding if ancient, certain, reasonable, continuous, not opposed to law or public policy; recognised by statutes (s.29(2) HMA)
  • Other authorities: juristic writings (persuasive), foreign judgments (persuasive), international law (through Art. 51(c) and judicial incorporation where not inconsistent with domestic law).

6.2 Precedent and the ratio decidendi

  • Stare decisis: courts stand by decided cases. Only the ratio decidendi (the reason for the decision, the principle of law necessary to decide the case) binds; obiter dicta (statements not necessary to the decision) are only persuasive, though obiter of the Supreme Court is given great weight in India.
  • Within the Supreme Court, a bench is bound by a decision of a larger bench, and a bench of equal strength should refer doubts to a larger bench rather than differ (Central Board of Dawoodi Bohra Community v State of Maharashtra, 2005).

Methods of determining the ratio

JuristMethod
------
Wambaugh (inversion test)Frame the proposition of law said to be the ratio, then invert it (insert a word reversing its meaning); if the decision would still have been the same, the proposition was not the ratio
SalmondThe ratio is the general principle of law which the case establishes, as gathered from the judgment read with the facts; a precedent is authoritative as to the principle on which it is based
Goodhart (material facts test)The ratio is found by taking account of the facts treated by the judge as material, and his decision based on them; a later court must reach the same conclusion unless there is an additional or different material fact
SimpsonCriticised Goodhart's test for ignoring the reasons judges give; a case may have more than one ratio; the ratio is the rule of law expressly or impliedly treated by the judge as necessary to reach the conclusion
CaseJacobs v London County Council (1950)

The plaintiff tripped on a stone forecourt. The House of Lords decided against her on one ground; another ground was also discussed. Lord Simonds held that where a case is decided on two grounds, both are ratios and binding; a court cannot disregard one as obiter merely because the decision could rest on the other. Simpson used this to show that a case can have more than one ratio.

Llewellyn's factors

Karl Llewellyn (The Common Law Tradition, 1960) listed "steadying factors" that make appellate judging predictable: law-conditioned officials, legal doctrine, known techniques, responsibility for justice, the tradition of one right answer, opinions of the court, a known bench, group decision, and judicial security and honesty. He also catalogued the many techniques by which courts expand or narrow precedents.

Making versus declaring law

  • The declaratory theory (Blackstone): judges do not make law but declare what it has always been.
  • Realists and modern theorists accept that judges make law, especially in hard cases. In India, prospective overruling (Golak Nath) and creative interpretation (Maneka Gandhi; Puttaswamy) show judicial law-making.
  • The syllabus note (with reference to Peter Brett) suggests, drawing on Gestalt psychology, that there is no real distinction between making and declaring: a judge perceives the case as a whole pattern; the "discovery" of the rule and its "creation" happen in the same act of perceiving the whole, just as a figure is perceived only with its ground.
Exam tip

Apply the methods to a familiar case: Donoghue v Stevenson's ratio by Goodhart's test (material facts: manufacturer, product reaching consumer without intermediate examination, injury) and by Salmond's (duty of care to the ultimate consumer).

6.3 Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseRohan Vijay Nahar v State of Maharashtra (2025)

Judicial discipline under Art. 141 requires High Courts and lower courts to follow binding Supreme Court precedent, not to depart from it.

CasePooja Ramesh Singh v J&K Bank (2026)

The Supreme Court set aside tribunal orders that relied on non-existent, AI-generated precedents, warning that judges and lawyers must verify every authority they cite.

Points to remember

  • Binding precedent and judicial discipline (Rohan Vijay Nahar, 2025); AI-fabricated precedents rejected (2026).
  • Authority comes from legislation, precedent and custom; Art. 141 makes Supreme Court law binding.
  • Only the ratio binds; obiter is persuasive.
  • Wambaugh's inversion test, Salmond's principle test, Goodhart's material facts test and Simpson's criticism.
  • A case decided on two grounds has two ratios (Jacobs v LCC).
  • Llewellyn's steadying factors explain predictability; Gestalt view collapses the making/declaring distinction.

Unit 7

Limits on legislative authority

Is a legislature free to make any law? Positivists say yes, natural lawyers and rationalists say no. Indian constitutional law answers through judicial review, the basic structure and the test of reasonableness.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Positivist view that there are no limits (Austin, Kelsen, Golak Nath, D.C. Wadhwa)
  • Natural law view (Aquinas, Finnis)
  • Rationalist view (Kant, Rawls)
  • Basic structure doctrine (Kesavananda Bharati)
  • The concept of reasonableness with Indian cases: V.G. Row, Dwarka Prasad, Krishan Chander Arora, Haradhan Saha, Nergesh Meerza, Maneka Gandhi

7.1 The positivist view: no limits

  • Austin: the sovereign is legally illimitable; a sovereign bound by law is a contradiction.
  • Kelsen: validity depends only on the norm's derivation from a higher norm; content does not matter. Any content can be law.
  • Indian illustrations:
  • In Golak Nath (1967) the minority treated Parliament's amending power as unlimited (a positivist view).
  • D.C. Wadhwa v State of Bihar (1987): the Bihar government repromulgated ordinances 256 times without placing them before the legislature. The Court held that repromulgation without legislative approval is a fraud on the Constitution and subverts the democratic process; the executive cannot usurp the legislative function. This shows that, even within positivist validity, the Constitution limits law-making.
  • Krishna Kumar Singh v State of Bihar (2017): a seven-judge bench held that the failure to place an ordinance before the legislature is a constitutional infraction, and repromulgation is a fraud on the Constitution.

7.2 The natural law view: limits of morality and natural justice

  • Aquinas: law is an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by one who has care of the community and promulgated. Human law that conflicts with natural law is not law but a perversion of law.
  • John Finnis (Natural Law and Natural Rights, 1980): there are basic goods (life, knowledge, play, aesthetic experience, sociability, practical reasonableness, religion) and requirements of practical reasonableness; an unjust law lacks the full moral authority of law, though it may be legally valid.
  • Natural justice in India: audi alteram partem and nemo judex in causa sua are read into statutes and constitutional rights (Maneka Gandhi, 1978).

7.3 The rationalist view: limits of rational principles of justice

  • Kant: law must be consistent with the principle that each person's freedom is compatible with the freedom of all under a universal law; persons must be treated as ends, not means.
  • Rawls: a just constitution protects equal basic liberties that cannot be traded for social advantage; legislation must fit the principles of justice.
  • In India, the doctrine of arbitrariness under Art. 14 (Royappa, 1974; Shayara Bano, 2017) embodies a requirement of rationality in legislation.

7.4 The basic structure doctrine

CaseKesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala (1973)

Parliament may amend any part of the Constitution but cannot alter its basic structure. Any amendment destroying the basic structure is not a valid law. This is a uniquely Indian answer: the limit comes from the Constitution's own identity, neither from external morality nor from an unlimited sovereign.

7.6 Recent judgments (2025–26)

CaseMadras Bar Association v Union of India (2025)

The legislature cannot simply re-enact provisions that the Court has struck down without removing the basis of the judgment; such a law is an impermissible legislative override.

CaseAshwini Kumar Upadhyay v Union of India (2026)

Creating new offences is a legislative function. The Court declined to frame a hate-speech offence by judicial direction.

Points to remember

  • No legislative override of judgments (MBA, 2025); courts do not create offences (2026).
  • Positivists (Austin, Kelsen) see no limits on the sovereign; but repromulgation of ordinances is a fraud on the Constitution (D.C. Wadhwa; Krishna Kumar Singh).
  • Natural law (Aquinas, Finnis) limits law by morality and natural justice.
  • Rationalists (Kant, Rawls) limit law by principles of justice; Art. 14 arbitrariness doctrine reflects this.
  • Basic structure limits even constitutional amendment (Kesavananda).
  • Reasonableness: V.G. Row, Dwarka Prasad, Krishan Chander Arora, Haradhan Saha, Nergesh Meerza, Maneka Gandhi.

Unit 8

The functions of law

Law maintains order, upholds morality, promotes welfare, resolves disputes and changes society. Different schools emphasise different functions.

What the syllabus lists for this unit
  • Law as upholder of moral order
  • Dharma and the connection between law and morality
  • Law for efficiency and social stability (utilitarian views)
  • Ends of a legal, political and religious order: are they interchangeable? Dialectics of law
  • Law as a means of social control
  • Law as Volksgeist

8.1 Law as upholder of the moral order

  • Devlin (The Enforcement of Morals, 1959): society is held together by a shared morality; the law may enforce it to prevent disintegration, as with treason.
  • Hart (Law, Liberty and Morality, 1963): following Mill's harm principle, law should restrict liberty only to prevent harm to others, not to enforce private morality.
  • The Hart-Devlin debate followed the Wolfenden Report (1957) on homosexuality and prostitution. In India, Navtej Singh Johar (2018) preferred "constitutional morality" over popular or social morality in decriminalising consensual same-sex relations.

8.2 Dharma and the link between law and morality

  • In classical Indian thought, dharma includes law, duty, righteousness and the order sustaining society. The king (raja) was bound to uphold dharma and was himself subject to it.
  • Sources: Vedas, Smritis, custom (sadachara) and conscience (atmatushti).
  • Dharma does not separate law and morality: legal rules are part of the moral order.
  • The Supreme Court has referred to dharma as a concept of duty and righteousness in constitutional interpretation, while constitutional morality, not religious dharma, is the touchstone of validity.

8.3 Utilitarian views: efficiency and stability

  • Bentham: law should aim at "the greatest happiness of the greatest number"; pain and pleasure are the sovereign masters; punishment is justified only if it prevents greater mischief.
  • J.S. Mill: liberty and the harm principle.
  • Law and economics (Posner): legal rules should promote efficiency, minimising transaction costs and maximising wealth.
  • Criticism: utilitarianism may sacrifice minority rights; rights theories (Dworkin, Rawls) set side constraints.

8.4 Legal, political and religious orders

OrderEndsMeans
---------
LegalJustice, order, certainty, protection of rightsRules enforced by courts and State sanctions
PoliticalPower, policy, public welfare, legitimacyElections, legislation, administration
ReligiousSalvation, spiritual and moral perfectionFaith, ritual, religious authority
  • Are they interchangeable? A secular constitutional State separates them (Arts. 25 to 28), but they interact: religion influences personal law; politics makes law; law limits politics. Replacing one by another (theocracy, or politics without law) threatens liberty.
  • Dialectics of law: law develops through conflict and resolution of opposing forces: Hegel's thesis, antithesis and synthesis; Marx's material dialectic of class struggle; and in practice, the tension between stability and change, individual and community, rights and duties.

8.5 Law as a means of social control

  • Roscoe Pound: law is social engineering; the task is to reconcile and balance conflicting individual, public and social interests with minimum friction and waste. He classified interests and set out "jural postulates" of civilised society.
  • Social control works through formal sanctions and through law's educative and expressive effect.
  • Law as an instrument of social change in India: abolition of untouchability, land reform, Hindu Code reforms, reservation, Right to Education.

8.6 Law as Volksgeist

  • Savigny (historical school): law is not made by legislators but grows organically from the common consciousness or spirit of the people (Volksgeist), like language. He opposed a hasty codification of German law (1814).
  • Henry Maine: the movement of progressive societies has been "from Status to Contract".
  • Criticism: the Volksgeist is vague; it ignores conscious reform; it was later misused by nationalism. In India, it explains the persistence of custom and personal law, and the difficulty of a Uniform Civil Code.
Exam tip

For "functions of law", organise by school: natural law (morality), utilitarian (welfare and efficiency), sociological (social control and engineering, Pound), historical (Volksgeist, Savigny), Marxist (class instrument and dialectics), Indian (dharma). End with law as an agent of social change in India.

Points to remember

  • Hart-Devlin debate on enforcement of morality; India prefers constitutional morality (Navtej Johar).
  • Dharma unites law and morality in classical Indian thought.
  • Utilitarians (Bentham, Mill) and law and economics see law as maximising welfare and efficiency.
  • Legal, political and religious orders have different ends; law develops dialectically.
  • Pound: law as social engineering and control.
  • Savigny: law as Volksgeist; Maine: status to contract.

Part A Short answer questions5 × 4 = 20 marks

Answer any FIVE of the following.

1.Austin's command theory
Model answer

John Austin (The Province of Jurisprudence Determined, 1832) defined law as a command of the sovereign, backed by a sanction. The sovereign is a determinate person or body whom the bulk of society habitually obeys and who habitually obeys no one. The elements are command, duty, sanction and sovereign.

Criticisms:

  • it ignores customary and international law;
  • it does not explain power-conferring rules;
  • it cannot account for continuity of law when sovereigns change (Hart); and
  • sovereignty is divided in federal constitutions such as India's.
2.Kelsen's Grundnorm
Model answer

Hans Kelsen's Pure Theory of Law treats law as a hierarchy of norms, each deriving validity from a higher norm, up to the basic norm (Grundnorm), which is presupposed, not posited. Example: "the first constitution ought to be obeyed".

  • The theory excludes morality, sociology and politics from legal science.
  • A revolution that succeeds creates a new Grundnorm. The theory was used in State v Dosso (Pakistan, 1958) and Uganda v Commissioner of Prisons, ex parte Matovu (1966).
  • Criticism: the Grundnorm is a fiction; efficacy decides validity in the end.
3.Hart's rule of recognition
Model answer

In The Concept of Law (1961), H.L.A. Hart describes law as a union of:

  • primary rules of obligation; and
  • secondary rules, which cure the defects of a pre-legal society:
  • recognition, which cures uncertainty;
  • change, which cures the static quality of rules; and
  • adjudication, which cures the inefficiency of diffuse pressure.

The rule of recognition is the ultimate criterion of validity in a legal system. It is accepted from the internal point of view by officials. In India, it would be roughly "what the Constitution, as interpreted by the Supreme Court, recognises as law".

4.Natural law
Model answer

Natural law is a higher law derived from nature, reason or God, by which positive law is judged. Its main phases are:

  • Greek (Stoics);
  • Roman (jus naturale);
  • medieval (Aquinas: lex aeterna, naturalis, humana); and
  • modern, with social contract theorists (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau).

A 20th-century revival came from Fuller (the inner morality of law), Finnis (basic goods) and Radbruch (extreme injustice is not law).

In India, natural law influence appears in:

  • Maneka Gandhi (fair procedure);
  • ADM Jabalpur, through Khanna J's dissent; and
  • the basic structure doctrine.
5.Ratio decidendi
Model answer

The ratio decidendi is the principle of law on which a case is decided, the binding part of a precedent.

  • Wambaugh's inversion test: reverse the proposition; if the decision would change, it is the ratio.
  • Goodhart's material facts test: the facts treated as material, plus the decision.

Only the ratio binds under Art. 141; obiter dicta are persuasive. A ratio cannot be extracted by reading a judgment like a statute (Islamic Academy of Education v State of Karnataka, 2003).

6.Obiter dicta
Model answer

Obiter dicta are statements of law made in passing, not necessary for the decision.

  • They are not binding as precedent in English law, but they are persuasive, depending on the court and the judge.
  • In India, considered obiter dicta of the Supreme Court are generally followed by lower courts (Mohandas Issardas v A.N. Sattanathan, 1955; Peerless General Finance, 1992), though only the ratio binds strictly.
7.American legal realism
Model answer
  • Holmes: law is "prophecies of what the courts will do in fact". The life of the law has been experience, not logic.
  • Llewellyn: "rule-scepticism".
  • Jerome Frank: "fact-scepticism", since uncertainty lies in fact-finding.

The realists focused on judicial behaviour, the role of judges' personalities, and the gap between "law in books" and "law in action". Scandinavian realists (Olivecrona, Ross) took a psychological approach.

Criticism: realism overstates uncertainty and ignores the normative quality of rules.

8.Pound's social engineering
Model answer

Roscoe Pound saw law as a tool of social engineering: balancing competing interests to satisfy the maximum of wants with the minimum of friction. He classified interests as:

  • individual: personality, domestic, substance;
  • public: those of the State; and
  • social: general security, social institutions, morals, resources, progress and individual life.

This approach shaped Indian welfare legislation and public interest litigation.

9.Hohfeld's analysis of rights
Model answer

Wesley Hohfeld identified eight fundamental legal conceptions:

Jural correlativesJural opposites
right – dutyright – no-right
privilege – no-rightprivilege – duty
power – liabilitypower – disability
immunity – disabilityimmunity – liability

A claim-right always implies a correlative duty in another person. The analysis clarifies the loose use of the word "right" in judgments.

Part B Essay questions2 × 15 = 30 marks

Answer any TWO of the following.

10.Critically examine H.L.A. Hart's concept of law.
Model answer

Context

Hart revised Austin's command theory while staying within positivism: the separation of law and morality.

Critique of Austin

  • Laws are not like a "gunman situation": being obliged is different from having an obligation.
  • Austin cannot explain power-conferring rules such as those on contracts, wills and marriage.
  • Austin cannot explain the continuity and persistence of law across changes of sovereign.
  • Laws bind the legislators themselves.

Union of primary and secondary rules

  • Primary rules impose duties.
  • In a primitive society, primary rules alone suffer from uncertainty, a static quality and inefficiency.
  • These defects are cured by secondary rules: recognition, change and adjudication.

Internal point of view

Rules exist when people accept them as standards of conduct, shown in criticism and demands for conformity. A mere habit of obedience is not enough.

The rule of recognition

It is the ultimate rule identifying valid law. Its existence is a matter of social fact, the practice of officials.

Open texture

Words have a "core of settled meaning" and a "penumbra of doubt" (the "vehicles in the park" example). In the penumbra, judges exercise discretion.

Minimum content of natural law

Hart accepted some overlap with morality because of five human truisms:

  1. human vulnerability;
  2. approximate equality;
  3. limited altruism;
  4. limited resources; and
  5. limited understanding and strength of will.

Criticisms

  • Dworkin: principles, not just rules, are part of law. Judges have no strong discretion; there is a "right answer" (the Riggs v Palmer example).
  • Fuller: the eight principles of legality form an "inner morality" of law (the Hart–Fuller debate on Nazi laws).
  • Finnis: the central case of law is morally justified.

Conclusion

Hart's work is the foundation of modern positivism. Its accounts of the internal point of view and the rule of recognition remain essential, though Dworkin's critique shows the role of principles in adjudication.

11.Explain the doctrine of precedent and its operation in India.
Model answer

Meaning

Stare decisis means "to stand by what has been decided". Like cases are to be decided alike, which brings certainty, equality and efficiency.

Binding effect in India

  • Art. 141: the law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts in India. The Supreme Court is not bound by its own decisions (Bengal Immunity Co. v State of Bihar, 1955), but departs from them only for compelling reasons.
  • Benches: a smaller bench is bound by a larger bench. A coordinate bench that doubts a precedent must refer it to a larger bench (Central Board of Dawoodi Bohra Community, 2005; National Insurance Co v Pranay Sethi, 2017).
  • High Courts: bound by Supreme Court decisions. Within a High Court, a single judge is bound by a Division Bench. One High Court's decisions are only persuasive for another.
  • Judicial discipline: lower courts must follow binding precedent and cannot refuse to follow it on the ground that it is wrong (Rohan Vijay Nahar v State of Maharashtra, 2025).

Exceptions to binding force

  • per incuriam: decided in ignorance of a statute or binding authority;
  • sub silentio: a point not argued;
  • obiter dicta; and
  • decisions overruled or altered by statute.

Techniques

  • distinguishing;
  • overruling, for example ADM Jabalpur by Puttaswamy, and Dheeraj Mor by Rejanish K.V. (2025);
  • prospective overruling (Golak Nath, 1967); and
  • reference to larger benches, such as the nine-judge reference in Jai Bir Singh (decided 2026).

Problems

  • conflicting decisions of benches of equal strength;
  • volume of judgments; and
  • reliance on unverified or AI-generated citations, which courts have condemned (Pooja Ramesh Singh v J&K Bank, 2026).

Conclusion

Precedent provides certainty while allowing growth. Its Indian form, centred on Art. 141, combines strict vertical binding with flexibility at the apex.

12."Why are laws obligatory?" Discuss the various theories.
Model answer

The question

What gives law its binding force: coercion, consent, morality or acceptance?

Theories

  1. Command and sanction (Austin; Bentham). Laws oblige because of the threat of sanction. Criticism: this confuses "being obliged" with "having an obligation".
  2. Natural law. Laws oblige because, and to the extent that, they conform to reason and morality (Aquinas). An unjust law is not law (lex injusta non est lex); Radbruch's formula applies to extreme injustice.
  3. Social contract. Hobbes argued that individuals surrendered their rights to a sovereign for security. Locke argued for consent of the governed with a limited government. Rousseau grounded obligation in the general will.
  4. Historical school (Savigny). Law springs from the Volksgeist, the spirit of the people, and is obeyed because it reflects popular consciousness.
  5. Realist and psychological theories (Olivecrona). Laws are "independent imperatives" producing psychological effects.
  6. Hart's acceptance. Officials accept the rule of recognition from the internal point of view, and citizens generally comply.
  7. Kelsen. Validity derives from higher norms, up to the presupposed Grundnorm, so obligation is normative, not psychological.
  8. Fairness and political obligation (Rawls; Hart's principle of fairness). Those who accept the benefits of a just cooperative scheme should bear its burdens.
  9. Raz's service conception. Legitimate authority helps subjects better conform to the reasons that already apply to them.

Civil disobedience

Gandhi's satyagraha, and Rawls on civil disobedience in a nearly just society, show the limits of the duty to obey.

Conclusion

No single theory suffices. In a constitutional democracy like India, obligation rests on the legitimacy of a constitution adopted by "We, the People", the rule of law, and the moral acceptability of laws, reinforced by sanctions.

13.What limits exist on legislative authority? Discuss with reference to India.
Model answer

Theoretical limits

  • Natural law: higher law limits positive law.
  • Fuller's inner morality: laws must be general, promulgated, prospective, clear, consistent, possible, stable and congruently administered.
  • Dicey: parliamentary sovereignty is unlimited in Britain, subject only to political and practical limits.

Limits under the Indian Constitution

  1. Federal distribution: legislative competence under Art. 246 and the Seventh Schedule; colourable legislation is void (K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo, 1953).
  2. Fundamental Rights: a law violating Part III is void (Art. 13(2)).
  3. Basic structure: constitutional amendments cannot destroy basic features (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).
  4. Territorial limits (Art. 245): subject to the nexus doctrine (GVK Industries, 2011).
  5. Delegation: the legislature cannot abdicate essential legislative functions (In re Delhi Laws Act, 1951).
  6. Separation of powers:
  • the legislature cannot simply overrule a judgment; it must remove its basis (Madras Bar Association v Union of India, 2025, on the Tribunals Reforms Act; S.R. Bhagwat, 2015);
  • courts in turn cannot create offences (Ashwini Kumar Upadhyay, 2026).
  1. Manifest arbitrariness: a ground to strike down legislation (Shayara Bano, 2017; Joseph Shine, 2018).
  2. Procedural limits: money Bill certification is subject to review (Rojer Mathew, 2019; referred to a larger bench).

Conclusion

Unlike Britain, India has a sovereign Constitution, not a sovereign legislature. Legislative authority is limited by competence, rights, the basic structure and the separation of powers, all enforced through judicial review.

Part C Problems2 × 10 = 20 marks

Answer any TWO of the following.

14.A High Court judge refuses to follow a Supreme Court decision, saying it was wrongly decided and ignores social realities. Comment.
Model answer

Issue: Can a High Court refuse to follow a binding Supreme Court precedent?

Law:

  • Art. 141 makes Supreme Court decisions binding on all courts.
  • A High Court cannot declare a Supreme Court judgment wrong or per incuriam. It may only distinguish it on facts or note a later binding decision.
  • Rohan Vijay Nahar v State of Maharashtra (2025) stressed judicial discipline.
  • Cassell & Co v Broome (1972) is the English parallel ("the hierarchical system").

Application: Disagreement on merits is no ground to refuse to follow. The judge may record his reservations, but must apply the precedent.

Conclusion: The refusal is improper and contrary to Art. 141. On appeal, the decision will be set aside.

15.The Supreme Court strikes down a statutory provision fixing a short tenure for tribunal members. Parliament then re-enacts the same provision in a new Act without changing its basis. Is the new law valid?
Model answer

Issue: Can the legislature override a judicial decision by re-enacting the invalidated provision?

Law:

  • The legislature may remove the basis of a judgment by changing the law. It cannot simply declare the judgment ineffective or re-enact the same defect (S.R. Bhagwat, 2015; Madras Bar Association v Union of India, 2021 and 2025).
  • In 2025, the Court struck down provisions of the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 as an impermissible legislative override and directed a National Tribunals Commission.

Application: Re-enacting the same tenure provision without curing its basis is an override of the judgment.

Conclusion: The new law is invalid. It violates the separation of powers and judicial independence.

16.A trial court relies on three Supreme Court "precedents" cited in a party's written submissions. It later turns out that none of them exists; they were generated by an AI tool. Discuss.
Model answer

Issue: The validity of a decision based on non-existent authorities, and the responsibilities of courts and counsel.

Law:

  • Only real decisions bind under Art. 141.
  • Counsel owe a duty of candour to the court (BCI Rules).
  • Pooja Ramesh Singh v J&K Bank (2026) set aside orders relying on AI-generated fake precedents, and courts have warned trial judges (Gummadi Usha Rani, 2026).
  • The Supreme Court has issued draft rules on the use of AI in courts (2026).

Application: The order rests on non-existent law, which is a manifest error. Counsel failed to verify the citations.

Conclusion: The order is liable to be set aside, and the matter remanded. Counsel may face professional action. Every authority must be verified against a reliable report before it is cited or relied on.

17.Under a former dictatorship, a woman reported her husband's anti-government remarks under a law making such remarks a capital offence. The husband was sentenced to death. After democracy is restored, she is prosecuted for unlawfully depriving him of liberty. She argues that she acted under the law then in force. Discuss with reference to theories of law.
Model answer

Issue: Was the oppressive law "law"? This is the grudge informer problem.

Views:

  • Hart (positivist): the Nazi-era statute was law, but too evil to be obeyed. The honest solution is retrospective legislation, openly choosing the lesser evil, not denying that it was law.
  • Fuller (natural law): the Nazi regime failed the inner morality of law. Its "laws" lacked legality, so they were not law, and the informer cannot rely on them.
  • Radbruch: a statute in intolerable conflict with justice must yield ("statutory lawlessness").
  • The German court (1949): convicted the woman, reasoning that the statute was "contrary to the sound conscience and sense of justice of all decent human beings".

Application in India: Under a written Constitution, such a law would be void under Arts. 14, 19 and 21, and the dispute would be resolved through judicial review.

Conclusion: The answer depends on one's theory. The case illustrates the Hart–Fuller debate on the separation of law and morality.
